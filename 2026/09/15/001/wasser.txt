The study of Ricci shrinkers lies at the intersection of critical metrics
and geometric flows, and it has been extensively studied, yielding numerous
significant results. In dimensions 2 and 3, all Ricci shrinkers have been
completely classified. Hamilton [15] demonstrated that any 2-dimensional
Ricci shrinker is either isometric to R2 or a finite quotient of S2 . Additionally,
Cao, Chen, and Zhu [2], Naber [25], and Ni and Wallach [26] established that
any 3-dimensional Ricci shrinker is isometric to a finite quotient of R3 , S3 ,
or S2 × R.
In higher dimensions, the classification of gradient shrinking Ricci solitons
becomes more complex, especially due to the Weyl tensor’s involvement. In
the four dimensional case, some classification results have been obtained under specific conditions. Locally conformally flat solitons are finite quotients
of S4 , R4 , or S3 × R. Half-conformally flat solitons are finite quotients of S4 ,
CP2 , R4 , or S3 × R. Bach-flat solitons are either Einstein or finite quotients
of R4 or S3 × R. Under certain vanishing conditions on the Weyl tensor,
solitons can be classified as Einstein or finite quotients of specific spaces.
Notably, the classification of four-dimensional Ricci shrinkers with constant
scalar curvature has recently been fully achieved through the works of Peterson and Wylie [28], Fernández-López and Garcı́a-Rı́o [14], and Cheng
and Zhou [10]. These studies have established that any 4-dimensional Ricci
shrinker with constant scalar curvature is isometric to one of the following:
an Einstein manifold, a finite quotient of R4 , S2 × R2 , or S3 × R.
More partial classification results regarding Ricci shrinkers in higher dimensions can be found in the works mentioned, including [3], [5], [12], [13],
[17], [23], [26], [29], and [36]. These contributions have shed light on the
understanding of Ricci shrinkers beyond dimension four, but a complete
classification is still an ongoing research endeavor.
The question of rigidity in geometry pertains to whether a particular
geometric object is uniquely determined within its moduli space. Rigidity
results aim to describe how “rigid” a given geometric structure is, i.e., how
few variations or deformations it allows. In the context of Ricci shrinkers,
there are some interesting rigidity results. In this paper, we are only interested in rigidity results for non-compact Ricci shrinkers. For the compact
case, see some important works [16], [18], [19], [22] and [31].
Let us list several types of different and related results. Firstly Yokota,
as referenced in [34] and [35], used techniques for estimating the reduced
volume to prove a result regarding Ricci shrinkers with entropy close to
zero. Yokota demonstrated that any Ricci shrinker (M n , g, f ) with entropy
2
sufficiently close to zero is actually the Gaussian shrinker (Rn , ge , |x|4 ). In
particular, this result proves the Carrillo–Ni conjecture, which states that

the Gaussian shrinker is the only Ricci shrinker with zero entropy (see [6],
Remark 7.2).
Secondly, Li and Wang [21] proved a rigidity result for Ricci shrinkers close
to the cylinder Ricci shrinker (Sn−1 (r) × R, gc , fc ). They showed that if a
Ricci shrinker is sufficiently close to the cylinder shrinker in the pointedGromov-Hausdorff topology, then it must be the cylinder shrinker itself.
This result implies that the cylinder Ricci shrinker is isolated in the moduli
space of all Ricci shrinkers with the pointed-Gromov-Hausdorff topology.
Lastly, Cheng and Zhou [9] proved that the spectrum of the drift Laplacian on a Ricci shrinker is discrete and there is a sharp lower bound for the
first nonzero eigenvalue, and equality is achieved if and only if the shrinker
splits off a line.
Colding and Minicozzi [11] showed a result known as propagation of almost splitting. It states that if a Ricci shrinker almost splits on one scale,
it also almost splits on larger scales. More precisely, if a Ricci shrinker is
sufficiently close to the cylinder Ricci shrinker (Sn−k (r) × Rk , gc , fc ) within
a large compact set, then it must be the cylinder shrinker itself.
To summarize, these rigidity results for non-compact Ricci shrinkers include the isolation of the cylinder shrinker in the moduli space, the confirmation of the Carrillo-Ni conjecture by Yokota’s work, the discreteness of
the spectrum of the drift Laplacian, and the propagation of almost splitting
property. The references mentioned, such as [16], [18], [19], [22], [31], [21],
[34], [35], [6], [9] and [11], likely correspond to specific papers or sources that
contain the complete details and proofs of the results discussed.
