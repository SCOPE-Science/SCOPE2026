# Exact intersection metric and sharp induced-subgraph corrections for cyclic subgroup graphs

## Statement

Let \(G\) be a finite group and let \(\Gamma(G)\) be its cyclic subgroup graph: the vertices are the cyclic subgroups of \(G\), and two vertices are adjacent when one is a maximal subgroup of the other. Write \(\Omega(m)\) for the number of prime factors of \(m\), counted with multiplicity.

### Theorem A: exact pairwise distance

For any cyclic subgroups \(H,K\le G\), with \(D=H\cap K\),
\[
 d_{\Gamma(G)}(H,K)
 =\Omega(|H:D|)+\Omega(|K:D|)
 =\Omega(|H|)+\Omega(|K|)-2\Omega(|D|).
\]
Moreover, there is an induced geodesic from \(H\) to \(K\) that passes through \(D\).

Consequently,
\[
 \operatorname{diam}\Gamma(G)=
 \max_{H,K\in C(G)}
 \bigl(\Omega(|H|)+\Omega(|K|)-2\Omega(|H\cap K|)\bigr),
\]
where \(C(G)\) denotes the set of cyclic subgroups.

This strictly refines the general bounds \(r\le \operatorname{diam}\Gamma(G)\le 2r\) in Theorem 2.11 of Cameron--Reddy--Sharma, where \(r\) is the maximum value of \(\Omega(|H|)\) over cyclic subgroups \(H\).

### Corollary B: the correct two-subgroup induced path

If \(H\ne K\) are cyclic subgroups of the same order \(n\), then \(\Gamma(G)\) contains an induced geodesic from \(H\) to \(K\) of length
\[
 2\Omega\!\left(\frac{n}{|H\cap K|}\right).
\]
In particular, the length is \(2\Omega(n)\) exactly when \(H\cap K=1\).

Thus the hypothesis "two distinct cyclic subgroups of order \(n\)" alone does not imply the length-\(2\Omega(n)\) conclusion asserted in Proposition 2.5(2) of arXiv:2409.13796v2.

A minimal transparent counterexample is
\[
 G=C_4\times C_2=\langle x\rangle\times\langle y\rangle,
 \qquad H=\langle x\rangle,\quad K=\langle xy\rangle.
\]
Here \(|H|=|K|=4\) and \(H\cap K=\langle x^2\rangle\) has order \(2\). The cyclic subgroup graph has vertices
\[
1,\ \langle x^2\rangle,\ \langle y\rangle,\ \langle x^2y\rangle,\ \langle x\rangle,\ \langle xy\rangle
\]
and edges
\[
1-\langle x^2\rangle,\quad 1-\langle y\rangle,\quad 1-\langle x^2y\rangle,
\quad \langle x^2\rangle-\langle x\rangle,\quad
\langle x^2\rangle-\langle xy\rangle.
\]
It is a tree of diameter \(3\), so it has no induced path of length \(4=2\Omega(4)\). The exact distance formula gives \(d(H,K)=2\).

### Theorem C: when the cyclic subgroup generated by one element supplies the claimed long induced cycle

Let
\[
 n=p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_k^{a_k},\qquad a_i\ge1,
 \qquad r=\Omega(n)=a_1+\cdots+a_k.
\]
Then \(\Gamma(C_n)\) contains an induced cycle of length \(2r\) if and only if one of the following holds:

1. \(k\ge3\);
2. \(k=2\) and \(a_1=a_2=1\);
3. \(k=2\) and \(a_1,a_2\ge2\).

Equivalently, the assertion fails precisely for prime powers and for \(n=p^a q\) (up to interchanging \(p,q\)) with \(a>1\).

Therefore Proposition 2.5(1) of arXiv:2409.13796v2 is false as stated. Already \(G=C_{p^2}\) satisfies its stated hypothesis but \(\Gamma(G)\) is a path and has no cycle. Even within the branch \(k\ge2\), \(G=C_{p^2q}\) gives \(\Gamma(G)\cong P_3\square P_2\), a ladder whose induced cycles all have length \(4\), not \(6=2\Omega(p^2q)\).

For a general finite group containing an element of order \(n\), the positive cases above remain sufficient: the cyclic subgroups of \(\langle g\rangle\cong C_n\) induce the same subgraph inside \(\Gamma(G)\).

## Proof of Theorem A

Fix a cyclic subgroup \(H\) and define, for every cyclic subgroup \(X\),
\[
 \Phi_H(X)=\Omega(|H|)+\Omega(|X|)-2\Omega(|H\cap X|).
\]
Suppose \(X<Y\) are adjacent. Since \(Y\) is cyclic, adjacency is equivalent to \(|Y:X|=p\) for a prime \(p\). The natural map
\[
 H\cap Y\longrightarrow Y/X
\]
has kernel \(H\cap X\). Hence
\[
 [H\cap Y:H\cap X]\in\{1,p\}.
\]
Thus passing across an edge changes \(\Omega(|X|)\) by \(1\) and changes \(\Omega(|H\cap X|)\) by either \(0\) or \(1\). Consequently
\[
 |\Phi_H(Y)-\Phi_H(X)|=1.
\]
The same holds with the edge traversed in the opposite direction. Since \(\Phi_H(H)=0\), every path from \(H\) to \(K\) has length at least
\[
 \Phi_H(K)=\Omega(|H:D|)+\Omega(|K:D|),\qquad D=H\cap K.
\]

For the reverse inequality, choose saturated subgroup chains
\[
 D=H_0<H_1<\cdots<H_a=H,
 \qquad
 D=K_0<K_1<\cdots<K_b=K.
\]
Because \(H\) and \(K\) are cyclic, each successive index is prime and
\[
 a=\Omega(|H:D|),\qquad b=\Omega(|K:D|).
\]
Traversing the first chain downward and the second upward gives a path of length \(a+b\), proving equality.

This path is induced. Nonconsecutive vertices on either one chain have an intermediate chain vertex, so they are not adjacent. A vertex strictly above \(D\) on the \(H\)-chain and a vertex strictly above \(D\) on the \(K\)-chain are incomparable: if one contained the other, the smaller subgroup would lie in \(H\cap K=D\), a contradiction. Hence there are no cross-chain chords. The path is therefore an induced geodesic.

The diameter formula follows by maximizing the exact pairwise distance.

## Proof of Theorem C

By the standard divisor-vector description,
\[
 \Gamma(C_n)\cong P_{a_1+1}\square\cdots\square P_{a_k+1},
\]
with vertices \((b_1,\dots,b_k)\), \(0\le b_i\le a_i\), and edges changing one coordinate by one.

If \(k=1\), this graph is a path, so it has no cycle.

If \(k=2\), the graph is a rectangular grid. When \(a_1=a_2=1\), it is \(C_4\), giving the required \(2r=4\) cycle. When \(a_1,a_2\ge2\), the outer boundary of the rectangle is an induced cycle of length \(2(a_1+a_2)=2r\): nonconsecutive points on opposite boundary sides are separated by at least two grid steps, so there are no chords. If, say, \(a_2=1<a_1\), the graph is a ladder \(P_{a_1+1}\square P_2\). Every simple cycle in a ladder is the boundary of a consecutive block of cells; if the block contains more than one cell, an interior rung is a chord. Thus every induced cycle is a single square, of length \(4<2(a_1+1)\).

Finally suppose \(k\ge3\). Take one saturated path from \((0,\ldots,0)\) to \((a_1,\ldots,a_k)\) by filling coordinates in the order \(1,2,\ldots,k\), and a second by filling them in the reverse order \(k,k-1,\ldots,1\). Each path has length \(r\). They meet only at their endpoints. Any pair of interior vertices, one from each path, differs in at least two unit coordinate steps, so no cross edge joins the two paths. Their union is therefore an induced cycle of length \(2r\).

## Relation to prior literature

Cameron, Reddy and Sharma, *Cyclic Subgroup Graph of a Group*, arXiv:2409.13796v2 (16 September 2026), define the graph and prove a product-of-paths description for cyclic groups, state Proposition 2.5 on induced paths/cycles, and prove the general diameter bounds in Theorem 2.11. The exact intersection distance formula above is not stated there; the proof of their upper-diameter example uses a related one-dimensional potential only for a special pair in \(C_n\times C_n\).

Malviy, Kakkar and Srivastava, *Line Graph Characterization of Cyclic Subgroup Graph*, arXiv:2503.12184v1 / National Academy Science Letters (2025), studies forbidden induced subgraphs for the line-graph property rather than the metric formula above. Tărnăuceanu, arXiv:2302.05784, studies edge counts. Standard subgroup-lattice and Hasse-diagram theory is background; the metric proof can be viewed as a rank/meet argument specialized to the meet-semilattice of cyclic subgroups.

Targeted searches for the exact distance formula, the intersection correction to Proposition 2.5(2), and a correction/erratum to arXiv:2409.13796 found no prior statement. Originality is therefore claimed only to the best of our knowledge.

## Limitations

The metric theorem concerns finite groups. The induced-cycle classification in Theorem C is exact for the cyclic subgroup \(C_n\), and gives sufficient conditions inside any ambient group containing an element of order \(n\); it is not claimed to classify all ambient groups that may acquire a \(2r\)-cycle from other cyclic subgroups. The underlying rank/meet argument is elementary and may be implicit in general poset folklore; no novelty is claimed for such an abstract order-theoretic lemma. No independent validation or formal verification is asserted.
