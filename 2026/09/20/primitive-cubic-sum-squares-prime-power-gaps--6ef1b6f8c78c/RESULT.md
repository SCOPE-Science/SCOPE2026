# Prime-power coordinate gaps in primitive solutions of \(x^2+y^2=z^3\)

Let \(x,y,z\) be positive integers with
\[
x^2+y^2=z^3,\qquad \gcd(x,y)=1,
\]
and put
\[
\Delta=|x-y|.
\]

The classical Gaussian-integer parametrization writes every such primitive solution, up to interchanging \(x\) and \(y\), in the form
\[
\{x,y\}=\left\{\left|a^3-3ab^2\right|,\;3a^2b-b^3\right\},\qquad
z=a^2+b^2,
\]
for coprime integers \(a>b>0\) of opposite parity.  The result below classifies exactly when the coordinate gap \(\Delta\) is a prime power.

## Theorem

Suppose
\[
\Delta=\ell^e
\]
for a prime \(\ell\) and an integer \(e\ge1\).  With normalized parameters \(a>b>0\) as above, exactly one of the following occurs.

1. **Star-number branch.**  We have \(\ell\ne3\), \(a=b+1\), \(b\ge2\), and
   \[
   \Delta=6b^2+6b+1=\ell^e.
   \]
   Conversely, every \(b\ge2\) for which \(6b^2+6b+1\) is a prime power gives a primitive solution
   \[
   x=(b+1)(2b^2-2b-1),\quad
   y=b(2b^2+6b+3),\quad
   z=2b^2+2b+1
   \]
   with that gap, up to interchanging \(x,y\).  The gap is the \((b+1)\)-st star number (centered dodecagonal number).

2. **Pell branch.**  We have \(\ell\ne3\),
   \[
   \Delta=s=a+b=\ell^e,
   \]
   and, with \(d=a-b\),
   \[
   s^2-3d^2=-2.
   \]
   Conversely, every positive solution of this Pell-type equation with \(s>1\) and \(s\) a prime power yields a primitive solution by
   \[
   a=\frac{s+d}{2},\qquad b=\frac{s-d}{2}.
   \]
   The positive Pell solutions are generated by
   \[
   s+d\sqrt3=(1+\sqrt3)(2+\sqrt3)^n,\qquad n\ge1.
   \]

3. **Exceptional \(3\)-power branch.**  There are exactly two possibilities:
   \[
   (a,b)=(2,1),\qquad (x,y,z)=(2,11,5),\qquad \Delta=9,
   \]
   or
   \[
   (a,b)=(7,2),\qquad (x,y,z)=(259,286,53),\qquad \Delta=27.
   \]

In particular, the only powers of \(3\) that occur as coordinate gaps in primitive positive solutions are \(9\) and \(27\).

## Proof

Because \(\gcd(x,y)=1\), the two coordinates have opposite parity.  In \(\mathbb Z[i]\), the factors \(x+iy\) and \(x-iy\) are therefore coprime: a common Gaussian prime would divide both \(2x\) and \(2y\), while the Gaussian prime above \(2\) cannot divide \(x+iy\) when \(x,y\) have opposite parity.  Since
\[
(x+iy)(x-iy)=z^3
\]
and \(\mathbb Z[i]\) is a unique factorization domain, \(x+iy\) is a cube up to a unit; the unit can be absorbed into the cube because cubing permutes the four Gaussian units.  After the usual sign, conjugation and coordinate symmetries, take coprime \(a>b>0\) of opposite parity and write
\[
A=a(a^2-3b^2),\qquad B=b(3a^2-b^2).
\]
Then
\[
\{x,y\}=\{|A|,B\},\qquad z=a^2+b^2.
\]

There are two sign regimes.

### 1. The regime \(A<0\)

Here \(a^2<3b^2\), and direct factorization gives
\[
\Delta=A+B=(a-b)(a^2+4ab+b^2).
\]
Put
\[
u=a-b,\qquad V=a^2+4ab+b^2.
\]
Since \(\gcd(a,b)=1\), we have \(\gcd(u,b)=1\); since \(a,b\) have opposite parity, \(u\) is odd.  Reducing \(V\) modulo \(u\) gives
\[
V=u^2+6bu+6b^2\equiv6b^2\pmod u,
\]
and hence
\[
\gcd(u,V)=\gcd(u,6)=\gcd(u,3)\in\{1,3\}. \tag{1}
\]

If \(\ell\ne3\), the two factors \(u,V\) of the prime power \(\ell^e\) are coprime.  Since \(V>1\), this forces
\[
u=1.
\]
Thus \(a=b+1\).  The condition \(A<0\) then gives \(b\ge2\), and
\[
\Delta=V=6b^2+6b+1.
\]
This proves the star-number branch and its converse by direct substitution.

Now let \(\ell=3\).  If \(3\nmid u\), (1) again forces \(u=1\), but then
\[
V=6b^2+6b+1\equiv1\pmod3,
\]
so \(V>1\) is not a power of \(3\).  If \(3\mid u\), then \(3\nmid b\), and
\[
V=u^2+6bu+6b^2
\]
has exactly one factor of \(3\): writing \(u=3t\),
\[
\frac V3=3t^2+6bt+2b^2\not\equiv0\pmod3.
\]
A pure power of \(3\) would therefore require \(V=3\), impossible because \(V\ge13\).  Hence no \(3\)-power gap occurs in the \(A<0\) regime.

### 2. The regime \(A>0\)

Now
\[
\Delta=|A-B|=(a+b)\,|a^2-4ab+b^2|.
\]
Put
\[
s=a+b,\qquad Q=a^2-4ab+b^2.
\]
Again \(s\) is odd, \(\gcd(s,b)=1\), and
\[
Q=s^2-6sb+6b^2\equiv6b^2\pmod s.
\]
Consequently
\[
\gcd(s,Q)=\gcd(s,3)\in\{1,3\}. \tag{2}
\]
Also, because \(a,b\) have opposite parity,
\[
Q\equiv1\pmod4. \tag{3}
\]

If \(\ell\ne3\), the factors \(s\) and \(|Q|\) in the prime power \(\Delta\) are coprime.  Since \(s>1\), we must have
\[
|Q|=1.
\]
By (3), \(Q=1\).  With \(d=a-b\),
\[
s^2-3d^2=-2Q=-2.
\]
Conversely, a positive solution \((s,d)\) of \(s^2-3d^2=-2\) with \(s>1\) has \(s,d\) odd, \(s>d>0\), and \(\gcd(s,d)=1\).  Therefore
\[
a=(s+d)/2,\qquad b=(s-d)/2
\]
are positive, coprime and of opposite parity.  Moreover \(Q=1\), so the resulting primitive cubic-sum solution has gap \(s\).  The standard Pell recurrence gives
\[
s+d\sqrt3=(1+\sqrt3)(2+\sqrt3)^n,\quad n\ge1.
\]

It remains to take \(\ell=3\).  Since \(s>1\) and \(s|\,\Delta\), we have \(3\mid s\), and \(\gcd(s,b)=1\) gives \(3\nmid b\).  From
\[
Q=s^2-6sb+6b^2
\]
we obtain
\[
v_3(Q)=1.
\]
Thus \(|Q|=3\), and (3) forces
\[
Q=-3.
\]
Writing \(s=3^r\), we get
\[
s^2-3d^2=-2Q=6,
\]
or equivalently
\[
d^2+2=3^{\,2r-1}. \tag{4}
\]
For \(r=1\), (4) gives \(d=1\), hence \((a,b)=(2,1)\) and \(\Delta=9\).  For \(r\ge2\), Nagell's theorem on
\[
X^2+2=Y^n
\]
states that the only solution with \(n>1\) is \(X=5,\;Y=3,\;n=3\).  Applied to (4), this gives \(2r-1=3\), hence \(r=2,d=5\), so \((a,b)=(7,2)\) and \(\Delta=27\).  This completes the classification. \(\square\)

## Consequences

For a **prime** coordinate gap, the exceptional \(3\)-power branch disappears.  Thus a prime gap occurs precisely through one of two one-parameter mechanisms:

- a prime star number \(6b^2+6b+1\) with \(b\ge2\); or
- a prime first coordinate \(s\) of a positive solution \(s^2-3d^2=-2\).

For example,
\[
9^2+46^2=13^3,\qquad |46-9|=37,
\]
comes from the star-number branch, while
\[
52^2+47^2=17^3,\qquad |52-47|=5,
\]
and
\[
2655^2+2636^2=241^3,\qquad |2655-2636|=19,
\]
come from the Pell branch.  The star branch also produces genuine higher prime powers, for example
\[
115^2+236^2=41^3,\qquad |236-115|=121=11^2.
\]

## Relation to earlier work and originality check

The underlying parametrization is classical and is not claimed here.  Beukers explicitly obtains
\[
x=a^3-3ab^2,\qquad u=b(3a^2-b^2)
\]
for primitive solutions of \(x^2+u^2=z^3\) by factorization in \(\mathbb Z[i]\), in the broader study of generalized Fermat equations.  He also notes that the spherical signature \(\{2,2,k\}\) has infinitely many solutions and is elementary.

The exceptional \(3\)-power analysis uses the classical theorem for \(X^2+2=Y^n\).  Cohn's 1993 discussion attributes the \(C=2\) result to Nagell, and Sury later gave an elementary proof that the only solutions for \(n>1\) are \((X,Y,n)=(\pm5,3,3)\).

To the best of our knowledge, searches for the equation together with coordinate difference/gap, prime-power difference, the exact factorizations above, star or centered-dodecagonal gaps, and the Pell equation did not locate the prime-power-gap classification or a stronger theorem implying it.  OEIS A003154 identifies \(6n(n-1)+1\) as the star/centered-dodecagonal numbers, but does not record this connection to primitive solutions of \(x^2+y^2=z^3\).

The main residual originality risk is that the result is an elementary refinement of a classical parametrization and could be present under different terminology in older books, theses, problem literature, or treatments of the spherical generalized-Fermat family that are not well indexed.

## Computational verification

The standalone script `artifacts/verify.py` enumerates every normalized parameter pair
\[
2\le a\le500,\qquad 1\le b<a,\qquad \gcd(a,b)=1,\qquad a\not\equiv b\pmod2.
\]
For each pair it checks the cubic identity, determines whether \(\Delta\) is a prime power by exact integer arithmetic, and compares that answer with the three branches of the theorem.

It checks 50,765 parameter pairs, finds 192 prime-power gaps, and reports zero classification mismatches.  The finite computation supports the proof but is not used to establish the unbounded theorem.

## Limitations

The theorem concerns positive primitive solutions of \(x^2+y^2=z^3\) and the specific invariant \(|x-y|\).  It does not classify which star numbers or Pell-sequence terms are prime powers, nor does it address nonprimitive solutions or analogous gap questions for \(x^2+y^2=z^k\) with \(k\ne3\).
