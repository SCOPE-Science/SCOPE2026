# Review

## Correctness

PASS. A finite theory mentions finitely many raw nouns, so every finite model is completely described by the nonnegative cardinalities of the finitely many Boolean atoms determined by those nouns. Every Boolean noun term is a union of atoms. Inclusion, overlap, weak cardinal comparison, and strict cardinal comparison therefore translate exactly to linear Presburger constraints. Existentially eliminating the atom counts gives a Presburger definition of the total model size, hence an effectively semilinear and ultimately periodic spectrum.

Uniformly replacing every model element by \(m\) copies preserves all four atomic sentence forms and therefore all sentence-level Boolean combinations, proving dilation closure. The arbitrary-\(k\) construction forces a partition of the universe into \(k\) equal blocks and therefore has exactly the positive multiples of \(k\) as its spectrum.

PASS. Jiang classifies the original language and the sentence-level Boolean extension, then shows in Section 5.1 that noun-level union can define divisibility by \(3\). The inspected text does not state a Presburger translation, semilinearity, ultimate periodicity, dilation closure, or arbitrary-\(k\) divisibility for the noun-Boolean extension.

The closest broader literature studies cardinality comparison over Boolean algebras and finite/infinite syllogistic reasoning, but the checked sources do not state this finite-spectrum consequence. Candidate-specific published-finding and web searches over Presburger, semilinear, ultimately periodic, Boolean-noun, and arbitrary-divisibility terminology found no equivalent theorem.

## Value

PASS. The source's modulus-\(3\) example shows that noun-level Boolean operations cross a genuine expressive threshold, but by itself leaves open how wild the resulting spectra can become. The Presburger theorem gives a global structural ceiling for every finite theory, while the arbitrary-\(k\) family shows that the extension realizes all pure divisibility patterns. This separates a broad new arithmetic expressivity from nonperiodic behavior and gives concrete impossible spectra such as primes and powers of two.

The theorem is not a complete characterization of realizable spectra, and “effective” does not mean computationally efficient.
