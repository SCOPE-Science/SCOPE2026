# Complete length-12 binary string-attractor census: 28 size-four words in 9 symmetry classes
## Finding
For \(w\in\{0,1\}^{12}\), let \(\gamma(w)\) be the minimum size of a string attractor. Exactly \(28\) words satisfy \(\gamma(w)=4\); the remaining \(4068\) words split as \(2\) with \(\gamma=1\), \(1332\) with \(\gamma=2\), and \(2734\) with \(\gamma=3\). The \(28\) size-four words form exactly \(9\) orbits under reversal and bit complementation, represented by 000100101011, 000100101110, 000100110101, 000100110111, 000101100111, 000110100111, 001011110100, 010011000101, and 011110100001.

## Assumptions and scope
A string attractor for a finite word is a set of positions such that every distinct nonempty contiguous substring has at least one occurrence crossing a selected position. The quantity \(\gamma(w)\) is the minimum cardinality of such a set. The classification is over all \(2^{12}=4096\) binary words of length \(12\). Symmetry means the Klein four-group generated by reversing the word and complementing every bit.

## Proof
For a fixed word, enumerate every distinct substring together with every interval in which it occurs. A candidate position set is an attractor exactly when, for every distinct substring, at least one of those occurrence intervals intersects the candidate. Exhaustive enumeration of position subsets in increasing cardinality therefore computes \(\gamma(w)\) directly from the definition.

Applying this test to every binary word of length \(12\) gives the exact distribution \(2,1332,2734,28\) at attractor sizes \(1,2,3,4\), respectively. The \(28\) size-four words are then partitioned by the operations \(w\mapsto w^R\) and \(w\mapsto \overline{w}\); their orbit sizes are \(4,4,4,2,4,2,2,4,2\), which sum to \(28\), and the nine lexicographically least representatives are those listed in the finding.

## Verification
`verify.py` contains two independent direct-definition engines. The first represents substring occurrences by interval bitmasks. The second independently builds, for each candidate set of positions, the set of all substring values touched by those positions. Both engines enumerate all \(4096\) length-12 words and return the same distribution. As an additional boundary check, the first engine verifies that no binary word of lengths \(1\) through \(11\) has attractor size \(4\), agreeing with the published extremal sequence. The script reconstructs every reversal/complement orbit and terminates with `VERIFY_OK`. `extremizers.csv` lists all \(28\) words, their canonical symmetry representative, and one minimum attractor in 1-based coordinates.

## Relationship to prior work
Kempa and Prezza introduced string attractors in 2017. Kempa, Policriti, Prezza and Rotenberg subsequently developed exact verification and optimization methods. OEIS A339391 records the maximum minimum-attractor size for each binary length and identifies `000100101011` as a shortest size-four witness, so the fact that size four first occurs at length \(12\) is prior knowledge. OEIS A339668 records that exactly \(1332\) length-12 words have minimum attractor size two. Bannai et al. gave MAX-SAT formulations for exact computation and explicitly cite the OEIS implementation. The contribution here is the complete length-12 census at the first size-four layer: the count \(28\), its full membership list, and its reduction to exactly \(9\) reversal/complement classes.

## Limitations
This is a finite classification at length \(12\). It does not assert a formula for the number of size-four words at larger lengths, nor a structural characterization of all binary words with a given attractor size. Novelty is supported by focused literature/database comparisons but, as with any finite literature search, cannot exclude an obscure unindexed prior enumeration.
