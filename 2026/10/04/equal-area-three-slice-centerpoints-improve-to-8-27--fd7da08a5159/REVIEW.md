# Review

## Correctness
PASS. Equal slice areas and the midpoint inclusion sandwich the area of \(\tfrac12(A_0+A_2)\) between \(a\) and \(a\). Equality in planar Brunn--Minkowski therefore applies, forcing \(A_0\) and \(A_2\) to be homothetic; equal area reduces this to translation, and equal-area inclusion identifies \(A_1\) with the midpoint translate. An affine shear makes all three slices identical. For any halfspace through or containing the middle centroid, the middle planar section contains the centroid and hence has area at least \(4a/9\). One outer section contains the middle section because the three parallel slice sections are nested. Thus two slices contribute at least \(8a/9\), which is \(8/27\) of the total mass. Horizontal halfspaces are even larger.

## Originality
PASS with residual bibliographic risk. The full text of Cheng--Basu arXiv:2609.32953v1 was inspected. It proves the universal sharp \(2/9\) theorem and gives sharpness by an asymptotically unbalanced area distribution, but it does not state an equal-area refinement or the constant \(8/27\). Searches using exact-constant, equal-area, balanced-slice, translation-rigidity, Brunn--Minkowski-equality, and Oertel-centerpoint formulations found no covering result. The related Cheng--Basu large-projection theorem addresses a different regime and does not imply this three-slice balanced statement.

## Value
PASS. The source's sharpness mechanism is maximally unbalanced, so asking what balance buys is a natural structural question rather than an arbitrary restriction. Exact balance forces a strong geometric rigidity that improves the certified depth from \(2/9\) to \(8/27\), a one-third relative improvement, at an explicit canonical point. The result cleanly separates a balanced regime from the source's extremal unbalanced construction and supplies a reusable mechanism for future quantitative stability questions. The claim is deliberately limited to a lower bound and does not overstate sharpness.

## Closest literature and limitations
The closest primary source is Cheng--Basu, arXiv:2609.32953v1. Their Theorem 1 gives the sharp universal \(2/9\) bound under midpoint inclusion, while their sharpness example uses slice areas tending to \(0,1/2,1/2\). The classical planar Grünbaum--Winternitz theorem supplies the \(4/9\) centroid halfplane bound used after translation rigidity. The present theorem does not determine the optimal equal-area constant, does not treat approximate balance, and does not extend to more slices or higher dimensions.
