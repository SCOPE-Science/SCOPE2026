# Exact classification of three-row ternary perfect hash families
## Finding
A three-row ternary perfect hash family of strength three is a \(3\times k\) array over \(\{0,1,2\}\) such that every three columns are pairwise distinct in at least one row. The largest possible number of columns is \(k=6\). Among the \(\binom{27}{6}\) labeled six-column subsets of \(\{0,1,2\}^3\), exactly \(36\) are perfect hash families. Under the standard equivalence generated by permuting the three rows and independently permuting the three symbols in each row, all \(36\) maximum families lie in one orbit. A representative is
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
0&0&1&1&2&2\\
0&1&0&2&1&2\\
0&1&2&1&2&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Thus the small extremal value is \(p_3(3,3)=6\), and the maximum family is unique up to the standard equivalence.

## Assumptions and scope
Columns are labeled vectors in \(\{0,1,2\}^3\). Repeated columns cannot occur in any strength-three perfect hash family with at least three columns, because a triple containing two equal columns cannot be separated by any row. Hence it is sufficient to consider subsets of the \(27\) possible columns. The equivalence group is generated by the \(3!\) row permutations and by a separate \(3!\) symbol permutation in each row; it has order \((3!)^4=1296\).

The primary literature direction uses the usual perfect-hash definition in which a family of functions separates every three-element domain subset injectively. With rows viewed as hash functions and columns as domain points, this is exactly the array definition above.

## Proof
The proof is a finite exhaustive classification, not a heuristic search. The accompanying verifier enumerates every six-subset and every seven-subset of \(\{0,1,2\}^3\). A candidate subset is accepted exactly when each of its three-element subsets has a coordinate in which the three entries are all distinct.

The enumeration finds \(36\) valid six-subsets and no valid seven-subset. Since the perfect-hash property is hereditary under deleting columns, the absence of a valid seven-subset rules out every family with more than six columns. The displayed six-column array supplies the matching lower bound.

For classification, the verifier computes the canonical image of each of the \(36\) maximum families under all \(1296\) standard equivalences. All canonical images agree. Independently, the orbit of that canonical representative contains exactly \(36\) labeled families, so its stabilizer has order \(1296/36=36\). This proves both the labeled count and uniqueness up to equivalence.

## Verification
Run `python verify.py certificate.json` with a standard Python 3 interpreter. The verifier constructs the \(27\)-column universe from first principles, tests all \(\binom{27}{6}=296010\) six-subsets and all \(\binom{27}{7}=888030\) seven-subsets, canonicalizes every maximum family under all \(1296\) equivalences, regenerates the representative orbit, and checks the certificate values. Its successful final line is `VERIFY_OK p3(3,3)=6 maxima=36 orbits=1 stabilizer=36`.

No integer-programming solver, randomized search, or external database is used in the correctness certificate.

## Relationship to prior work
Bshouty defines \((n,q,d)\)-perfect hash families and develops explicit construction-size bounds; the paper notes that small strengths have been studied but does not state this exact ternary three-row classification. The first arXiv appearance of that source is 2014-06-09.

Kim's 2003 thesis displays an explicit \(\operatorname{PHF}(3;6,3,3)\), so existence of six columns is old. The inspected construction does not state optimality or classify the maximum families. Walker and Colbourn's 2007 general tables report lower bounds on the largest number of columns supported by a given row count; for three rows and three symbols their table reaches six columns, while the paper explicitly says those tabulated values are lower bounds because exact perfect-hash-family numbers are unknown in general. Their statement therefore supplies the same lower bound but not the nonexistence of seven columns or the one-orbit classification. Shangguan and Ge study the asymptotic quantity \(p_3(3,q)\) and prove subquadratic growth, not the exact case \(q=3\).

Targeted searches under the exact array notation, the \(p_3(3,3)\) notation, and equivalence-class language found no statement implying the complete claim above. A plausible residual risk is an unindexed small-parameter classification in older theses or historical online tables.

## Limitations
The result is only for three rows, ternary alphabet, and strength three. The exhaustive certificate proves the finite statement exactly but gives no new asymptotic bound as the alphabet grows. Historical sources whose available metadata gives only a year were used only for comparison; no day-level date was inferred from them.
