# Incidence precision law for all inversion indicators of a random permutation

## Finding

Let \(\pi\) be uniform on \(S_n\), with \(n\ge3\). Put
\[
E=\{(i,j):1\le i<j\le n\},
\qquad
m=\binom n2,
\]
and define the inversion indicators
\[
I_{ij}=\mathbf 1\{\pi(i)>\pi(j)\}.
\]

Orient the complete graph \(K_n\) from the smaller to the larger label, and let \(B\) be its \(n\times m\) incidence matrix. Thus the column for \(e=(i,j)\) is
\[
b_e=e_i-e_j.
\]

For
\[
C=\operatorname{Cov}\bigl((I_e)_{e\in E}\bigr),
\]
the entire covariance matrix is
\[
\boxed{
C=\frac1{12}\bigl(I_m+B^\top B\bigr).
}
\tag{1}
\]
Its inverse has the equally sparse closed form
\[
\boxed{
C^{-1}
=
12\left(I_m-\frac1{n+1}B^\top B\right).
}
\tag{2}
\]

Consequently,
\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{spec}(C)
=
\left\{
\frac{n+1}{12}\text{ with multiplicity }n-1,\quad
\frac1{12}\text{ with multiplicity }\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}2
\right\},
}
\tag{3}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{
\det C
=
12^{-\binom n2}(n+1)^{n-1}.
}
\tag{4}
\]

For two distinct comparisons \(e,f\), put
\[
s_{ef}=b_e^\top b_f.
\]
If the comparisons are disjoint then
\[
s_{ef}=0,
\qquad
\operatorname{Cov}(I_e,I_f)=0,
\qquad
(C^{-1})_{ef}=0.
\tag{5}
\]
If they share one index then
\[
s_{ef}\in\{-1,1\}
\]
and
\[
\operatorname{Corr}(I_e,I_f)=\frac{s_{ef}}3.
\tag{6}
\]

Define the full-order linear partial correlation as the correlation of the least-squares residuals after projecting \(I_e\) and \(I_f\) on all the other inversion indicators. Then
\[
\boxed{
\rho^{\mathrm{lin}}_{ef\cdot-(e,f)}
=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{s_{ef}}{n-1},& e,f\text{ share one index},\\[2mm]
0,& e,f\text{ are disjoint}.
\end{cases}
}
\tag{7}
\]

Thus the covariance and precision matrices have exactly the same off-diagonal support: the line graph of \(K_n\). Full linear adjustment preserves the sign of every incident-pair association, reduces its magnitude from \(1/3\) to \(1/(n-1)\), and creates no inverse-covariance fill-in between disjoint comparisons.

The same formulas hold for the comparison indicators
\[
\mathbf 1\{X_i>X_j\}
\]
generated by any iid sample from a continuous distribution.

## Assumptions and scope

The permutation is uniform. Equivalently, the comparison vector may be generated by iid continuous observations, because their rank permutation is uniform.

The orientation \(i<j\) fixes signs. Changing the orientation of one graph edge simultaneously complements the corresponding Bernoulli comparison and conjugates the centered covariance and precision matrices by the corresponding sign change; the support and absolute correlations are unchanged.

“Linear partial correlation” means least-squares residual correlation. The comparison vector is discrete and highly non-Gaussian, so a zero precision entry is not asserted to be nonlinear conditional independence.

## Proof

Every comparison is fair:
\[
\Pr(I_{ij}=1)=\frac12,
\]
so
\[
\operatorname{Var}(I_{ij})=\frac14.
\tag{8}
\]

Consider two distinct comparisons. If their index pairs are disjoint, the relative order within one pair is independent of the relative order within the other pair. Hence their covariance is zero.

Now suppose the comparisons share one index. There are two sign types. If the common index occurs with the same incidence orientation in the two columns, both events correspond to the common observation being simultaneously above, or simultaneously below, two other observations. The joint probability is
\[
\frac13,
\]
so the covariance is
\[
\frac13-\frac14=\frac1{12}.
\tag{9}
\]
If the shared index has opposite incidence orientations, the two events force one of the three observations to be the middle rank. The joint probability is
\[
\frac16,
\]
so the covariance is
\[
\frac16-\frac14=-\frac1{12}.
\tag{10}
\]

For incidence columns,
\[
b_e^\top b_e=2,
\]
while for distinct columns
\[
b_e^\top b_f=
\begin{cases}
1,&\text{shared index with equal incidence sign},\\
-1,&\text{shared index with opposite incidence sign},\\
0,&\text{disjoint}.
\end{cases}
\]
Equations (8)--(10) are therefore exactly the matrix identity (1).

The complete-graph incidence matrix satisfies
\[
BB^\top=nI_n-J_n,
\tag{11}
\]
where \(J_n\) is the all-ones matrix. Since
\[
B^\top\mathbf 1=0,
\]
if
\[
Q=B^\top B,
\]
then
\[
Q^2
=
B^\top(BB^\top)B
=
nB^\top B
=
nQ.
\tag{12}
\]
Thus
\[
(I_m+Q)
\left(
I_m-\frac{Q}{n+1}
\right)
=
I_m+Q-\frac{Q+Q^2}{n+1}
=
I_m.
\]
This proves (2).

Equation (11) also shows that \(Q\) has eigenvalue \(n\) with multiplicity \(n-1\), and eigenvalue zero with multiplicity
\[
m-(n-1)
=
\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}2.
\]
Applying (1) gives (3), and multiplying the eigenvalues gives (4).

The diagonal entries of the precision matrix are all
\[
(C^{-1})_{ee}
=
12\left(1-\frac2{n+1}\right)
=
12\frac{n-1}{n+1}.
\tag{13}
\]
For \(e\ne f\),
\[
(C^{-1})_{ef}
=
-\frac{12}{n+1}s_{ef}.
\tag{14}
\]
The standard least-squares precision identity gives
\[
\rho^{\mathrm{lin}}_{ef\cdot-(e,f)}
=
-\frac{(C^{-1})_{ef}}
{\sqrt{(C^{-1})_{ee}(C^{-1})_{ff}}}.
\]
Substituting (13)--(14) proves (7).

Finally, if \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) are iid from any continuous distribution, their rank permutation is uniform and
\[
\mathbf 1\{X_i>X_j\}
\]
is exactly the corresponding rank-comparison indicator. Hence every formula above is distribution-free over continuous iid parent laws.

## Verification

The accompanying exact checker exhaustively enumerates permutations through \(n=7\). It reconstructs every covariance entry from the full sample space and compares it with the incidence formula (1).

Independently of enumeration, it checks
\[
(B^\top B)^2=nB^\top B,
\]
multiplies the proposed inverse by the covariance factor \(I+B^\top B\), verifies the determinant from the two eigenvalue multiplicities, and checks every stated partial-correlation class through \(n=14\).

These finite calculations are supplementary. The all-\(n\) theorem follows from the three-point ordering probabilities and the complete-graph incidence identity (11).

## Relationship to prior work

Işlak represents the inversion number of a uniform random permutation as a sum of pairwise comparison indicators generated by iid continuous variables and uses that representation for inversion-count asymptotics. This supplies the exact indicator representation used here, but the paper studies the scalar inversion statistic and related shuffle statistics rather than the covariance inverse of the full comparison vector.

Bhattacharya and Mukherjee study the random permutation graph whose edge set is precisely the inversion set of a random permutation. Their work focuses on degree sequences and limiting graph statistics. It therefore treats the same primitive edge indicators in aggregated form, but does not state the incidence-matrix covariance, its cut/cycle spectrum, or the precision and partial-correlation formulas above.

Later work on random permutation graphs continues to study graph-level statistics such as degrees, components, and cliques. That literature confirms that the inversion indicators are natural atomic random-graph variables, while leaving the present second-order vector geometry as a separate question.

The covariance calculation itself is elementary once the pairwise comparison representation is written down, and inversion-count variance formulas necessarily aggregate the same local covariances. The structural content here is the full matrix identity, its exact inverse with no sparsity fill-in, the cut/cycle spectral decomposition, and the resulting full-order linear partial correlations.

## Limitations

The theorem concerns second-order and linear-adjustment structure only. It does not claim that zero precision entries correspond to nonlinear conditional independence.

The all-comparison vector is special: deleting coordinates before inversion generally need not preserve the no-fill-in property.

The originality search was targeted. Older ranking, social-choice, linear-ordering, Hodge-decomposition, or permutation-statistics literature may contain the same incidence covariance or inverse under different notation.
