# Prime-factorial normal forms for low-support Jordan–Pólya numbers
## Finding
Let \(p_1=2,p_2=3,p_3=5,p_4=7,p_5=11\). For each \(1\le r\le5\), a positive integer \(n\) whose largest prime divisor is \(p_r\) is a Jordan–Pólya number if and only if it has the unique representation
\[
n=\prod_{i=1}^r (p_i!)^{c_i},\qquad c_1,\ldots,c_{r-1}\ge0,\quad c_r\ge1.
\]
Thus, if \(J_r(X)\) counts these integers at most \(X\), then
\[
J_r(X)=\frac{(\log X)^r}{r!\prod_{i=1}^r\log(p_i!)}+O((\log X)^{r-1}).
\]
The simple normal form first fails at the next prime support. The number \(14!\) is Jordan–Pólya and has largest prime divisor \(13\), but it is not in the submonoid generated by \(2!,3!,5!,7!,11!,13!\). Its unique integral coordinates in that prime-factorial basis are
\[
(1,-1,-1,1,0,1),
\]
equivalently
\[
14!\,3!\,5!=2!\,7!\,13!.
\]

## Assumptions and scope
A Jordan–Pólya number is a positive integer expressible as a finite product of factorials. Factors \(0!\) and \(1!\) are immaterial. The result concerns the exact strata whose largest prime divisor is one of \(2,3,5,7,11\), followed by the first obstruction at largest prime divisor \(13\). No assertion is made that analogous prime-factorial normal forms hold for larger supports.

## Proof
Fix \(r\le5\), and let \(q\) be the prime immediately following \(p_r\). If a Jordan–Pólya number \(n\) has largest prime divisor \(p_r\), then every factorial factor \(m!\) in any product representation must satisfy \(m<q\); otherwise \(q\mid m!\mid n\), contradicting the choice of largest prime divisor.

For all composite indices that can occur before the relevant next prime, the following identities reduce the factorial to prime-index factorials:
\[
4!=(2!)^2 3!,\qquad 6!=3!5!,\qquad 8!=(2!)^3 7!,
\]
\[
9!=2!(3!)^2 7!,\qquad 10!=3!5!7!,\qquad 12!=2!3!11!.
\]
For \(r=1,2,3,4,5\), these identities cover every composite \(m\) with \(2\le m<q\). Hence every Jordan–Pólya number in the stratum is generated by \(2!,3!,5!,7!,11!\) up to \(p_r!\). The converse is immediate because any such product is itself a product of factorials, and the condition \(c_r\ge1\) makes \(p_r\) the largest prime divisor.

Uniqueness follows from prime valuations. Let \(A_r=(v_{p_j}(p_i!))_{1\le j,i\le r}\). If \(j>i\), then \(p_j>p_i\) and \(v_{p_j}(p_i!)=0\), while \(v_{p_i}(p_i!)=1\). Therefore \(A_r\) is triangular with diagonal entries all equal to \(1\), so \(\det A_r=1\). The exponent vector of \(n\) determines one and only one integral coefficient vector \((c_1,\ldots,c_r)\), proving uniqueness.

For counting, put \(w_i=\log(p_i!)\) and \(L=\log X\). After replacing \(c_r\) by \(d_r+1\), the problem is to count nonnegative lattice points satisfying
\[
\sum_{i=1}^r w_i d_i\le L-w_r.
\]
For fixed positive weights, enclosing the unit cubes based at these lattice points between two translates of the weighted simplex gives
\[
\#\{d\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^r:\sum_iw_i d_i\le T\}
=\frac{T^r}{r!\prod_i w_i}+O(T^{r-1}).
\]
Taking \(T=L-w_r\) yields the stated asymptotic.

At the next support, include \(13!\) in the prime-factorial valuation basis. The same valuation matrix remains unimodular, so integral coordinates are unique. Direct valuation subtraction gives the coordinate vector \((1,-1,-1,1,0,1)\) for \(14!\), equivalently \(14!\,3!\,5!=2!\,7!\,13!\). The negative entries prove that \(14!\) does not lie in the nonnegative prime-factorial submonoid, while \(14!\) is itself a factorial and hence Jordan–Pólya. This establishes the claimed first failure.

## Verification
The accompanying `verify.py` checks the six factorial identities used in the reduction, verifies determinant \(1\) for each valuation matrix through \(11!\), computes the coordinate vector of \(14!\), checks the displayed cross-multiplied identity, and compares the semigroup generated by all factorials \(2!,\ldots,12!\) with the semigroup generated by \(2!,3!,5!,7!,11!\) through \(10^{10}\). Its successful output is

`VERIFY_OK bound=10000000000 semigroup_size=734 strata=2:33,3:203,5:290,7:187,11:20 coord14=1,-1,-1,1,0,1`

The finite semigroup comparison is corroborative only. The infinite classification and asymptotic follow from the symbolic reduction, unimodular valuation argument, and fixed-dimensional lattice-point estimate above.

## Relationship to prior work
Luca studies when a factorial itself can be represented as a product of smaller factorials, including finiteness questions and a density-zero result for admissible factorial indices. De Koninck, Doyon, Razafindrasoanaivolala and Verreault study the global counting function of Jordan–Pólya numbers and record several small factorial identities, including identities involving \(9!\) and \(10!\). Their global estimates do not imply the fixed-largest-prime normal forms or the explicit \((\log X)^r\) laws above. OEIS A001013 tabulates Jordan–Pólya numbers but does not supply this fixed-support theorem.

The present statement is therefore a fixed-support structural refinement: it gives canonical coordinates, exact free-monoid behavior through largest prime \(11\), an explicit asymptotic for each such stratum, and the natural boundary witness at \(14!\).

## Limitations
The theorem stops at largest prime divisor \(11\); \(14!\) shows why the same prime-factorial argument cannot simply be extended through largest prime divisor \(13\). It does not estimate the full Jordan–Pólya counting function. The asymptotic has a fixed-\(r\) error term and does not claim uniformity as the support grows. A residual originality risk remains that an unindexed note or exercise may have observed the same low-support normal form. The 2007 source was compared through publisher abstract and bibliographic metadata rather than complete full text; the later open full-text Jordan–Pólya paper was inspected directly.
