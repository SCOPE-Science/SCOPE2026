# Sharp coordinate-wise extraction bases for Pell solutions
## Finding
Let \(d\ge 2\) be a nonsquare integer, let \((a,y)=(X_1,Y_1)\) be the fundamental positive solution of
\[
X^2-dY^2=1,
\]
and define \((X_n,Y_n)\) by
\[
X_n+Y_n\sqrt d=(a+y\sqrt d)^n\qquad(n\ge 0).
\]
For an integer base \(b\ge 2\), consider the two coefficient-extraction formulas
\[
\mathcal X_b(n)=
\left\lfloor
\frac{b^{n^2+2n}-a b^{n^2+n}}
     {b^{2n}-2a b^n+1}
\right\rfloor\bmod b^n
\]
and
\[
\mathcal Y_b(n)=
\left\lfloor
\frac{y b^{n^2+n}}
     {b^{2n}-2a b^n+1}
\right\rfloor\bmod b^n.
\]
Call \(b\) \(X\)-admissible, respectively \(Y\)-admissible, when the displayed denominator is positive and the corresponding formula returns the required coordinate for every integer \(n\ge1\).

Then the least coordinate-wise admissible bases are
\[
b_X=2a(a+1)-1,\qquad b_Y=2a(y+1).
\]
In particular,
\[
b_Y<b_X,
\]
so the least common admissible base is \(b_X\). The second threshold can be substantially smaller than the common threshold; for the source example \(d=7\), \((a,y)=(8,3)\), it gives \(b_X=143\) and \(b_Y=64\).

## Assumptions and scope
The result concerns the two generating-function formulas above and the standard Pell sequence generated by the fundamental solution. It does not assert that these are globally shortest arithmetic expressions among all possible representations of Pell coordinates.

For every positive Pell solution one has \(a>y\), because \(d\ge2\) and \(a^2-dy^2=1\). The proof below uses only the recurrence
\[
S_{n+2}=2aS_{n+1}-S_n
\]
for each coordinate, positivity of the Pell coordinates, and \(a>y\).

The denominator is positive for integer \(b\ge2a\). For \(2\le b<2a\), its value at \(n=1\) is \(b(b-2a)+1<0\), so such bases are outside the admissible domain.

## Proof
Put \(\Lambda=b^n\). The rational generating functions are
\[
\sum_{m\ge0}X_m z^m=\frac{1-az}{1-2az+z^2},
\qquad
\sum_{m\ge0}Y_m z^m=\frac{yz}{1-2az+z^2}.
\]
Hence, after multiplying by \(\Lambda^n\), the two extraction fractions equal an integer finite prefix ending in \(X_n\), respectively \(Y_n\), plus the positive tails
\[
T_n=\sum_{i\ge1}\frac{X_{n+i}}{\Lambda^i},
\qquad
U_n=\sum_{i\ge1}\frac{Y_{n+i}}{\Lambda^i}.
\]
Thus a tail \(<1\), together with the coordinate being \(<\Lambda\), is sufficient for correct extraction.

For the \(X\)-coordinate, put
\[
b_X=2a(a+1)-1.
\]
At \(n=1\),
\[
T_1=\frac{b(2a^2-1)-a}{b^2-2ab+1},
\]
and \(T_1<1\) is equivalent to
\[
f(b)=b^2-(2a^2+2a-1)b+(a+1)>0.
\]
At \(b=b_X\), \(f(b_X)=a+1>0\). For \(n\ge2\), the recurrence gives
\[
X_m<(2a)^m.
\]
Also
\[
b_X^2-(2a)^3-2a=4a^4-6a+1>0,
\]
and multiplication by \(b_X>2a\) yields inductively
\[
b_X^n>(2a)^{n+1}+2a\qquad(n\ge2).
\]
Therefore
\[
T_n<
\frac{(2a)^{n+1}}{b_X^n-2a}<1,
\]
and \(X_n<b_X^n\). Increasing \(b\) only decreases the positive tail and increases the modulus, so every \(b\ge b_X\) is \(X\)-admissible.

Now prove sharpness for \(X\). At \(b=2a\), the \(n=1\) denominator is \(1\) and the displayed numerator is divisible by \(b\), so the formula returns \(0\), not \(a\). Suppose
\[
2a<b<b_X.
\]
The quadratic \(f\) has one root in \((0,1)\) and one in \((b_X-1,b_X)\), because
\[
f(0)=a+1>0,\quad f(1)<0,\quad f(b_X-1)<0,\quad f(b_X)>0.
\]
Thus \(T_1>1\). If the \(n=1\) formula nevertheless returned \(a\), then \(\lfloor T_1\rfloor\) would be a positive multiple of \(b\), so \(T_1\ge b\). Write \(b=2a+s\), where \(s\ge1\), and put
\[
D=b^2-2ab+1=bs+1.
\]
The inequality \(T_1\ge b\) implies
\[
D<2a^2-1,
\]
hence \(s\le a-1\).

At \(n=2\),
\[
T_2=
\frac{(4a^3-3a)b^2-(2a^2-1)}
     {b^4-2ab^2+1}.
\]
Subtracting the denominator from the numerator gives
\[
b^2\bigl(4a^3-a-b^2\bigr)-2a^2.
\]
For \(1\le s\le a-1\),
\[
4a^3-a-b^2
\ge
4a^3-9a^2+5a-1>0,
\]
with the last expression equal to \(5\) at \(a=2\) and larger for \(a\ge3\). Hence \(T_2>1\). On the other hand,
\[
T_2<b^2,
\]
because its numerator is \(<4a^3b^2\), while its denominator is \(>4a^3\). Since \(X_2=2a^2-1<b^2\), the nonzero integer \(\lfloor T_2\rfloor<b^2\) cannot disappear modulo \(b^2\). Thus the \(X\)-formula fails by \(n=2\). This proves that \(b_X\) is the least \(X\)-admissible base.

For the \(Y\)-coordinate, put
\[
b_Y=2a(y+1).
\]
At \(n=1\),
\[
U_1=\frac{y(2ab-1)}{b^2-2ab+1},
\]
and \(U_1<1\) is equivalent to
\[
g(b)=b^2-2a(y+1)b+(y+1)>0.
\]
At \(b=b_Y\), \(g(b_Y)=y+1>0\).

The recurrence gives
\[
Y_m\le y(2a)^{m-1}\qquad(m\ge1).
\]
For \(n\ge2\),
\[
U_n<
\frac{y(2a)^n}{b_Y^n-2a}<1,
\]
because
\[
b_Y^n-y(2a)^n
=(2a)^n\bigl((y+1)^n-y\bigr)>2a.
\]
Also \(Y_n<b_Y^n\). Hence every \(b\ge b_Y\) is \(Y\)-admissible.

It remains to prove sharpness. The base \(b=2a\) again returns \(0\) at \(n=1\), not \(y\). Suppose
\[
2a<b<b_Y.
\]
The quadratic \(g\) has one root in \((0,1)\) and its other root in \((b_Y-1,b_Y)\), so \(U_1>1\). If the \(n=1\) formula nevertheless returned \(y\), then \(\lfloor U_1\rfloor\) would be a positive multiple of \(b\), hence \(U_1\ge b\). With \(b=2a+s\) and \(D=bs+1\), this gives
\[
D<2ay.
\]
Consequently \(1\le s\le y-1\), so in particular \(y\ge2\).

At \(n=2\),
\[
U_2=
\frac{y\bigl((4a^2-1)b^2-2a\bigr)}
     {b^4-2ab^2+1}.
\]
The numerator minus the denominator is
\[
b^2B-(2ay+1),
\]
where
\[
B=4a^2(y-1)-4as-s^2+2a-y.
\]
Because \(s\le y-1\) and \(a\ge y+1\),
\[
B\ge
4a(a-1)(y-1)-(y-1)^2+2a-y>0.
\]
Thus \(U_2>1\). Moreover \(U_2<b^2\): its numerator is \(<4a^2yb^2\), while the denominator is \(>4a^2y\). Since \(Y_2=2ay<b^2\), again the nonzero integer \(\lfloor U_2\rfloor<b^2\) cannot vanish modulo \(b^2\). Therefore every \(b<b_Y\) in the positive-denominator domain fails by \(n=2\), proving that \(b_Y\) is least.

Finally,
\[
b_X-b_Y=2a(a-y)-1>0,
\]
so the least common base is \(b_X\).

## Verification
The accompanying `verify.py` independently constructs fundamental Pell solutions by continued fractions for every nonsquare \(2\le d<200\) with \(X_1\le50\). There are \(54\) such cases.

For each case it exhaustively checks every integer base in the positive-denominator range below the asserted coordinate threshold and confirms failure by \(n\le2\). It checks \(67{,}240\) sub-threshold \(X\)-bases and \(8{,}682\) sub-threshold \(Y\)-bases. It also checks each threshold and the next two bases through \(n=8\), for \(2{,}592\) successful coordinate evaluations.

The recorded output is:
`VERIFY_OK pell_cases=54 below_x=67240 below_y=8682 threshold_checks=2592`

These finite checks corroborate the algebra. The theorem for all nonsquare \(d\), all \(n\ge1\), and all integer bases in the stated ranges rests on the proof above, not on enumeration.

## Relationship to prior work
Prunescu and Sauras-Altuzarra give the two Pell generating-function extraction formulas for every sufficiently large base and, for \(d=7\), exhibit base \(143\) for the \(X\)-coordinate and base \(64\) for the \(Y\)-coordinate. Their statement does not give general least separate bases.

Dumitru and Prunescu subsequently prove that
\[
2X_1(X_1+1)-1
\]
is the least base that makes both coordinate formulas correct for every \(n\ge1\). Their necessity proof explicitly uses the two coordinates together and the coprimality of \(X_1\) and \(Y_1\). They also note the \(d=7\) pair of separate bases \(143\) and \(64\).

The present result separates the two optimization problems. It proves that the common threshold is already individually sharp for the \(X\)-formula, while the exact \(Y\)-threshold is
\[
2X_1(Y_1+1),
\]
which can be much smaller. The proof also identifies why a smaller base can sometimes survive the \(n=1\) reduction: a whole multiple of the modulus may be hidden in the first tail. In every such case the second extraction step forces a nonzero tail strictly between \(1\) and the modulus, so failure occurs by \(n=2\).

## Limitations
The result optimizes only the integer base within the two fixed rational generating-function formulas. It does not optimize operation count, total expression length, or intermediate bit length over all possible arithmetic-term or straight-line representations.

The originality comparison is literature-based rather than exhaustive in an absolute sense. The two closest inspected sources are the 2025 generating-function paper and the September 2026 straight-line-program paper; neither states the general coordinate-wise minimality theorem proved here.
