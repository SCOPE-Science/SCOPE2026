# Sharp two-piece min-max diameter of the three-dimensional cube

## Finding

Let
\[
Q=[0,1]^3.
\]
For a two-division of \(Q\) by a single hyperplane, define its cost to be the larger Euclidean diameter of the two closed convex pieces. If \(D_2(Q)\) denotes the minimum possible cost, then
\[
\boxed{D_2(Q)=\frac32}.
\]
A coordinate midplane, such as
\[
x_1=\frac12,
\]
attains the optimum. Conversely, every hyperplane cut leaves at least one piece of diameter at least \(3/2\).

## Assumptions and scope

A two-division is understood in the sense of Cañete, Fernández, and Márquez: one hyperplane cuts a convex body into two closed convex subsets with nonempty interior. The diameter of a compact set \(E\) is
\[
D(E)=\max\{\lVert x-y\rVert:x,y\in E\}.
\]
The min-max value \(D_2(Q)\) is the infimum, equivalently the attained minimum in this setting, of the larger diameter of the two pieces.

Only the three-dimensional unit cube and two pieces are claimed here. No classification of all optimal cutting hyperplanes is asserted.

## Proof

Translate and dilate the cube to
\[
C=[-1,1]^3.
\]
Diameters are multiplied by \(2\), so it is enough to prove that the optimal value for \(C\) is \(3\).

Consider any valid cutting plane
\[
H=\{x:\langle a,x\rangle=t\},
\]
where \(a\ne0\). Replacing \((a,t)\) by \((-a,-t)\) if necessary only swaps the two sides, so assume \(t\ge0\). One of the two pieces is
\[
C_-=C\cap\{x:\langle a,x\rangle\le t\}.
\]
It contains the central half
\[
P(a)=C\cap\{x:\langle a,x\rangle\le0\}.
\]
Therefore every cut has cost at least \(D(P(a))\). It remains to prove
\[
D(P(a))\ge3
\]
for every nonzero \(a\).

Signed coordinate permutations preserve \(C\). After applying one of them and multiplying \(a\) by a positive scalar, write
\[
a=(\alpha,\beta,\gamma),
\qquad
\alpha\ge\beta\ge\gamma\ge0,
\qquad
\alpha>0.
\]
There are two cases.

If
\[
\alpha\ge\beta+\gamma,
\]
then
\[
p=\left(\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\alpha},-1,1\right),
\qquad
q=(-1,1,-1)
\]
belong to \(P(a)\). Indeed, \(p\in C\) because
\[
0\le\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\alpha}\le1,
\]
and
\[
\langle a,p\rangle=0,
\qquad
\langle a,q\rangle=-\alpha+\beta-\gamma\le0.
\]
Their squared distance is
\[
\lVert p-q\rVert^2
=8+\left(1+\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\alpha}\right)^2
\ge9.
\]
Hence \(D(P(a))\ge3\).

If
\[
\alpha<\beta+\gamma,
\]
then \(\beta>0\), and
\[
p=\left(-1,\frac{\alpha-\gamma}{\beta},1\right),
\qquad
q=(1,-1,-1)
\]
belong to \(P(a)\). Since \(\alpha\ge\gamma\) and \(\alpha-\gamma<\beta\),
\[
0\le\frac{\alpha-\gamma}{\beta}<1,
\]
so \(p\in C\), while
\[
\langle a,p\rangle=0,
\qquad
\langle a,q\rangle=\alpha-\beta-\gamma<0.
\]
Now
\[
\lVert p-q\rVert^2
=8+\left(1+\frac{\alpha-\gamma}{\beta}\right)^2
\ge9,
\]
again giving \(D(P(a))\ge3\).

Thus every hyperplane cut of \(C\) has one piece of diameter at least \(3\). For the reverse inequality, the central coordinate cut \(x_1=0\) produces two boxes of side lengths \(1,2,2\), each of diameter
\[
\sqrt{1^2+2^2+2^2}=3.
\]
Therefore the exact two-piece min-max diameter of \(C\) is \(3\). Scaling by \(1/2\) yields
\[
D_2(Q)=\frac32.
\]

## Verification

The proof is exact and elementary. The packaged `verify.py` independently checks, with rational arithmetic, the two witness constructions over all ordered integer normals
\[
1\le\alpha\le30,
\qquad
0\le\gamma\le\beta\le\alpha,
\]
including membership in the central half and the lower bound \(\lVert p-q\rVert^2\ge9\). It also verifies that a coordinate half-box of \([-1,1]^3\) has squared diameter exactly \(9\) and that rescaling gives \(3/2\).

The replay output is:

`VERIFY_OK cube two-division diameter`

These finite checks are consistency tests only. The proof for every real normal vector is the two-case symbolic argument above.

## Relationship to prior work

Cañete, Fernández, and Márquez introduce the min-max diameter value \(D_n(C)\) for divisions generated by successive hyperplane cuts and prove existence of balanced optimal divisions. Their Theorem 2 gives general lower and upper bounds. Applied to the unit three-cube with two pieces, its orthotope construction gives the upper bound \(3/2\), for example by halving one coordinate. The published theorem does not give a matching cube-specific lower bound.

Their Remark 3 discusses how to choose the mesh parameters in the upper bound and reports numerical behavior for hypercubes. The inspected discussion does not state that the actual min-max diameter of the three-dimensional cube with two pieces equals \(3/2\).

Earlier work of Cañete and González Merino concerns minimizing maximum diameter for planar bisections. It does not cover the spatial cube. Targeted searches for the exact value, for cube or cuboid hyperplane bisections, and for equivalent min-max-diameter formulations did not locate a prior statement of the result above.

## Limitations

The argument proves only the case of two pieces in dimension three. It does not determine \(D_n(Q)\) for more pieces, higher-dimensional cubes, or general orthotopes, and it does not classify every optimal cutting plane.

The originality search included the terminology used in the primary source as well as older bisection terminology. A differently phrased or unindexed prior observation about cutting a cube remains a residual risk.

The earliest exact public date recorded here is the opening day of the 2022 RSME Bienal, whose abstract for this joint work cites a 2021 preprint. No exact public day for that cited 2021 preprint was verified, so no day in 2021 is invented.
