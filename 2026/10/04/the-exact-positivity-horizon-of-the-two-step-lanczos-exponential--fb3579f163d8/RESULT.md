# The exact positivity horizon of the two-step Lanczos exponential
## Finding
Let
\[
A=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)
\]
be positive definite, let
\[
b_i>0
\qquad
(1\le i\le n),
\]
and consider the standard two-step symmetric Lanczos approximation to
\[
e^{-tA}b.
\]
Write
\[
v_1=\frac{b}{\lVert b\rVert_2},
\qquad
T_2=V_2^{\mathsf T}AV_2,
\]
and let
\[
\theta_1<\theta_2
\]
be the two Ritz values of \(T_2\).

If the spectral support of \(b\) contains at most two distinct eigenvalues of \(A\), the Lanczos approximation is exact after at most two steps and is therefore componentwise positive for every
\[
t\ge0.
\]

Assume instead that the spectral support contains at least three distinct eigenvalues, and put
\[
L=\max_i\lambda_i.
\]
Then
\[
\lambda_{\min}(A)<\theta_1<\theta_2<L.
\]
The two-step Lanczos approximation has the exact coordinate form
\[
y_2(t)=q_t(A)b,
\]
where \(q_t\) is the affine interpolant of \(e^{-t\lambda}\) at the two Ritz values:
\[
q_t(\lambda)
=
\frac{
(\theta_2-\lambda)e^{-t\theta_1}
+
(\lambda-\theta_1)e^{-t\theta_2}
}{
\theta_2-\theta_1
}.
\]

Define
\[
t_*=
\frac{1}{\theta_2-\theta_1}
\log\!\left(
\frac{L-\theta_1}{L-\theta_2}
\right).
\]
Then \(t_*>0\), and the physical-coordinate sign pattern is completely classified:
\[
0\le t<t_*
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
y_2(t)>0,
\]
\[
t=t_*
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
y_{2,i}(t)=0
\ \text{exactly on the coordinates with }\lambda_i=L,
\]
and
\[
t>t_*
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
y_{2,i}(t)<0
\ \text{for every coordinate with }\lambda_i=L.
\]

Thus every nontrivial two-step Lanczos exponential on a positive diagonal system with at least three distinct active eigenvalues has a finite positivity horizon, even though the exact semigroup
\[
e^{-tA}b
\]
is strictly positive for every finite \(t\ge0\).

Dimension three is minimal for this sign failure. An exact three-dimensional witness is
\[
A=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,4),
\qquad
b=
\begin{pmatrix}
\sqrt5\\
\sqrt{15}\\
1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
For this pair,
\[
\alpha_1=\frac{13}{7},
\qquad
\beta_1^2=\frac{20}{49},
\qquad
\alpha_2=\frac{37}{14},
\]
so
\[
\sigma(T_2)=\left\{\frac32,3\right\}.
\]
Hence
\[
t_*=\frac23\log\!\left(\frac52\right).
\]
At
\[
t_0=\frac23\log4>t_*,
\]
the interpolation polynomial is exactly
\[
q_{t_0}(\lambda)=\frac{7-2\lambda}{16},
\]
and therefore
\[
y_2(t_0)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
5\sqrt5/16\\
3\sqrt{15}/16\\
-1/16
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
By contrast,
\[
e^{-t_0A}b
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\sqrt5\,4^{-2/3}\\
\sqrt{15}\,4^{-4/3}\\
4^{-8/3}
\end{pmatrix}
>0.
\]

## Assumptions and scope
The result is in exact arithmetic and concerns the standard symmetric Lanczos approximation
\[
y_m(t)=\lVert b\rVert_2V_m e^{-tT_m}e_1
\]
with \(m=2\). The matrix is diagonal positive definite and the starting vector has strictly positive physical coordinates.

The diagonal assumption is deliberate: it isolates coordinatewise sign loss from any sign changes generated by the exact semigroup itself. For diagonal \(A>0\), the exact evolution simply applies positive scalar decays to the coordinates.

If fewer than three distinct eigenvalues occur in the spectral support of \(b\), the Krylov subspace is already invariant by the second step and the approximation is exact. The finite horizon therefore concerns precisely the first nontrivial spectral case.

No claim is made here about coordinatewise positivity for higher Lanczos dimensions, nonsymmetric Arnoldi approximations, restarted variants, finite precision, or non-diagonal positive semigroups.

## Proof
For every polynomial \(q\) of degree at most one, the Lanczos polynomial exactness identity gives
\[
q(A)v_1=V_2q(T_2)e_1.
\]
Let \(q_t\) be the affine polynomial that interpolates
\[
f_t(\lambda)=e^{-t\lambda}
\]
at the two eigenvalues \(\theta_1,\theta_2\) of \(T_2\). Since \(T_2\) is symmetric with two distinct eigenvalues,
\[
q_t(T_2)=e^{-tT_2}.
\]
Multiplying the polynomial exactness identity by \(\lVert b\rVert_2\) gives
\[
y_2(t)=q_t(A)b.
\]
The displayed interpolation formula follows immediately.

We next prove the strict Ritz inequalities. By Rayleigh–Ritz,
\[
\lambda_{\min}(A)\le\theta_1<\theta_2\le L.
\]
Suppose \(\theta_2=L\). Then the two-dimensional Krylov space
\[
\mathcal K_2(A,b)=\operatorname{span}\{b,Ab\}
\]
contains a nonzero vector supported entirely in the \(L\)-eigenspace. Such a vector has the form
\[
p(A)b
\]
for an affine polynomial \(p\). Since the spectral support contains at least three distinct eigenvalues, \(p\) would have to vanish at at least two distinct eigenvalues below \(L\), forcing \(p\equiv0\), a contradiction. Hence
\[
\theta_2<L.
\]
The same argument at the lower endpoint gives
\[
\lambda_{\min}(A)<\theta_1.
\]

For \(t>0\), the affine interpolant \(q_t\) is strictly decreasing because
\[
e^{-t\theta_2}<e^{-t\theta_1}.
\]
Thus its smallest value on the physical spectrum is \(q_t(L)\). Directly,
\[
q_t(L)
=
\frac{
-(L-\theta_2)e^{-t\theta_1}
+
(L-\theta_1)e^{-t\theta_2}
}{
\theta_2-\theta_1
}.
\]
After multiplication by the positive factor
\[
(\theta_2-\theta_1)e^{t\theta_2},
\]
the sign is the sign of
\[
(L-\theta_1)
-
(L-\theta_2)e^{t(\theta_2-\theta_1)}.
\]
This expression is strictly decreasing in \(t\), is positive at \(t=0\), and has its unique zero at
\[
t_*=
\frac{1}{\theta_2-\theta_1}
\log\!\left(
\frac{L-\theta_1}{L-\theta_2}
\right).
\]
Therefore \(q_t(L)>0\) before \(t_*\), equals zero at \(t_*\), and is negative afterwards.

Because
\[
y_{2,i}(t)=q_t(\lambda_i)b_i
\]
with \(b_i>0\), this proves every sign assertion. At the crossing time, strict decrease of \(q_t\) implies
\[
q_{t_*}(\lambda)>0
\]
for every \(\lambda<L\), so zeros occur exactly on the top eigenspace.

If the spectral support has at most two distinct eigenvalues, its minimal polynomial has degree at most two and two-step Lanczos is exact. Hence no one- or two-dimensional diagonal system can show the sign failure, while the explicit three-dimensional example does.

For the witness, the normalized spectral weights are
\[
\frac5{21},
\qquad
\frac{15}{21},
\qquad
\frac1{21}.
\]
The first Lanczos diagonal coefficient is the weighted first moment
\[
\alpha_1=\frac{13}{7},
\]
and
\[
\beta_1^2
=
\frac{27}{7}
-
\left(\frac{13}{7}\right)^2
=
\frac{20}{49}.
\]
The second diagonal coefficient is
\[
\alpha_2=\frac{37}{14}.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\operatorname{tr}(T_2)=\frac92,
\qquad
\det(T_2)=\frac92,
\]
which gives the Ritz values \(3/2\) and \(3\). Substitution yields the stated horizon and exact negative coordinate.

## Verification
The accompanying `verify.py` uses only exact rational arithmetic.

It reconstructs the spectral moments of the explicit witness, verifies
\[
\alpha_1=\frac{13}{7},
\qquad
\beta_1^2=\frac{20}{49},
\qquad
\alpha_2=\frac{37}{14},
\]
and checks that the characteristic polynomial of \(T_2\) has roots
\[
\frac32
\quad\text{and}\quad
3.
\]

At the chosen time \(t_0\), the checker uses the exact values
\[
e^{-t_0(3/2)}=\frac14,
\qquad
e^{-t_0 3}=\frac1{16}
\]
to reconstruct
\[
q_{t_0}(\lambda)=\frac{7-2\lambda}{16}.
\]
It then checks the three exact coordinate multipliers
\[
\frac5{16},
\qquad
\frac3{16},
\qquad
-\frac1{16}.
\]
It also verifies the strict inequality \(t_0>t_*\) without floating-point logarithms by exponentiating the common positive factor:
\[
4>\frac52.
\]

The universal horizon theorem is proved analytically above. The finite checker corroborates the witness and the critical algebra rather than replacing the proof by enumeration.

## Relationship to prior work
Polynomial Krylov and Lanczos approximations of matrix exponentials are classical. Hochbruck and Lubich establish the polynomial exactness relation for Krylov bases and analyze norm error for matrix-exponential actions. Van den Eshof and Hochbruck formulate the symmetric Lanczos approximation
\[
V_m e^{-tT_m}e_1
\]
for positive semidefinite matrices arising from parabolic problems and study its convergence and preconditioning. Those approximation formulas and convergence results are prior work.

A particularly close comparison is Druskin's 2008 paper on monotonicity of the Lanczos matrix-exponential approximation. Its full theorem concerns strict decrease of the Euclidean approximation error and strict increase of the approximation norm as the Krylov dimension grows. Its auxiliary positivity lemma proves positivity of the reduced-coordinate vector
\[
e^{tT_m}e_1
\]
because the Jacobi matrix has positive off-diagonal entries. That is not coordinatewise positivity after multiplication by the signed Lanczos basis \(V_m\). The present result concerns precisely those original physical coordinates and is compatible with Druskin's reduced-coordinate positivity and norm monotonicity.

Targeted searches under matrix-exponential, Lanczos, Krylov, positivity-preserving, nonnegative-vector, sign-loss, and interpolation terminology did not locate a statement giving the exact two-step physical-coordinate positivity horizon above. The closest sources analyze norm convergence, reduced Jacobi-matrix positivity, preconditioning, or rational time-stepping positivity rather than sign preservation of the projected exponential in the original basis.

## Limitations
The result is deliberately a complete \(m=2\) classification on diagonal positive systems, not a theorem for arbitrary Krylov dimension. Higher-degree interpolating polynomials can have several sign changes, and their physical-coordinate positivity geometry is more complicated.

The analysis is exact-arithmetic. Loss of Lanczos orthogonality in finite precision introduces an additional mechanism not covered here.

The full Druskin monotonicity article was inspected and does not identify original-coordinate sign preservation as its monotonicity notion. Nonetheless, older matrix-function or positive-systems literature may contain the same two-step affine-interpolation observation under different terminology. That remains the main originality risk.
