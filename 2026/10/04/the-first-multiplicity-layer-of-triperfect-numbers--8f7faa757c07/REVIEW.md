## correctness

PASS

For \(\Omega(n)\le4\), the five exponent partitions give sharp prime-minimized abundancy upper bounds, the largest being \(14/5<3\), so no triperfect number occurs. If \(\Omega(n)=5\), an odd triperfect number is impossible because the modern stronger theorem on odd triperfect numbers gives at least twelve distinct prime factors; independently, the parity criterion also forces an odd triperfect integer to be a square, contradicting odd \(\Omega\). Writing \(n=2^a m\), the cases \(a=5,4,3,2,1\) close exactly as in the submitted proof. Fresh exact-rational reconstruction reproduced the critical maxima, the unique \(a=3\) solution \((q,r)=(3,5)\), and no \(a=2\) squarefree-three-prime solution. Cohen's Theorem 4 independently confirms that a triperfect integer divisible by \(2^2\cdot3\cdot5\) is \(120\).

## originality

PASS

Cohen proves strong divisibility consequences for even triperfect numbers and lower bounds for odd ones, while Kishore strengthens the odd case to twelve distinct prime factors. Those results do not state a global lower bound in prime-factor multiplicity or classify the equality layer. Cohen's \(120\) uniqueness theorem applies after the factors \(2^2\cdot3\cdot5\) are already known to divide the number; the audited argument must first force exactly that configuration from the sole hypothesis \(\Omega(n)=5\). No stronger Resultary theorem or exact multiplicity-layer table was found.
