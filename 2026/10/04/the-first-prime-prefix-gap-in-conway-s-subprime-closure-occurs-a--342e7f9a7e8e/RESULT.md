# The first prime-prefix gap in Conway's subprime closure occurs at generation \(27\)
## Finding
Let \(s(m)=m\) when \(m\) is prime, and let \(s(m)=m/\operatorname{lpf}(m)\) otherwise, where \(\operatorname{lpf}(m)\) is the least prime factor of \(m\). Define \(C_0=\{1\}\) and \(C_{n+1}=C_n\cup\{s(a+b):a,b\in C_n\}\). Let \(M_n=\max C_n\), let \(R_n\) be the least prime absent from \(C_n\), and let \(Q_n\) be the prime immediately preceding \(R_n\). Then \(Q_n=M_n\) for every \(1\le n\le 26\), while at generation \(27\) one has \(|C_{27}|=170224\), \(M_{27}=262331\), \(R_{27}=262321\), and \(Q_{27}=262313\). Hence generation \(27\) is the first stage at which \(C_n\) fails to contain every prime up to its maximum. In particular, \(262321\notin C_{27}\) but \(262331\in C_{27}\); the latter is generated by \(100188+162143=262331\) with both summands in \(C_{26}\).

Equivalently, the first index \(n\ge 1\) for which the least missing prime satisfies \(R_n\le M_n\) is \(n=27\). At that stage the gap is already visible inside the top prime interval:
\[
262313=Q_{27}<R_{27}=262321<M_{27}=262331.
\]

## Assumptions and scope
For a positive integer \(m\), write \(\operatorname{lpf}(m)\) for its least prime factor when \(m\) is composite, and define Conway's subprime function by
\[
s(m)=
\begin{cases}
m,&m\text{ prime},\\
m/\operatorname{lpf}(m),&m\text{ composite}.
\end{cases}
\]
The closure starts from \(C_0=\{1\}\) and obeys
\[
C_{n+1}=C_n\cup\{s(a+b):a,b\in C_n\}.
\]
The result is finite and exact through generation \(27\). It makes no claim about whether further prime-prefix gaps occur, how often they occur, or the size of later gaps.

## Proof
The computation is an exhaustive implementation of the defining recurrence using only integer operations.

Given an exact finite set \(C_n\), encode it as a bitset with bit \(j\) set exactly when \(j\in C_n\). The bitwise union of all shifts of that bitset by elements of \(C_n\) has bit \(m\) set exactly when \(m=a+b\) for some \(a,b\in C_n\). A least-prime-factor sieve up to \(2M_n\) then evaluates \(s(m)\) exactly for every attainable sum. Taking the union with \(C_n\) therefore produces exactly \(C_{n+1}\). Induction from \(C_0=\{1\}\) proves that every stage reconstructed by `verify.py` is the defining set, not a sample.

The reconstructed cardinalities through generation \(27\) are
\[
1,2,3,4,6,8,12,18,25,38,56,89,138,218,342,547,882,1429,2299,3705,5961,9615,15524,25057,40442,65247,105412,170224,
\]
which exactly agree with the published cardinalities through the same range. For every \(1\le n\le 26\), the exact set reconstruction gives \(Q_n=M_n\).

At generation \(26\),
\[
|C_{26}|=105412,\qquad M_{26}=162143.
\]
The exact next step gives
\[
|C_{27}|=170224,\qquad M_{27}=262331,
\]
and the least prime absent from \(C_{27}\) is \(262321\), whose preceding prime is \(262313\).

There is also a compact positive witness for the maximum:
\[
100188,162143\in C_{26},\qquad 100188+162143=262331.
\]
Since \(262331\) is prime, this pair generates \(262331\in C_{27}\).

For the missing prime, every sum from \(C_{26}\) is at most
\[
2M_{26}=324286<2\cdot262321.
\]
If a composite sum \(m\) satisfied \(s(m)=262321\), then \(m=q\cdot262321\) for a prime \(q\ge2\), forcing \(m\ge2\cdot262321\), impossible in this range. Thus \(262321\) could enter \(C_{27}\) only as a direct sum \(a+b=262321\) with \(a,b\in C_{26}\). The exact sumset check finds no such pair. Because \(262321>M_{26}\), it was not retained from the previous stage either. Hence \(262321\notin C_{27}\).

Together with \(Q_n=M_n\) for \(1\le n\le26\), this proves that generation \(27\) is the first prime-prefix failure.

## Verification
Run
`python3 verify.py`
in the artifact directory. The verifier reconstructs every \(C_n\) from \(n=0\) through \(n=27\), checks the published cardinality sequence, verifies the prime-prefix property through \(n=26\), certifies the values \(R_{27}=262321\), \(Q_{27}=262313\), and \(M_{27}=262331\), checks the positive witness for \(262331\), and exhaustively excludes all pairs from \(C_{26}\) summing to \(262321\). The generated `stage_stats.csv` records the exact stage statistics.

The replay uses no floating-point arithmetic, heuristic primality tests, random sampling, or timeout-based inference.

## Relationship to prior work
Caragiu, Vicol, and Zaki introduced these sets and published the cardinalities \(|C_n|\) through \(n=32\), with computational details, but their table records cardinalities and consecutive ratios rather than the first missing prime relative to the stage maximum.

Popescu's 2026 paper introduces \(M_n\), the first missing prime \(R_n\), and its predecessor \(Q_n\), and proves the asymptotic theorem
\[
M_n\sim Q_n\sim c\varphi^n
\]
for a positive constant \(c\). The finite statement above is not implied by that asymptotic equivalence: it determines the exact first stage where the two prime-frontier quantities cease to coincide. Direct inspection of the relevant source statements and searches for the exact values and equivalent first-gap formulation found no prior report of this onset.

OEIS A292772 tabulates only \(|C_n|\) for this closure, while OEIS A117818 records the subprime function itself; neither entry records the finite frontier statistic proved here.

## Limitations
This is an exact finite boundary result, not an asymptotic theorem. It does not establish a recurrence for \(M_n\), \(Q_n\), or \(R_n\), and it does not predict the next prime-prefix failure. The originality assessment is based on direct inspection of the most relevant primary sources, the exact OEIS entries, and semantic/exact-value searches; as usual, that cannot logically exclude every unindexed or unpublished computation.
