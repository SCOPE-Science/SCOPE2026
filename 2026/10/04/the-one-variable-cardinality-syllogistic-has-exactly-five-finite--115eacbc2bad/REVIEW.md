# Review

## Correctness

PASS. With one raw variable there are only two nouns, \(p\) and \(\bar p\). Every sentence reduces to one of eight nontrivial conditions on \(k=|p|\), plus tautologies and contradictions. Any consistent conjunction therefore collapses to one strongest lower bound and one strongest upper bound. Those bounds come from two five-element lists, and direct comparison of the \(25\) possibilities yields only \(\varnothing,T_1,T_2,T_3,E_2\).

Each of the five spectra has an explicit witness theory. The checker independently enumerates all \(256\) subsets of the eight nontrivial semantic conditions and reproduces the same five spectra.

## Originality

PASS. Jiang's 2026 paper classifies spectra for the unrestricted vocabulary and uses increasing families of nouns to realize arbitrary thresholds. It does not state the one-variable fragment classification or the sharp threshold ceiling at \(3\).

Targeted searches for one-variable syllogistic cardinality spectra, fixed-vocabulary spectrum hierarchies, and the specific five-spectrum list did not locate an equivalent theorem. The older Moss literature develops satisfiability and proof theory rather than this finite-spectrum slice.

## Value

PASS. The source paper identifies lower bounds and parity as the two global spectrum phenomena, but leaves open how much vocabulary is needed to realize them. The one-variable theorem gives the first exact vocabulary-size boundary: parity already appears with one raw variable, while lower-bound expressivity collapses to thresholds at most \(3\). This provides a natural base case for a finite-variable spectrum hierarchy and separates two expressive resources that look similar in the unrestricted theorem.

## Closest literature and limitations

The closest source is Jiang (2026), especially Definition 2.2, Definition 2.4, Theorem 3.16, the characteristic-theory construction, and the discussion explaining how chains of differently sized nouns force lower bounds.

The result does not address two or more raw variables, where the hierarchy is more complicated.
