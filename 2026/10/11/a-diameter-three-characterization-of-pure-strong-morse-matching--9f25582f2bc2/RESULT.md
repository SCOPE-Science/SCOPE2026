# A diameter-three characterization of pure strong Morse matching complexes of trees

## Finding

**Theorem.** Let \(T\) be a finite simple tree with \(N\ge3\) vertices. Let \(\mathcal{SM}(T)\) be the simplicial complex of strong discrete-Morse matchings arising from vertex filtrations as defined by Knudson and Owens-White [1]. Then
\[
\boxed{\mathcal{SM}(T)\ \text{is pure}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\operatorname{diam}(T)\le3.}
\]
The maximum-dimensional facets have cardinality \(N-1\) and are exactly the \(N\) oriented rooted-tree gradients \(F_r\), one for each vertex \(r\) of \(T\). Consequently, for every such tree,
\[
\mathcal{SM}_{\mathrm{pure}}(T)=\mathfrak M_{\mathrm{pure}}(T),
\]
where \(\mathfrak M\) denotes the ordinary discrete-Morse complex [2,3]. For \(\operatorname{diam}(T)\le3\) the full strong complex is therefore strongly collapsible. The strong collapsibility of \(\mathfrak M_{\mathrm{pure}}(T)\) itself was proved previously [2]; it is not asserted to be a new result here.

## Assumptions and scope

A tree is a finite connected simple graph without cycles; graph distance counts edges. Interpret \(T\) as a one-dimensional simplicial complex. A **vertex filtration** is a total order \(\prec\) on its vertices. When a vertex \(v\) is inserted, it contributes a strong gradient pair \((v,\{v,u\})\) exactly when its set of previously inserted neighbors consists of the single vertex \(u\). A descending link with zero or at least two vertices does not yield a pair: such a link is empty or is a discrete noncone. This is exactly the strong-gradient construction for a one-dimensional simplicial complex [1, Definition 3.2 and Algorithm 1]. A **strong matching** is any subset of a gradient realized by such an order. The simplicial complex \(\mathcal{SM}(T)\) is formed by all these subsets. Its **pure part** is generated by the facets of maximum cardinality, not by all inclusion-maximal faces.

Write \(v\to u\) for the incidence pair \((v,\{v,u\})\), pointing from the matched vertex toward its unique preceding neighbor. For a root \(r\), let \(p_r(v)\) be the next vertex on the unique path from \(v\ne r\) to \(r\), and set \(F_r=\{v\to p_r(v):v\ne r\}\). Every matching contains at most one arrow for each source vertex and at most one per underlying edge. These conventions fix orientation and purity. The minimum-size hypothesis is essential to the stated contractibility corollary: for a single edge the full strong complex is two isolated vertices, hence is pure but not contractible.

## Proof

**Step 1 (exact top facets).** A filtration ordering vertices by nondecreasing distance from \(r\), beginning with \(r\), inserts each nonroot vertex after its unique parent and before all its children, and therefore yields exactly \(F_r\). Hence every \(F_r\) is a strong matching of size \(N-1\). No matching has more than \(N-1\) incidences, since each uses a different edge of \(T\). Conversely, suppose a strong matching has \(N-1\) pairs. All edges are matched, all but one vertex are sources, and each matched vertex has precisely one outgoing arrow. A directed cycle is impossible: a cycle of length at least three would give an undirected cycle in \(T\), and a two-cycle would use one edge twice. Iterating arrows must therefore reach the unique unmatched vertex \(r\), so every arrow points along the tree toward \(r\). The matching is \(F_r\). Distinct roots yield distinct orientations, so exactly \(N\) top facets exist. The analogous characterization of ordinary maximum Morse matchings as rooted spanning trees [3, Proposition 3.1] gives \(\mathcal{SM}_{\mathrm{pure}}(T)=\mathfrak M_{\mathrm{pure}}(T)\).

**Step 2 (short diameters).** If \(\operatorname{diam}(T)\le3\), \(T\) is a star or a double star, i.e. there are one or two adjacent central vertices such that all others are leaves. In a star with center \(x\), every matched leaf arrow is leaf-to-center. If the center is matched, it can point to only one leaf \(a\); choose root \(a\). Otherwise choose root \(x\). Every arrow belongs to the chosen \(F_r\).

For a double star with centers \(x,y\), all matched leaves likewise point to their centers. If the center \(x\) points to a leaf \(a\) adjacent to \(x\), the filtration must insert \(x\) before \(y\): otherwise \(x\) would have both \(a\) and \(y\) as preceding neighbors, contrary to its required unique preceding neighbor. Symmetrically, a matched arrow \(y\to b\) to a leaf \(b\) forces \(y\) before \(x\). Thus **both leafward central arrows cannot occur simultaneously**. If \(x\to a\) occurs, root at \(a\); if instead \(y\to b\) occurs, root at \(b\). These choices accommodate any matched arrow along the central edge. If neither central vertex points to a leaf, root at \(y\) if \(x\to y\) occurs, and at \(x\) otherwise. In every case each matched arrow points toward that root and hence belongs to \(F_r\). Every simplex extends to a top facet, so \(\mathcal{SM}(T)\) is pure.

**Step 3 (sharp obstruction).** If \(\operatorname{diam}(T)\ge4\), fix a simple path of four edges with five consecutive vertices \(v_0,v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4\). Begin a filtration in this order: \(v_0,v_4,v_1,v_3\), followed by all other vertices in any order. Because \(T\) is a tree, \(v_1\) has exactly one earlier neighbor, \(v_0\), and \(v_3\) has exactly one earlier neighbor, \(v_4\). Therefore its strong gradient contains \(M=\{v_1\to v_0,v_3\to v_4\}\). For a rooted facet \(F_r\) to contain the first arrow, \(r\) must be in the component of \(T-\{v_0v_1\}\) containing \(v_0\). For it to contain the second arrow, \(r\) must belong to the component of \(T-\{v_3v_4\}\) containing \(v_4\). These components are disjoint. Thus \(M\) is contained in no top-dimensional facet. Since the complex is finite, \(M\) extends to an inclusion-maximal face; that face has strictly fewer than \(N-1\) vertices by Step 1. Consequently the complex is nonpure. The diameter threshold is sharp.

**Step 4 (homotopy consequence and provenance).** For short-diameter trees the full strong complex equals its pure part, and Step 1 identifies it with the ordinary pure Morse complex. Donovan, Lin, and Scoville [2, Proposition 18] previously proved that the latter is strongly collapsible for every tree with at least three vertices; hence so is the full strong complex in the pure regime. This does not assert new collapsibility of the ordinary complex.

## Verification

The accompanying `verify_tree_purity.py` uses only Python's standard library and builds all strong gradients by a subset dynamic program over possible vertex orders; it independently constructs every root facet, checks its incidence bits, and calculates graph diameters. It confirms the pure/nonpure dichotomy and top-facet equality for all \(1,440\) labeled trees of orders three through six, plus \(30\) labeled tests of orders seven through nine, totaling \(1,470\) instances. It also checks the basic leaf-facet elimination relation and the minimal four-edge path obstruction. Run `python3 verify_tree_purity.py` in a directory containing this file. Expected leading output: `VERIFY_OK 1470 labeled-tree test instances;`.

The exhaustive enumeration is finite corroboration, not an infinite proof. The infinite result follows from Steps 1--3, not from the program.

## Relationship to prior work

Knudson and Owens-White [1, Sections 3, 6, 7, 10] introduced the strong complex and computed stars, paths, cycles and several higher-dimensional examples. Their Proposition 6.1 calculates the pure strong path complex; their Proposition 6.2 and subsequent discussion exhibit nonpurity for the four-edge path. The present theorem supplies the **tree-wide if-and-only-if** classification, identifies every strong top facet with an ordinary rooted orientation, and proves the double-star extension and universal diameter-four obstruction.

Chari and Joswig [3, Proposition 3.1] identified ordinary Morse matchings on graphs with rooted forests. Donovan, Lin and Scoville [2, Proposition 18], building on Ayala et al., established strong collapsibility of ordinary pure tree Morse complexes. Neither is reclassified as a new result. The new ingredient is the filtration-specific comparison of **all** strong matchings to rooted top facets across arbitrary tree shapes.

## Limitations

This proves purity and equality of pure complexes, not the homotopy type of the **full** strong complex for trees of diameter at least four. It does not characterize the dimensions or number of all lower maximal faces, or generalize to graphs with cycles. The original 2026 paper already includes the path and star subfamilies; those cases are not separately novel. Source coverage cannot rule out mathematically equivalent unindexed publications or unpublished work. No independent audit has been performed.
