# Scientific review: cubic numerical-radius stability

## Correctness — PASS

The source definitions use \(R=\omega(T)\), \(Q=\omega_2(T)\), and phase-self-adjoint distance \(d(T)\). The proof reconstructs the support function of the centrally symmetric numerical range, rotates a maximal radial point onto the real axis, and integrates a nonnegative excess on a one-sided interval forced by the transverse support height. The integral identity and inequality \(s-\arctan s\ge0\) give the cubic coefficient for every nonzero bounded operator. The distance identity minimizes the norm of the imaginary part over phases; existence of a minimizing phase is justified. The explicit normal \(4\times4\) model gives \(d\sim\varepsilon\) and deficit \(\sim4\varepsilon^3/(3\pi)\). No finite enumeration is used for the universal theorem.

## Originality — PASS, with residual risks

Audeh--Moradi--Sahoo, arXiv:2610.04844v1, Theorem 4, proves \(Q^2\ge R^2/2\), but its proof yields neither an inverse near-equality modulus nor a phase-self-adjoint operator-norm distance estimate. Their other theorems address bounds from Cartesian parts and operator matrices. The classical Cartesian paper of Kittaneh--Moslehian--Yamazaki proves an angular supremum identity, not the difference between angular mean square and squared supremum. The present claim applies to every bounded operator, with normal rank-four sharpness. Searches for angular integral, support-function stability, equivalent phase distance, and cubic normal counterexamples did not identify a stronger encompassing result. The principal residual risk is unindexed older convex-support stability theory; abstracts/full texts could not be material-read for some secondary sources. These are uncertainties, not purported proofs of uniqueness.

## Mathematical value — PASS

The original inequality gives only qualitative closeness at equality; the new estimate yields a universal, dimension-free strong-operator-norm stability statement with a sharp power-law modulus and an explicit normal obstruction showing that the exponent cannot be improved. Such a near-extremizer theorem describes structurally which operators nearly attain the sharp angular inequality, a natural mathematical question motivated by the new invariant. Sharpness is genuinely analytic, rather than a finite table.

## Limits of verification

The constant \(1/(2\pi)\) is not claimed optimal. Some adjacent historical full texts were inaccessible and only their available material was compared. The same mathematical analysis verifies the current proof; no independent certification is implied.
