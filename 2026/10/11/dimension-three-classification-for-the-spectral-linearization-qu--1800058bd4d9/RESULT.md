# Dimension-three classification for the spectral-linearization question
## Finding
Let \(a=(a_1,a_2,a_3)\in\mathbb R^3\). There exists a linear map
\[
\mathcal L_a:\mathrm S_2\mathbb R^3\longrightarrow \mathrm S_2\mathbb R^6
\]
such that, for every real symmetric \(3\times3\) matrix \(A\), the eigenvalue multiset of \(\mathcal L_a(A)\) is
\[
\{\langle \sigma a,\lambda(A)\rangle:\sigma\in\mathfrak S_3\}
\]
if and only if at least two coordinates of \(a\) are equal.

Consequently, the spectral-linearization question posed by Sanyal and Saunderson has a negative answer already in dimension \(3\) for every weight with three distinct coordinates. The repeated-coordinate cases are exactly the realizable ones.

## Assumptions and scope
Here \(\mathrm S_2\mathbb R^m\) denotes the real symmetric \(m\times m\) matrices, \(\lambda(A)\) is the multiset of the three eigenvalues of \(A\), and \(\mathfrak S_3\) permutes coordinates of \(a\). The target dimension \(6=3!\) is exactly the dimension required in the question.

Adding a multiple of \((1,1,1)\), multiplying \(a\) by a nonzero scalar, or permuting its coordinates preserves existence: these operations respectively add a scalar multiple of \(\operatorname{tr}(A)I_6\), scale the map, or leave the target multiset unchanged.

## Proof
First suppose, after permuting coordinates, that \(a=(\alpha,\beta,\beta)\). Put
\[
B(A)=\beta\operatorname{tr}(A)I_3+(\alpha-\beta)A
\]
and
\[
\mathcal L_a(A)=B(A)\oplus B(A).
\]
If the eigenvalues of \(A\) are \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3\), then the eigenvalues of \(B(A)\) are
\[
\beta(\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\lambda_3)+(\alpha-\beta)\lambda_i
=\alpha\lambda_i+\beta\!\!\sum_{j\ne i}\lambda_j.
\]
Each occurs twice in the direct sum, exactly matching the six permutation pairings. Thus every repeated-coordinate weight is realizable.

Now assume that \(a_1,a_2,a_3\) are pairwise distinct and suppose such a map \(\mathcal L_a\) exists. For a diagonal matrix \(D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,d_2,d_3)\), write
\[
\ell_\sigma(d)=\langle \sigma a,d\rangle.
\]
Choose \(d^0\) so that the six values \(\ell_\sigma(d^0)\) are distinct. In an orthonormal eigenbasis of \(\mathcal L_a(D^0)\), every \(\mathcal L_a(\operatorname{diag}(h))\) is diagonal. Indeed, for sufficiently small \(t\), each ordered eigenvalue of
\[
\mathcal L_a(D^0+t\operatorname{diag}(h))
\]
is a linear function of \(t\). The standard second-derivative formula for a simple eigenvalue of a symmetric pencil gives, for the largest eigenvalue,
\[
0=2\sum_{j>1}\frac{|u_j^{\mathsf T}\mathcal L_a(\operatorname{diag}(h))u_1|^2}{\mu_1-\mu_j}.
\]
All denominators are positive, so every coupling from \(u_1\) vanishes. Restricting to its orthogonal complement and iterating proves simultaneous diagonalization. Labeling the basis by \(\sigma\in\mathfrak S_3\), one therefore has
\[
\mathcal L_a(D)=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\ell_\sigma(d)\bigr)_{\sigma\in\mathfrak S_3}.
\]

For \(p<q\), let \(H_{pq}=E_{pq}+E_{qp}\) and write
\[
B^{pq}=\mathcal L_a(H_{pq})
\]
in this fixed basis. The first derivatives of the eigenvalues of \(D+tH_{pq}\) vanish at \(t=0\), hence every diagonal entry of \(B^{pq}\) is zero. Comparing second derivatives gives, for every generic \(d\) and every \(\sigma\),
\[
\sum_{\tau\ne\sigma}
\frac{(B^{pq}_{\sigma\tau})^2}
     {(\sigma a-\tau a)\cdot d}
=
\frac{(\sigma a)_p-(\sigma a)_q}{d_p-d_q}.
\tag{1}
\]
For distinct coordinates of \(a\), the six permutation vectors are the vertices of a genuine permutahedron. From any fixed vertex, no two difference vectors to other vertices are proportional. Hence the five denominator hyperplanes on the left of (1) are distinct. The only one equal to \(d_p=d_q\) comes from the permutation obtained by swapping the \(p\)- and \(q\)-positions. Pole comparison in (1) therefore forces
\[
B^{pq}_{\sigma\tau}=0
\]
unless \(\tau=\sigma\circ(pq)\), and on that matching
\[
(B^{pq}_{\sigma,\sigma\circ(pq)})^2
=
\bigl((\sigma a)_p-(\sigma a)_q\bigr)^2.
\tag{2}
\]

Thus, for every zero-diagonal symmetric matrix
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}
0&x&y\\
x&0&z\\
y&z&0
\end{pmatrix},
\]
the matrix \(\mathcal L_a(A)\) is supported on the Cayley graph of \(\mathfrak S_3\) generated by its three transpositions. This graph is bipartite by permutation parity, so
\[
\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\mathcal L_a(A)^3\bigr)=0.
\tag{3}
\]

On the other hand, summing the cubes of the prescribed eigenvalues gives the symmetric identity
\[
\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak S_3}\langle \sigma a,\lambda\rangle^3
=
\alpha(\operatorname{tr}A)^3+
\beta\operatorname{tr}(A)\operatorname{tr}(A^2)+
\gamma\operatorname{tr}(A^3),
\]
where the coefficient needed here is
\[
\gamma=
(a_1-2a_2+a_3)(a_1+a_2-2a_3)(2a_1-a_2-a_3).
\tag{4}
\]
For the zero-diagonal matrix above, \(\operatorname{tr}(A)=0\) and
\[
\operatorname{tr}(A^3)=6xyz.
\]
Equations (3)--(4), valid for all \(x,y,z\), imply \(\gamma=0\). Since the coordinates of \(a\) are distinct, one coordinate is therefore the arithmetic mean of the other two. By the invariances noted above, it remains only to rule out
\[
a=(1,0,-1).
\]

For this normalized weight, (2) and parity imply that every zero-diagonal \(A\) has
\[
\mathcal L_a(A)=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&C(x,y,z)\\
C(x,y,z)^{\mathsf T}&0
\end{pmatrix},
\]
where \(C\) is a \(3\times3\) matrix whose entries are linear forms in \(x,y,z\). Therefore
\[
\det\mathcal L_a(A)=-\det C(x,y,z)^2.
\tag{5}
\]
The prescribed six eigenvalues are all ordered differences \(\lambda_i-\lambda_j\), so their product is
\[
-\prod_{i<j}(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)^2=-\Delta(A),
\tag{6}
\]
where \(\Delta(A)\) is the discriminant of the characteristic polynomial of \(A\). For the displayed zero-diagonal matrix,
\[
\det(tI-A)=t^3-(x^2+y^2+z^2)t-2xyz,
\]
hence
\[
\Delta(A)=4(x^2+y^2+z^2)^3-108x^2y^2z^2.
\tag{7}
\]
Equations (5)--(7) would make \(\Delta(A)\) a square polynomial. Setting \(z=0\) would then make
\[
4(x^2+y^2)^3
\]
a square in \(\mathbb R[x,y]\), impossible because \(x^2+y^2\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb R\) and occurs with odd exponent \(3\). This contradiction excludes the last distinct-coordinate case and completes the classification.

## Verification
The accompanying `verify.py` independently expands the cubic trace identity, checks the factorization (4), checks the zero-diagonal characteristic polynomial and discriminant identity (7), and tests the explicit repeated-coordinate construction on exact rational examples. It is corroborative; the proof above is the arbitrary-matrix argument.

## Relationship to prior work
Sanyal and Saunderson introduced this exact linearization problem while constructing spectrahedral representations of spectral polyhedra. They prove the required construction for dimension \(2\), note Schur-functor constructions for certain special weights, state that they had not been able to construct the map for dimension at least \(3\), and ask whether it exists. Their published paper retains the question. The present result resolves the first open dimension completely: in dimension \(3\), repeated-coordinate weights are precisely the realizable weights, and all weights with three distinct coordinates fail.

Searches of the published-result database and the web for the exact question, the notation \(\mathcal L_a\), the permutation-pairing eigenvalue condition, and dimension-three solutions found the source question but no prior dimension-three classification or obstruction. This is not a proof that no unindexed source exists; that residual risk is recorded in the review.

## Limitations
The theorem is only for \(d=3\). It does not classify existence in dimensions \(d\ge4\), nor does it optimize spectrahedral representation size by other constructions. The nonexistence proof uses the exact requirement that one \(6\times6\) symmetric linear pencil realize all \(3!\) permutation pairings for every symmetric input matrix.
