# Exact Euclidean Banach--Mazur distance of the \((3/2,3)\) mixed-power Luxemburg plane
## Finding
Let \(X\) be \(\mathbb R^2\) with Luxemburg unit ball
\[
B=\{(x,y): |x|^{3/2}+|y|^3\le 1\}.
\]
Let \(z\in(0,1)\) be the unique root of
\[
z^3+z^2+z-1=0,
\]
put \(u=z^3\), and define
\[
r_*=\frac{1+z+z^2}{2\,[z(1+z)]^{1/3}}.
\]
Then the Euclidean pullback
\[
H_*(x,y)=\sqrt{r_*x^2+y^2}
\]
is Banach--Mazur optimal, uniquely up to an overall positive scalar, and
\[
d_{\mathrm{BM}}(X,\ell_2^2)^2=(1+z^3)(1-z^3)^{1/3}
=1.094867917570886489\ldots .
\]
Equivalently, \(d_{\mathrm{BM}}(X,\ell_2^2)^6\) is the unique positive root of
\[
W^3+76W^2+4080W-5488=0.
\]

Numerically,
\[
z=0.54368901269207636157\ldots,\qquad
r_*=0.97495070568547563567\ldots,
\]
and
\[
d_{\mathrm{BM}}(X,\ell_2^2)=1.04635936349367395006\ldots .
\]

## Assumptions and scope
The space is the two-coordinate Musielak--Orlicz/Luxemburg space generated by the Young functions \(s\mapsto s^{3/2}\) and \(s\mapsto s^3\). The claim concerns arbitrary real linear isomorphisms to the Euclidean plane, not only diagonal maps. The exponents straddle the Hilbert exponent \(2\), so the one-variable Euclidean profile is neither globally convex nor globally concave.

## Proof
Let \(N\) denote the Luxemburg norm of \(X\). For a positive-definite real symmetric matrix \(Q\), write
\[
m(Q)=\inf_{N(w)=1} w^TQw,\qquad
M(Q)=\sup_{N(w)=1} w^TQw.
\]
The squared distortion of the corresponding Euclidean pullback is \(M(Q)/m(Q)\).

The norm \(N\) is invariant under the two independent coordinate sign changes. Averaging \(Q\) over these four sign conjugations removes the off-diagonal terms while preserving the same two-sided inequalities
\[
m(Q)N(w)^2\le w^TQw\le M(Q)N(w)^2.
\]
Hence some diagonal quadratic form has distortion no larger than that of \(Q\). It is therefore enough first to optimize diagonal forms.

After an overall positive rescaling, write the diagonal form as
\[
H_r(x,y)^2=r x^2+y^2,\qquad r>0.
\]
On the first-quadrant unit sphere set
\[
t=|x|^{3/2}\in[0,1].
\]
Then \(|x|^2=t^{4/3}\), \(|y|^2=(1-t)^{2/3}\), and
\[
F_r(t)=H_r(x,y)^2=r t^{4/3}+(1-t)^{2/3}.
\]
Thus the squared distortion is
\[
D(r)=\frac{\max_{0\le t\le1}F_r(t)}{\min_{0\le t\le1}F_r(t)}.
\]

A derivative calculation gives
\[
F_r'(t)=\frac43 r t^{1/3}-\frac23(1-t)^{-1/3},
\]
so an interior critical point satisfies
\[
t(1-t)=\frac1{8r^3}.
\]
If \(0<r<2^{-1/3}\), there is no critical point and \(F_r\) is strictly decreasing, hence \(D(r)=1/r\), which strictly decreases with \(r\).

For \(r\ge2^{-1/3}\), let \(u\in(0,1/2]\) be the smaller critical point. The larger one is \(v=1-u\), and
\[
r=\frac1{2[u(1-u)]^{1/3}}.
\]
At a critical point \(t\),
\[
F_r(t)=\frac{1-t/2}{(1-t)^{1/3}}.
\]
Consequently
\[
m_c(u):=F_r(u)=\frac{2-u}{2(1-u)^{1/3}},
\qquad
M_c(u):=F_r(1-u)=\frac{1+u}{2u^{1/3}}.
\]
The endpoint values are \(F_r(0)=1\) and \(F_r(1)=r\).

The equality \(m_c(u)=r\) is equivalent to
\[
u^{1/3}(2-u)=1.
\]
Writing \(z=u^{1/3}\), the nontrivial solution in \((0,1)\) is exactly the unique root of
\[
z^3+z^2+z-1=0,
\]
because
\[
z(2-z^3)=1
\iff
z^4-2z+1=(z-1)(z^3+z^2+z-1)=0.
\]
Denote this solution by \(z\) and put \(u_*=z^3\). Since the cubic is strictly increasing on \([0,\infty)\), this root is unique. It satisfies \(u_*<1/2\).

There are three monotonic regimes. First, while the outer maximum is the endpoint value \(1\) and the minimum is \(r\), one has
\[
D(r)=\frac1r=2[u(1-u)]^{1/3},
\]
which decreases as \(u\) decreases from \(1/2\). Second, once the interior maximum dominates but \(m_c(u)\ge r\), one has
\[
D(r)=\frac{M_c(u)}r=(1+u)(1-u)^{1/3}.
\]
Its logarithmic derivative with respect to \(u\) is
\[
\frac1{1+u}-\frac1{3(1-u)}>0
\quad\text{for }0<u<1/2,
\]
so this distortion decreases as \(r\) increases, equivalently as \(u\) decreases. Third, after \(m_c(u)\le r\), one has
\[
D(r)=\frac{M_c(u)}{m_c(u)}
=\frac{1+u}{2-u}\left(\frac{1-u}u\right)^{1/3}.
\]
Its logarithmic derivative is
\[
\frac1{1+u}+\frac1{2-u}-\frac1{3(1-u)}-\frac1{3u}
=
\frac{8u(1-u)-2}{3u(1-u)(1+u)(2-u)}<0
\]
for \(0<u<1/2\). Hence in this regime the distortion increases as \(r\) increases. Therefore the unique global minimum occurs exactly at the transition
\[
m_c(u_*)=r_*.
\]

Using \(u_*=z^3\) and \(1-z^3=z+z^2=z(1+z)\), the optimal coefficient is
\[
r_*=\frac{1+z+z^2}{2[z(1+z)]^{1/3}}.
\]
At the optimum the minimum equals \(r_*\), attained both at \(t=u_*\) and at the endpoint \(t=1\), while the maximum is attained at \(t=1-u_*\). Therefore
\[
d_{\mathrm{BM}}(X,\ell_2^2)^2
=\frac{M_c(u_*)}{r_*}
=(1+u_*)(1-u_*)^{1/3}
=(1+z^3)(1-z^3)^{1/3}.
\]

It remains to exclude tilted ellipses and prove uniqueness. Suppose an arbitrary positive-definite \(Q\) is optimal. Its sign average must be the unique optimal diagonal form up to scale. After rescaling, write
\[
Q=\begin{pmatrix}r_*&c\\c&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
At the maximizing absolute-value pair corresponding to \(t=1-u_*\), the two sign choices give quadratic values
\[
M_* \pm 2c|xy|.
\]
If \(c\ne0\), one of these is strictly larger than \(M_*\). At the axis point corresponding to \(t=1\), the value remains \(r_*\), so the minimum is at most \(r_*\). The distortion would therefore be strictly larger than the optimum, a contradiction. Thus \(c=0\), proving uniqueness up to scale.

Finally, cubing the formula for the squared distance and eliminating \(z\) from
\[
z^3+z^2+z-1=0
\]
gives
\[
W^3+76W^2+4080W-5488=0,
\qquad
W=d_{\mathrm{BM}}(X,\ell_2^2)^6.
\]
This cubic has exactly one positive root by Descartes' rule of signs.

## Verification
Every extremum of the one-variable profile is accounted for explicitly. The critical-point equation has no interior solution for \(r<2^{-1/3}\) and exactly two for \(r>2^{-1/3}\), with the larger critical point equal to \(1-u\). The ordering changes only when an interior critical value meets an endpoint value; the two relevant ratios have derivatives of fixed sign on \(0<u<1/2\), so no unexamined local minimum of the distortion remains.

The proof does not use finite sampling as a substitute for the global argument. The decimal values above are consistency checks only. Substitution gives
\[
F_{r_*}(u_*)=F_{r_*}(1)=r_*,
\]
and
\[
F_{r_*}(1-u_*)=1.06744224886812295177\ldots .
\]

## Relationship to prior work
Kobos, arXiv:1305.6288, gives a finite-dimensional Musielak--Orlicz definition by coordinate Young functions and the Luxemburg gauge, and explicitly discusses Banach--Mazur distance in the surrounding finite-dimensional geometry. Its theorems concern equilateral dimension, not the optimal Euclidean ellipse of this mixed-power plane.

The closest published comparison located with the same exponents is a conjugate Day--James plane that uses \(\ell_3\) in two quadrants and \(\ell_{3/2}\) in the other two. That is a different, nonabsolute unit ball and does not imply the present coordinatewise modular result. Another close published comparison treats the different globally mixed norm \((\|\cdot\|_p^2+\lambda\|\cdot\|_q^2)^{1/2}\). Published comparison results cover the boundary case with one exponent equal to \(2\) and the strictly superquadratic regime \(2<p<q\); neither covers the cross-Hilbert pair \((3/2,3)\), where the optimal ellipse is anisotropic.

A modern general treatment of distance ellipsoids proves uniqueness for planar symmetric convex bodies, but it does not compute the distance or optimal ellipse for this Musielak--Orlicz body. The uniqueness proved above is therefore also obtained directly from the contact structure used for the exact value.

## Limitations
The theorem is for the single canonical Hölder-conjugate cross-Hilbert pair \((3/2,3)\). It does not claim a closed formula for all \(1<p<2<q\); in that larger region the ordering of endpoint and interior contacts has additional parameter regimes. No claim is made about higher-dimensional mixed-power Musielak--Orlicz spaces.
