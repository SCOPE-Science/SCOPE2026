# Exact Euler–Lagrange profile of the affine-regular hexagonal plane
## Finding
Let \(X_H=(\mathbb R^2,N)\), where
\[
N(s,t)=\max\{|s|,|t|,|s-t|\}.
\]
For \(\lambda,\mu>0\), put
\[
M=\max\{\lambda,\mu\},\qquad m=\min\{\lambda,\mu\},\qquad r=\frac mM.
\]
For the Liu–Li Euler–Lagrange constant
\[
L_{\mathrm{YJ}}(\lambda,\mu,X)
=
\sup_{(x,y)\ne(0,0)}
\frac{\|\lambda x+\mu y\|^2+\|\mu x-\lambda y\|^2}
{(\lambda^2+\mu^2)(\|x\|^2+\|y\|^2)},
\]
one has the exact profile
\[
L_{\mathrm{YJ}}(\lambda,\mu,X_H)
=
\frac{2+r^2+r\sqrt{r^2+4}}{2(1+r^2)}.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
L_{\mathrm{YJ}}(\lambda,\mu,X_H)
=
\frac{2M^2+m^2+m\sqrt{m^2+4M^2}}{2(M^2+m^2)}.
\]
The profile has global maximum
\[
\frac43,
\]
attained exactly when one of \(\lambda,\mu\) is \(\sqrt2\) times the other. At \(\lambda=\mu\), the formula gives
\[
L_{\mathrm{YJ}}(\lambda,\lambda,X_H)=\frac{3+\sqrt5}{4}.
\]

## Assumptions and scope
All scalars are real. The unit ball of \(X_H\) is the centrally symmetric hexagon with vertices
\[
(1,1),\ (1,0),\ (0,-1),\ (-1,-1),\ (-1,0),\ (0,1).
\]
The definition is homogeneous in the pair \((\lambda,\mu)\), and interchanging \(x\) and \(y\) shows
\[
L_{\mathrm{YJ}}(\lambda,\mu,X)=L_{\mathrm{YJ}}(\mu,\lambda,X).
\]
Thus it suffices to normalize the larger parameter to \(1\) and write the smaller as \(r\in(0,1]\).

## Proof
Write nonzero vectors as \(x=a u\) and \(y=b v\), where \(a,b\ge0\) and \(u,v\in S_{X_H}\). By homogeneity in \((a,b)\), impose
\[
a^2+b^2=1.
\]
For fixed \(a,b\), define
\[
F_{a,b}(u,v)
=
N(a u+r b v)^2+N(r a u-b v)^2.
\]
On the product of any two edges of the hexagonal unit sphere, \(F_{a,b}\) is convex jointly in \((u,v)\): the square of a norm is convex, affine composition preserves convexity, and the two summands have nonnegative coefficients. Every point of an edge product is a convex combination of its four corner pairs, so its value is at most the largest corner value. Consequently the supremum over \(u,v\in S_{X_H}\) is attained at an ordered pair of the six vertices.

Let
\[
\mathcal F=\{\pm s,\ \pm t,\ \pm(s-t)\}
\]
be the six signed facet functionals. For a fixed ordered vertex pair \((u,v)\), each norm square is the maximum of the squares of these functionals. Hence
\[
F_{a,b}(u,v)
=
\max_{f,g\in\mathcal F}
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\end{pmatrix}
Q_{u,v;f,g}(r)
\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix},
\]
where
\[
Q_{u,v;f,g}(r)=
\begin{pmatrix}
f(u)^2+r^2g(u)^2&r\bigl(f(u)f(v)-g(u)g(v)\bigr)\\
r\bigl(f(u)f(v)-g(u)g(v)\bigr)&g(v)^2+r^2f(v)^2
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Because every facet evaluation on every hexagon vertex belongs to \(\{-1,0,1\}\), there are only eighteen distinct coefficient types among these matrices.

Set
\[
E(r)=\frac{2+r^2+r\sqrt{r^2+4}}2.
\]
For every one of the eighteen matrix types, both diagonal entries are at most \(1+r^2\), while
\[
E(r)-(1+r^2)=\frac r2\bigl(\sqrt{r^2+4}-r\bigr)\ge0.
\]
The exact eighteen-case determinant check in the bundled verifier gives
\[
\det\bigl(E(r)I-Q_{u,v;f,g}(r)\bigr)\ge0
\qquad(0\le r\le1).
\]
More precisely, after using
\[
E(r)^2=(2+r^2)E(r)-1,
\]
every determinant is one of seven explicitly nonnegative forms:
\[
0,
\qquad E(r)^2,
\qquad \frac r2\bigl(r+\sqrt{r^2+4}\bigr),
\]
\[
\frac14\bigl(r^2-r\sqrt{r^2+4}-2\bigr)^2,
\qquad
\frac{r^2}{4}\bigl(r+\sqrt{r^2+4}\bigr)^2,
\]
\[
\frac{r^2}{4}\bigl(r-\sqrt{r^2+4}\bigr)^2,
\qquad
\frac r2(r-1)(r+1)\bigl(r-\sqrt{r^2+4}\bigr).
\]
The last expression is nonnegative on \([0,1]\) because both \(r-1\) and \(r-\sqrt{r^2+4}\) are nonpositive. Thus \(E(r)I-Q\) is positive semidefinite in every case, and
\[
F_{a,b}(u,v)\le E(r)(a^2+b^2)=E(r).
\]
Dividing by \(1+r^2\) yields
\[
L_{\mathrm{YJ}}(1,r,X_H)\le\frac{E(r)}{1+r^2}.
\]

For sharpness, take the unit vertices
\[
u=(1,1),\qquad v=(1,0)
\]
and let
\[
\tau=\frac{r+\sqrt{r^2+4}}2.
\]
Set \(x=u\) and \(y=\tau v\). Then
\[
N(x+r y)=1+r\tau,
\qquad
N(r x-y)=\tau.
\]
Since \((1,\tau)\) is a top eigenvector of
\[
\begin{pmatrix}1&r\\r&1+r^2\end{pmatrix}
\]
with eigenvalue \(E(r)\), the quotient at this pair equals \(E(r)/(1+r^2)\). This proves the exact profile.

It remains to optimize the parameter ratio. The inequality
\[
\frac{2+r^2+r\sqrt{r^2+4}}{2(1+r^2)}\le\frac43
\]
is equivalent to
\[
3r\sqrt{r^2+4}\le2+5r^2.
\]
Both sides are nonnegative, and after squaring the difference is
\[
(2+5r^2)^2-9r^2(r^2+4)=4(2r^2-1)^2\ge0.
\]
Equality holds exactly at \(r=1/\sqrt2\), giving the stated global maximum and its two symmetric parameter ratios.

## Verification
The bundled `verify.py` reconstructs the six vertices and six signed facet functionals, enumerates all \(36\) ordered vertex pairs and all \(36\) ordered facet pairs, and confirms that exactly eighteen coefficient-matrix types occur. It uses exact formal arithmetic in the quadratic extension generated by a symbol \(S\) satisfying \(S^2=r^2+4\) to verify that every determinant certificate is exactly one of the seven forms displayed in the proof. It also verifies the algebraic identity for the sharp eigenvector and the factorization controlling the global maximum.

The script is a finite certificate only for the polyhedral matrix step. The reduction from arbitrary unit vectors to vertex pairs and the passage from determinant certificates to a global bound are proved analytically above.

## Relationship to prior work
Liu and Li introduced \(L_{\mathrm{YJ}}(\lambda,\mu,X)\) from the Euler–Lagrange identity and established general bounds and geometric consequences. Their article treats a regular octagonal example only through an estimate; it does not state an exact affine-regular hexagonal profile.

Yang, Li and Yang later computed exact values of \(L_{\mathrm{YJ}}\) and its unit-sphere variant for the regular octagon. Their space has an octagonal unit ball and their result does not cover the six-vertex Day–James plane here.

Yang and Yang proved a general inequality relating \(L_{\mathrm{YJ}}\) to a generalized James constant. That inequality supplies comparison information but does not imply the present equality, because it does not identify the maximizing pair or collapse to the displayed hexagonal profile.

The balanced specialization \(\lambda=\mu\) is the classical von Neumann–Jordan constant, so the value \((3+\sqrt5)/4\) is not asserted here as a new isolated constant. The new claim is the complete two-parameter profile, including its strict off-balanced maximum \(4/3\) at parameter ratio \(\sqrt2\).

## Limitations
The theorem concerns one canonical two-dimensional polyhedral Banach space. It does not classify \(L_{\mathrm{YJ}}\) for arbitrary Day–James spaces, arbitrary hexagonal norms, or higher-dimensional polyhedral spaces. The literature and research-index searches cannot prove absolute novelty; the residual risk is a differently named equivalent formula outside the inspected sources. The finite checker does not replace the analytic convexity reduction.
