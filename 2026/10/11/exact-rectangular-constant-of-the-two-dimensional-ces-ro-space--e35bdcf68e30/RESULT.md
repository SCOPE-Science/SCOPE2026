# Exact rectangular constant of the two-dimensional Cesàro space
## Finding
Let \(X=\mathrm{ces}_2^{(2)}\) be the real plane with
\[
\|(x,y)\|_X=\left(x^2+\left(\frac{|x|+|y|}{2}\right)^2\right)^{1/2}.
\]
Let \(z_*\) be the unique positive root of
\[
25z^6+60\sqrt5\,z^5+260z^4+96\sqrt5\,z^3+45z^2-12\sqrt5\,z-10=0.
\]
Then \(1/3<z_*<1/\sqrt5\), and the rectangular constant is
\[
\mu(X)=
\frac{z_*+1/\sqrt5}{\sqrt{1+z_*^2+2z_*/\sqrt5}}
+
\frac{\sqrt{\frac8{25}(5z_*^2+4\sqrt5\,z_*+5)}}{1+z_*^2+2z_*/\sqrt5}
=
1.830537342565917\ldots .
\]
The maximizing Birkhoff--James configuration is unique up to the signed-coordinate and coordinate-swap isometries of the normalized plane and positive rescaling of the orthogonal vector.

With
\[
c=\frac1{\sqrt5},\quad A(z)=1+z^2+2cz,\quad
B(z)=\frac8{25}(5z^2+4\sqrt5\,z+5),
\]
a normalized extremal pair is
\[
x_*=\frac{(1,z_*)}{\sqrt{A(z_*)}},\qquad
y_*=\frac{(-(z_*+c),1+cz_*)}{\sqrt{A(z_*)}(z_*+c)}.
\]

## Assumptions and scope
All scalars are real. Birkhoff--James orthogonality is
\[
x\perp_B y\iff \|x\|\le\|x+\lambda y\|\quad(\lambda\in\mathbb R),
\]
and
\[
\mu(X)=\sup_{x\perp_B y,\ x,y\ne0}\frac{\|x\|+\|y\|}{\|x+y\|}.
\]
Only \(\mathrm{ces}_2^{(2)}\) is treated.

## Proof
A linear change and constant norm scaling reduce the Cesàro norm to
\[
M(s,t)^2=s^2+t^2+2c|st|,\qquad c=\frac1{\sqrt5}.
\]
By signed-coordinate and swap symmetry, every smooth unit point is represented by
\[
x_z=\frac{(1,z)}{\sqrt{A(z)}},\qquad0<z\le1.
\]
The unique Birkhoff tangent is generated by
\[
w_z=(-(z+c),1+cz),
\]
and
\[
M(w_z)^2=B(z)=\frac8{25}(5z^2+4\sqrt5\,z+5).
\]

For \(t\ge0\), direct comparison of the two tangent orientations gives
\[
M((1,z)-tw_z)\ge M((1,z)+tw_z).
\]
Indeed their Euclidean squared norms differ by \(4ct(1-z^2)\), their coordinate products differ by \(-2t(1-z^2)\), and
\[
\bigl||a|-|b|\bigr|\le|a-b|.
\]
Thus it suffices to use the plus orientation.

Before the first coordinate vanishes, namely for
\[
0\le t\le t_+(z)=\frac1{z+c},
\]
the active quadratic branch gives
\[
M((1,z)+tw_z)^2=A(z)\left(1+\frac45t^2\right).
\]
Hence the ratio is
\[
F_z(t)=\frac{1+t\sqrt{B(z)/A(z)}}{\sqrt{1+4t^2/5}}.
\]
Its unconstrained stationary value satisfies
\[
U(z)^2=\frac{5(3z^2+2\sqrt5\,z+3)}{5z^2+2\sqrt5\,z+5},
\]
and \(U\) is increasing on \([0,1]\). Feasibility of that stationary point is equivalent to
\[
5z^4+6\sqrt5\,z^3+12z^2+2\sqrt5\,z-1\le0.
\]
The left side is strictly increasing and is already positive at \(z=1/5\). Thus every feasible interior stationary value has \(z<1/5\). Moreover
\[
U(1/5)^2=\frac{241+13\sqrt5}{82}
<
\left(\frac{\sqrt2+\sqrt5}{2}\right)^2,
\]
so no such interior point is globally extremal.

At \(t=t_+(z)\), the first coordinate is zero and the ratio is
\[
R(z)=\frac{z+c}{\sqrt{A(z)}}+\frac{\sqrt{B(z)}}{A(z)}.
\]
After this crossing the opposite-sign quadratic branch is active. Its ratio derivative has affine numerator with negative slope; at the crossing that numerator equals
\[
-\frac{A(z)}{z+c}
\left[\sqrt{A(z)}(1+c^2+2cz)+(c-z)\sqrt{B(z)}\right],
\]
which is negative. For \(z\ge c\), positivity of the bracket follows from
\[
A(z)(1+c^2+2cz)^2-(z-c)^2B(z)
=
-\frac4{25}(5z^4-4\sqrt5\,z^3-30z^2-12\sqrt5\,z-7)>0.
\]
Therefore the ratio strictly decreases after the crossing, and the smooth-point problem reduces to maximizing \(R(z)\).

For \(z>0\), the equation \(R'(z)=0\) is equivalent to
\[
2B(z)A'(z)-B'(z)A(z)=\frac85\sqrt{A(z)B(z)}.
\]
Both sides are positive. Squaring and simplifying gives
\[
P(z)=25z^6+60\sqrt5\,z^5+260z^4+96\sqrt5\,z^3+45z^2-12\sqrt5\,z-10=0.
\]
The coefficients of \(P\) have exactly one sign change, so \(P\) has one positive root. Also
\[
P(1/3)=-\frac{16(9\sqrt5+80)}{729}<0,\qquad
P(1/\sqrt5)=\frac{96}{5}>0.
\]
Thus that root is \(z_*\in(1/3,1/\sqrt5)\), and the derivative changes from positive to negative there.

It remains to treat nonsmooth coordinate axes. At \(e=(1,0)\),
\[
e\perp_B(a,b)\iff |a|\le c|b|.
\]
After scaling take \(b=1\) and \(0\le r\le c\). The direction \((r,1)\) gives a squared ratio below \(2\). For the worse direction \((-r,1)\), put
\[
h^2=1+r^2+2cr,\quad g^2=1+r^2-2cr,\quad q=c-r.
\]
Before its coordinate crossing, the squared denominator is \(1+2qs+g^2s^2\). Enlarging to all real \(s\), the maximum squared ratio is
\[
\frac{g^2-2qh+h^2}{g^2-q^2}\le3,
\]
because \(g^2-q^2=4/5\),
\[
h^2-2g^2+3q^2=\frac25(5r^2-1)\le0,
\]
and \(-2qh\le0\). After the crossing the ratio decreases. Hence every axis point contributes at most \(\sqrt3<R(z_*)\).

Therefore \(\mu(X)=R(z_*)\), and numerical evaluation of the proved algebraic formula gives
\[
z_*=0.35016603386984658446\ldots,\qquad
\mu(X)=1.83053734256591701739\ldots .
\]

## Verification
The proof exhausts smooth and nonsmooth sphere points. Smooth points have one Birkhoff tangent and are reduced by symmetry to \(z\in(0,1]\); both tangent orientations and both sign chambers are treated. Coordinate axes are handled separately using their full Birkhoff cones.

The sextic is obtained from an exact derivative equation whose two sides are positive, so the relevant root is not introduced by an uncontrolled squaring. Descartes' rule and exact endpoint signs prove uniqueness. The decimal is only an evaluation of the exact formula.

## Relationship to prior work
Maligranda, Petrot, and Suantai study the same two-dimensional Cesàro plane and compute its James constant, not its rectangular constant.

Paul, Ghosh, and Sain study rectangular constants generally, prove finite-dimensional attainment, and give a reduction for polygonal planes. Their searchable full text contains no Cesàro example, and this Cesàro unit sphere is not polygonal.

Baronti, Casini, and Papini revisit rectangular constants and discuss generalized constants, \(\ell_p\) estimates, and hexagonal planes; the accessible article description contains no Cesàro evaluation.

Del Río and Benítez's 1977 paper is a highly relevant general two-dimensional source. Open searches located its bibliographic record but not inspectable full text, so possible coverage there remains a residual risk.

## Limitations
The theorem does not address other finite Cesàro exponents, infinite-dimensional Cesàro spaces, or the full rectangular modulus. The unavailable 1977 full text is a specific bibliographic residual risk.
