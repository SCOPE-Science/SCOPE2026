# Review of Quadratic Schwarzian shadow law for affine-balanced harmonic maps

## Correctness

PASS. Writing
\[
Q=P_H-P_F
=\frac{\overline\Omega\,\Omega'}{1-|\Omega|^2}
\]
gives the exact identity
\[
S_H-S_F
=
\frac{\overline\Omega\,\Omega''}{1-|\Omega|^2}
-P_FQ+\frac12Q^2.
\]
The source controls \(Q\) sharply. The new second-derivative term is controlled by a directly proved second-order Schwarz--Pick estimate. This yields the displayed finite bound with no missing derivative term.

The lower family
\[
F_k(z)=z+\frac{k}{3}\overline z^{\,3}
\]
is balanced and globally univalent, has pre-Schwarzian norm \(O(k^2)\), and its exact Schwarzian gap at \(|z|^2=1/3\) is
\[
\frac8{27}k^2+O(k^4).
\]
Thus the quadratic upper order cannot be improved. The endpoint \(M=0\) is separately rigid because \((P_F)_{\overline z}=0\) forces constant dilatation and balancing forces that constant to vanish.

## Originality

PASS. The fresh primary source explicitly poses the sharp Schwarzian-shadow problem and states that second derivatives of the dilatation enter, with no second-order conclusion asserted. Its proved theorem controls only the pre-Schwarzian gap.

The older harmonic-Schwarzian literature defines the invariant, studies univalence and valence criteria, and gives global norm results for the harmonic Schwarzian itself. It does not formulate the affine-balanced analytic-shadow gap or determine its small-dilatation exponent.

Targeted searches under the exact source, the stated open problem, Schwarzian analytic-shadow terminology, small-dilatation quadratic bounds, and second-derivative formulations found no implication-equivalent published statement.

Residual risk is limited to an unindexed very recent observation deriving the same quadratic-order answer from the new affine-balancing paper.

## Value

PASS. The result answers a mathematically explicit part of a fresh open problem: it determines the optimal exponent in the small-dilatation regime, rather than merely supplying an unspecified bound. The explicit lower witness proves that the second-order scale is intrinsic and cannot be improved to a higher order.

The theorem also isolates exactly where sharpness remains open: the leading coefficient lies between two explicit constants, giving a concrete target for further extremal analysis.
