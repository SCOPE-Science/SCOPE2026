# Square-completion decomposition of scale-two graph Čech nerves

## Finding

**Theorem.** Let \(G=(V,E)\) be any finite connected simple triangle-free graph with at least one edge. Suppose that any two distinct vertices have at most two common neighbors; equivalently, \(G\) has no (not necessarily induced) \(K_{2,3}\) subgraph. Let \(c_4(G)\) be the number of unoriented embedded four-cycles. Construct the square-completion CW complex \(Q(G)\) by attaching a separate closed two-disk along the boundary walk of every embedded four-cycle of \(G\). Then the intrinsic graph Čech complex at scale two satisfies

\[
\mathcal N(G,2)\simeq Q(G)\vee\bigvee^{c_4(G)}S^2.
\]

More precisely, \(\mathcal N(G,2)\) **elementarily simplicially collapses** onto a two-dimensional simplicial complex \(Y(G)\): the union of \(G\) and the tetrahedral boundary on the four vertices of every embedded four-cycle. The CW square complex \(Q(G)\) is a subcomplex of \(Y(G)\) after choosing one of the two diagonals in each square, and the homotopy splitting comes from attaching the second disk for each square along an already null-homotopic loop.

**A natural genus example.** For all integers \(m,n\ge5\), with \(C_m\mathbin{\square}C_n\) denoting the Cartesian product of the two cycles,

\[
\mathcal N(C_m\mathbin{\square}C_n,2)\simeq T^2\vee\bigvee^{mn}S^2,
\]

where \(T^2=S^1\times S^1\) is a torus. Thus integral homology in positive dimensions is \(H_1\cong\mathbb Z^2\), \(H_2\cong\mathbb Z^{mn+1}\), and zero above dimension two. The product of the two primitive degree-one torus cohomology classes is nonzero in integral degree-two cohomology. Therefore these examples are **not homotopy equivalent to wedges of spheres**, despite every added summand being a sphere.

## Assumptions and scope

All edges in the geometric realization of \(G\) have length one. The Čech nerve \(\mathcal N(G,2)\) has **one vertex for each graph vertex** and a simplex for every set of vertex-centered closed metric balls of radius one with nonempty intersection inside the realized metric graph. This is distinct from a nerve using centers at all metric-graph points. The even-scale neighborhood formula of Adams–Shukla–Singh, Lemma 2.2, identifies the nerve with the simplicial complex generated by all closed graph neighborhoods \(N[v]=\{v\}\cup N(v)\).

An embedded four-cycle is an unoriented cyclic sequence of four distinct vertices linked by four edges, modulo cyclic rotation and reversal. Triangle-freeness makes such a cycle chordless. The bound on common neighbors means that a diagonal pair in a four-cycle determines its opposite two vertices uniquely. The wedge count is a count of actual four-cycles, **not** all induced paths of length two or all pairs at distance two. The theorem does not require bipartiteness, regularity, planarity, or fixed degree.

## Proof

**Step 1: unique neighborhoods for large simplices.** For distinct vertices \(u,v\), their closed neighborhoods intersect in at most two vertices: if adjacent, triangle-freeness makes \(N[u]\cap N[v]=\{u,v\}\); if nonadjacent, their closed-neighborhood intersection is their common-neighbor set and has at most two vertices. Hence every simplex with three or more vertices lies in a *unique* generating closed-neighborhood simplex \(\Delta(N[v])\). This also covers all faces that appear during collapse.

For each vertex \(v\) and each subset \(F\subseteq N(v)\) having at least three elements, remove the pair \((F,F\cup\{v\})\), ordered by decreasing cardinality \(|F|\) (ties arbitrarily). The face \(F\) lies in just the simplex generated at \(v\), and its only coface remaining at the instant of removal is \(F\cup\{v\}\): every larger coface has been removed at an earlier cardinality. Each pair is therefore an **elementary simplicial collapse**. At the end, all simplices of dimension greater than two are gone. Remaining triangular faces are exactly \(\{v,a,b\}\) with \(a,b\in N(v)\), while the one-skeleton consists of the edges of \(G\) and the pairs of vertices having a common neighbor.

**Step 2: remove unique length-two chords.** Consider a nonedge \(\{a,b\}\) with exactly one common neighbor \(v\). The only surviving triangle containing \(\{a,b\}\) is \(\{a,v,b\}\). Remove this unique free edge and that triangle by one elementary simplicial collapse. Do this for every such unordered pair; the removals do not interfere because distinct pairs involve different free edges and triangles.

**Step 3: identify the residual complex.** Every remaining edge is either an original edge or a pair \(\{a,b\}\) with exactly two common neighbors \(u,v\). In the latter case the four vertices \(a,u,b,v\) form a unique embedded four-cycle. The four triangles \(\{a,b,u\}\), \(\{a,b,v\}\), \(\{u,v,a\}\), and \(\{u,v,b\}\) are exactly the four faces of a tetrahedral boundary on \(\{a,b,u,v\}\). Conversely, every residual triangle arises this way. Therefore the residual is exactly \(Y(G)\), the original graph augmented by the tetrahedral boundary of each four-cycle; no other higher-dimensional faces remain.

**Step 4: square disks and splitting.** On each four-cycle \(a,u,b,v,a\), choose one diagonal, say \(\{a,b\}\), and take the two triangles meeting along it. Their union is a simplicial disk with boundary the original four edges. Such chosen disks meet one another only in their boundary graph, because any pair of nonadjacent vertices has at most two common neighbors. They form a triangulation of the square-completion CW complex \(Q(G)\).

The other two triangles on that four-cycle form a second disk with exactly the same boundary, and they introduce their *other* diagonal. Its attaching map is null-homotopic in \(Q(G)\), because the boundary already bounds the first disk. Attach the remaining disks one by one; none uses edges in the interior of another disk. The elementary fact that attaching a two-cell along a null-homotopic loop to a connected CW complex adds one wedged two-sphere gives \(Y(G)\simeq Q(G)\vee\bigvee^{c_4(G)}S^2\). Together with Steps 1–3 this proves the theorem for every admissible finite connected graph.

**Torus deduction.** In \(C_m\mathbin{\square}C_n\) with \(m,n\ge5\), each four-cycle is the boundary of a unique elementary square. There are \(mn\) such squares, and there are no other four-cycles. Gluing these squares to the graph is the standard CW torus. The wedge splitting gives the asserted integral homology. The projection onto the torus summand induces the usual nonzero integral cup pairing on the two degree-one generators, proving the last assertion.

## Verification

The included executable `verify.py` implements the neighborhood definition, performs **each elementary collapse with an exact free-face test**, and checks the final residual against an independently generated union of tetrahedral boundaries. It independently computes reduced-modulo-two homology by exact binary Gaussian elimination. Run `python3 verify.py`; success is indicated by `VERIFY_OK`.

It checks seven named examples: the \(3\times4\) rectangular grid, one four-cycle, a \(C_5\mathbin{\square}P_3\) cylinder, a \(C_5\mathbin{\square}C_5\) toroidal grid, hypercubes of dimensions three and four, and the Petersen graph. It also exhaustively checks all **429 labeled graphs with at most five vertices** satisfying the local hypotheses (including disconnected test cases). These are finite sanity checks; the proof above, not finite extrapolation, establishes the arbitrary-graph theorem. Example reduced mod-two ranks for the \(5\times5\) toroidal graph are \((1,2,26)\) in degrees zero, one, and two.

## Relationship to prior work

Adams, Shukla and Singh established the vertex-centered intrinsic graph Čech construction and its closed-neighborhood formula for even scales. They also proved the scale-two sphere-wedge classification for **hypercube graphs and special induced subgraphs**. This theorem takes a different direction: arbitrary triangle-free graphs without a \(K_{2,3}\) subgraph, a canonical square-completion CW space, and examples carrying a nontrivial cup product. The already-known hypercube conclusion is a special case, not independently claimed here.

Corrine Previte's 2014 dissertation defined the identical \(D=\{0,1\}\) neighborhood object and calculated a single four-cycle as a tetrahedral sphere, a cube example with second-homology rank seven, and homology for one-point unions and unicyclic graphs. Those are acknowledged known partial cases; its one-point-union homology theorem does not imply a universal homotopy decomposition for graphs whose four-cycles overlap along edges, such as toroidal grids.

Matsushita examined closed-neighborhood complexes of general graphs and established fundamental-group correspondences, a suspension relation involving independence complexes, and particular Cartesian-product examples. The present decomposition determines the **full homotopy type** via elementary collapses, including the exact second-homology sphere summands; it is not a restatement of a fundamental-group theorem. Shukla's earlier neighborhood-complex results primarily concern the **open-neighborhood** construction, which must not be conflated with the present closed-neighborhood nerve. Related graph Vietoris–Rips complexes use a different simplicial criterion.

## Limitations

This theorem assumes both triangle-freeness and the two-common-neighbor bound. It makes no assertion about graphs with triangles or a \(K_{2,3}\) subgraph, about other Čech scales, about maps in persistence, or about equivariant naturality of the wedge decomposition. The decomposition of \(Y(G)\) is homotopy-theoretic; no assertion is made that the wedge splitting itself is a simplicial collapse. A bibliographically unindexed equivalent decomposition cannot be excluded. The result has received no independent mathematical audit.
