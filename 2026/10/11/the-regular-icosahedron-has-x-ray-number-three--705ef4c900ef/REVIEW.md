# Review

## Correctness
**PASS.** The proof uses the published Gauss-image characterization of X-raying and an exact coordinate model of the regular icosahedron. At the representative vertex \((\varphi,0,1)\), every incident facet normal has strictly positive first coordinate, so the first coordinate line X-rays that vertex. Signed-coordinate symmetry covers every vertex with one of the three axes, and Gauss-image inclusion from a face to any of its vertices promotes vertex coverage to all boundary points. The universal bound \(X(K)\ge3\) supplies minimality. The packaged exact-arithmetic verifier independently reconstructs all twenty facets and checks every vertex-axis sign condition.

## Originality
**PASS, with residual unindexed-literature risk.** The materially inspected Bezdek--Kiss paper proves \(X=d\) for almost smooth bodies and supplies the Gauss-image criterion, but its full text contains no icosahedron or Platonic-solid specialization. The regular icosahedron is demonstrably outside that theorem's hypothesis: two incident unit normals have inner product \(1/3<1/2\). Bezdek's earlier affine-subspace illumination theorem gives a broader but weaker three-dimensional illumination statement, not this exact X-ray value. Targeted searches of the exact object, invariant, aliases, and coordinate-axis formulation found no covering statement.

## Value
**PASS.** The regular icosahedron is a canonical Platonic solid, and the X-ray number is a classical convex-geometric invariant tied directly to the illumination problem. Showing that this nonsmooth body nevertheless attains the absolute dimension lower bound gives a clean benchmark outside the almost-smooth class, with an explicit optimal set of directions and a transparent normal-fan mechanism.
