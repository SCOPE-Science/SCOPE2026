# X-ray numbers of all five Platonic solids
## Finding
For the five regular Platonic solids in \(\mathbb R^3\),
\[
X(T)=4,\qquad
X(C)=4,\qquad
X(O)=3,\qquad
X(D)=3,\qquad
X(I)=3,
\]
where \(T,C,O,D,I\) denote the regular tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron, and icosahedron.

Explicit optimal line sets are available in each case. The tetrahedron is X-rayed by its four vertex-radius lines. The cube is X-rayed by its four body-diagonal lines. The regular octahedron and icosahedron are X-rayed by the three coordinate axes in the standard coordinate models below. The regular dodecahedron is X-rayed by the three lines
\[
\mathbb R(1,1,0),\qquad
\mathbb R(1,-1,0),\qquad
\mathbb R(0,0,1).
\]

## Assumptions and scope
For a convex body \(K\subset\mathbb R^3\), a boundary point \(p\) is X-rayed along a line \(L\) through the origin if the line parallel to \(L\) through \(p\) intersects \(\operatorname{int}K\). The X-ray number \(X(K)\) is the least number of line directions needed to X-ray every point of \(K\).

For a polytope, let \(N(v)\) be the cone generated by the outward normals of the facets incident with a vertex \(v\). The Gauss-image criterion says that the line \(\mathbb Rd\) X-rays \(v\) exactly when
\[
d^\perp\cap N(v)=\{0\}.
\]
Equivalently, all incident facet normals have strictly positive dot product with one of \(d,-d\). If a chosen line X-rays a vertex \(v\), then it also X-rays every proper face containing \(v\), because the Gauss image of such a face is contained in the Gauss image of \(v\). Thus it is enough to cover all vertices.

The coordinate models are used only to certify the line sets. Scaling and rigid motion do not change the X-ray number.

## Proof
### Tetrahedron
Take the four vertices
\[
v_1=(1,1,1),\quad
v_2=(1,-1,-1),\quad
v_3=(-1,1,-1),\quad
v_4=(-1,-1,1).
\]
They satisfy
\[
v_i\cdot v_i=3,
\qquad
v_i\cdot v_j=-1\quad(i\ne j).
\]
The three facets incident with \(v_i\) are the facets opposite \(v_j\), \(j\ne i\), and their outward normals may be taken as \(-v_j\). Hence
\[
v_i\cdot(-v_j)=1\quad(j\ne i),
\]
so the line \(\mathbb Rv_i\) X-rays \(v_i\). The four vertex-radius lines therefore give
\[
X(T)\le4.
\]

For the reverse inequality, the regular tetrahedron is weakly neighbourly because every two vertices form an edge. It is also antipodal. Indeed, for a pair \(v_i,v_j\), the linear functional with normal \(v_i-v_j\) takes value \(4\) at \(v_i\), value \(-4\) at \(v_j\), and value \(0\) at the other two vertices. Thus \(v_i\) and \(v_j\) lie on parallel supporting planes. Proposition 4.2 of Bezdek and Kiss states that a weakly neighbourly antipodal polytope with \(v\) vertices has X-ray number at least \(v\). Therefore
\[
X(T)\ge4,
\]
and \(X(T)=4\).

### Cube
Let
\[
C=[-1,1]^3.
\]
At the vertex
\[
s=(s_1,s_2,s_3),\qquad s_i\in\{-1,1\},
\]
the incident outward facet normals are
\[
s_1e_1,\qquad s_2e_2,\qquad s_3e_3.
\]
A line with direction \(d=(d_1,d_2,d_3)\) X-rays \(s\) exactly when
\[
s_1d_1,\quad s_2d_2,\quad s_3d_3
\]
are all strictly positive or all strictly negative. Consequently one line can X-ray at most one antipodal pair of cube vertices. Since the cube has four antipodal vertex pairs,
\[
X(C)\ge4.
\]
The four body-diagonal lines
\[
\mathbb R(1,1,1),\quad
\mathbb R(1,1,-1),\quad
\mathbb R(1,-1,1),\quad
\mathbb R(-1,1,1)
\]
cover the four antipodal pairs, so \(X(C)=4\).

### Octahedron
Take
\[
O=\operatorname{conv}\{\pm e_1,\pm e_2,\pm e_3\}.
\]
Its facet normals are the eight sign vectors
\[
(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,\varepsilon_3),
\qquad
\varepsilon_i\in\{-1,1\}.
\]
At \(+e_i\), every incident facet normal has \(i\)-th coordinate \(+1\); at \(-e_i\), every incident facet normal has \(i\)-th coordinate \(-1\). Hence \(\mathbb Re_i\) X-rays both vertices \(\pm e_i\). The three coordinate axes give \(X(O)\le3\). The universal lower bound in dimension three is \(X(K)\ge3\), hence
\[
X(O)=3.
\]

### Dodecahedron
Put
\[
\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}.
\]
Use the standard dodecahedral vertex set
\[
(\pm1,\pm1,\pm1),
\]
together with all independent sign choices in
\[
(0,\pm\varphi^{-1},\pm\varphi),\qquad
(\pm\varphi^{-1},\pm\varphi,0),\qquad
(\pm\varphi,0,\pm\varphi^{-1}).
\]
The twelve outward facet-normal directions can be taken as all independent sign choices in
\[
(0,\pm\varphi,\pm1),\qquad
(\pm\varphi,\pm1,0),\qquad
(\pm1,0,\pm\varphi).
\tag{1}
\]
Direct substitution shows that every normal in (1) has support value
\[
\varphi^2
\]
and exposes exactly five of the twenty vertices; every vertex lies on exactly three such pentagonal facets.

Now take
\[
d_1=(1,1,0),\qquad d_2=(1,-1,0),\qquad d_3=(0,0,1).
\]
For cube-type vertices \((\pm1,\pm1,\pm1)\), choose \(d_1\) when the first two signs agree and \(d_2\) when they differ. For vertices
\[
(0,\pm\varphi^{-1},\pm\varphi),
\]
choose \(d_3\). For vertices
\[
(\pm\varphi^{-1},\pm\varphi,0),
\]
choose \(d_1\) when the two nonzero signs agree and \(d_2\) otherwise. Finally, every vertex
\[
(\pm\varphi,0,\pm\varphi^{-1})
\]
is X-rayed by \(d_1\). In each case the three incident normals in (1) have strictly one sign after taking the dot product with the chosen direction. The bundled exact checker enumerates all twenty vertices and all twelve facet normals over \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)\) and verifies these strict signs without floating point.

Thus \(X(D)\le3\), and the universal lower bound gives
\[
X(D)=3.
\]

### Icosahedron
Use the standard vertex set consisting of all independent sign choices in
\[
(0,\pm1,\pm\varphi),\qquad
(\pm1,\pm\varphi,0),\qquad
(\pm\varphi,0,\pm1).
\]
Its twenty triangular facet-normal directions can be taken as the eight sign vectors
\[
(\pm1,\pm1,\pm1)
\]
and the twelve independent sign choices in
\[
(0,\pm\varphi,\pm\varphi^{-1}),\qquad
(\pm\varphi^{-1},0,\pm\varphi),\qquad
(\pm\varphi,\pm\varphi^{-1},0).
\tag{2}
\]
Every normal in (2) has support value \(\varphi^2\) and exposes exactly three vertices; each vertex lies on five triangular facets.

At a vertex whose coordinate of magnitude \(\varphi\) occurs in position \(i\), all five incident normals have nonzero \(i\)-th coordinates of one sign. Therefore the coordinate line \(\mathbb Re_i\) X-rays that vertex. The three coordinate axes cover all twelve vertices, so \(X(I)\le3\), while the universal lower bound gives
\[
X(I)=3.
\]

Combining the five cases proves the classification.

## Verification
The standalone `verify.py` uses exact arithmetic in \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)\), implemented from rational pairs rather than floating point.

It verifies the tetrahedral Gram relations, the four explicit vertex X-ray certificates, and the supporting-plane witness for antipodality of every vertex pair. For the cube it checks that the four body diagonals cover all eight vertices and that every nonzero coordinate sign cell can cover only one antipodal vertex pair.

For the dodecahedron it reconstructs twenty vertices, twelve pentagonal support facets, thirty edges, and uniform edge length from the displayed coordinate system, then checks that \(d_1,d_2,d_3\) cover every vertex normal cone. For the icosahedron it similarly reconstructs twelve vertices, twenty triangular facets, thirty edges, and uniform edge length, then checks the three coordinate-axis certificates. The octahedral check is exact directly from its eight sign-vector facet normals.

No finite experiment is used to infer a continuum statement. The only external mathematical inputs are the Gauss-image characterization of X-raying, the general three-dimensional lower bound \(X(K)\ge3\), and Proposition 4.2 for the tetrahedral lower bound.

## Relationship to prior work
Bezdek and Kiss give the Gauss-image characterization, the universal lower bound \(X(K)\ge d\), and the theorem \(X(M)=d\) for almost smooth convex bodies. Their full text also proves Proposition 4.2 for weakly neighbourly antipodal polytopes. The paper does not mention Platonic solids, tetrahedra, octahedra, dodecahedra, or icosahedra by name.

The regular Platonic solids are not covered automatically by the almost-smooth theorem: in dimension three that hypothesis requires normalized dot product at least \(1/2\) for every pair of outer normals at a boundary point, whereas the vertex normal cones of the Platonic solids contain pairs below this threshold.

Bezdek's earlier full paper on illumination by affine subspaces proves a different statement: every three-dimensional convex polyhedron with affine symmetry has its boundary illuminated by two external affine lines. That illumination invariant allows the illuminating point to vary along each external line and is not the X-ray number, which fixes line directions through every tested point. The earlier theorem therefore does not imply the classification above.

Targeted searches for the exact five-solid classification, the individual dodecahedral and octahedral X-ray numbers, and Platonic-solid X-ray terminology did not locate a published statement of this classification. The exact icosahedral value is a component already independently derivable from the same normal-fan criterion; the mathematical contribution assessed here is the complete five-solid classification, including the nontrivial \(4,4,3,3,3\) split and optimal line sets in every case.

## Limitations
The result is restricted to the five regular Platonic solids in three-dimensional Euclidean space. It does not classify Archimedean, Catalan, or general regular polytopes in higher dimensions.

The theorem concerns X-ray number, not the ordinary illumination number and not illumination by external affine line sources.

The literature search cannot rule out an unindexed classical observation under older terminology. The two closest general primary papers were inspected materially, including the complete 1991 affine-subspace illumination paper and the complete 2009 X-ray paper, and neither states this Platonic classification.
