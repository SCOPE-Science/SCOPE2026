# Arithmetic sharp cutoffs for reflected factors of generalized Thue–Morse words
## Finding
For integers \(b\ge2\) and \(m\ge1\), define the generalized Thue–Morse word
\[
T_{b,m}(n)=s_b(n)\bmod m\qquad(n\ge0),
\]
where \(s_b(n)\) is the sum of the base-\(b\) digits of \(n\). Put \(g=\gcd(b-1,m)\). Write \(\rho_{b,m}(h)\) for the number of distinct length-\(h\) factors and \(r_{b,m}(h)\) for the number of these factors up to reversal. A factor is **reversible** when its reversal also occurs.

There is an exact arithmetic trichotomy.

* **Moduli \(m\le2\):** the language is closed under reversal, so there is no finite reversible-factor cutoff.
* **Moduli \(m\ge3\) with \(g\in\{1,2\}\):** every reversible length-\(3\) factor is a palindrome, there are exactly \(2m\) such palindromes, and no reversible factor has length at least \(4\). The sharp cutoff is \(4\).
* **Moduli \(m\ge3\) with \(g\ge3\):** no reversible factor has length at least \(2\). The sharp cutoff is \(2\).

In particular, for every \(b\ge2\) and \(m\ge3\),
\[
r_{b,m}(h)=\rho_{b,m}(h)\qquad(h\ge3).
\]
The exact length-two reflection count, valid also for \(m=1,2\), is
\[
\rho_{b,m}(2)=\frac{m^2}{g},\qquad
r_{b,m}(2)=
\begin{cases}
\frac{m(m+1)}2,&g=1,\\
\frac{m^2}{4},&g=2,\\
\frac{m^2}{g},&g\ge3.
\end{cases}
\]
Consequently, when \(m\ge3\), the entire reflection complexity is determined by the already-known factor complexity together with this one length-two correction and \(r_{b,m}(0)=1\), \(r_{b,m}(1)=m\).

## Assumptions and scope
An occurrence of a finite word \(u\) is a contiguous block of the one-sided sequence \(T_{b,m}(0),T_{b,m}(1),\ldots\). Reversal is the ordinary order-reversing involution \(u_1\cdots u_h\mapsto u_h\cdots u_1\); it does **not** negate or permute letters. A factor is reversible when both \(u\) and \(u^R\) occur, regardless of whether they are equal.

The **sharp cutoff** is the least positive \(H\) such that no factor of any length \(h\ge H\) is reversible. In the length-\(4\) branch, a reversible palindrome of length \(3\) is explicitly constructed; in the length-\(2\) branch, every single letter is trivially reversible. The no-cutoff branch is proved by reversal closure.

The upper results hold uniformly over all bases and all moduli in the indicated ranges. The factor-complexity formula for generalized Thue–Morse words, and in particular its length-two count, was established previously; originality is asserted for the ordinary-reflection cutoff classification and its sharp arithmetic dependence on the parameters, not for the underlying digit-sum identity or factor-complexity theory.

## Proof
### 1. Carries, shifts, and the exact two-letter language
For every \(n\ge0\), let \(\nu_b(n+1)\) be the largest integer \(j\ge0\) for which \(b^j\mid n+1\). Incrementing an integer changes its digit sum by
\[
s_b(n+1)-s_b(n)=1-(b-1)\nu_b(n+1).
\]
Thus all adjacent differences belong to the coset
\[
D=1-(b-1)\mathbb Z_m\subseteq\mathbb Z_m.
\]
Every difference in \(D\) occurs: choose \(n=b^j-1\), with \(j\ge1\), or an integer \(n\) not congruent to \(-1\pmod b\) when \(j=0\).

Furthermore the language is invariant under every uniform alphabet translation. To see this, if a length-\(h\) factor occurs at \(n\), choose \(K\) with \(b^K>n+h\). Then for any integer \(t\ge0\), the corresponding factor beginning at \(t b^K+n\) is obtained by adding the residue \(s_b(t)\bmod m\) to every symbol. Every residue is achievable as \(s_b(t)\bmod m\), for example by taking a suitable number of separated base-\(b\) digits equal to \(1\). Hence
\[
F_{b,m}(2)=\{(a,a+d):a\in\mathbb Z_m,\ d\in D\}.
\]
Because the subgroup generated by \(b-1\) in \(\mathbb Z_m\) has order \(m/g\), this gives
\[
\rho_{b,m}(2)=m|D|=m^2/g.
\]

Reversal changes an adjacent difference from \(d\) to \(-d\). The entire two-letter language is reversible exactly when \(D=-D\). Since \(D=1-H\), where \(H=(b-1)\mathbb Z_m\), this is equivalent to \(2\in H\), or \(g\mid2\). If \(g\ge3\), there is not even one reversible factor of length \(2\).

If \(g=1\), the two-letter language is the full square \(\mathbb Z_m^2\), so reversal has \(m\) fixed pairs and \((m^2-m)/2\) two-element orbits, giving \(r_{b,m}(2)=m(m+1)/2\). If \(g=2\), all admissible adjacent differences are odd, so no two-letter factor is a palindrome. All \(m^2/2\) factors occur in reversal pairs, giving \(r_{b,m}(2)=m^2/4\). For \(g\ge3\), no two distinct factors are identified by reversal, giving \(r_{b,m}(2)=m^2/g\).

### 2. No reversible length-four factors when \(m\ge3\)
Write the difference triple of a length-four factor as \((d_1,d_2,d_3)\). The difference triple of its reversal is
\[
(-d_3,-d_2,-d_1)\pmod m.
\]

Suppose first that \(b\ge3\). Among three consecutive transitions, at most one crosses a multiple of \(b\), so every occurring difference triple contains at least two instances of \(+1\). If its reversal also occurred, the original triple would contain at least two instances of \(-1\). That is impossible in three positions, because \(+1\ne-1\pmod m\) for \(m\ge3\).

For \(b=2\), separate starts according to parity. At an even start \(n=2t\), the difference triple has the form
\[
(1,x,1),\qquad x=-\nu_2(t+1)\pmod m.
\]
At an odd start \(n=2t+1\), it has the form
\[
(x,1,y),\qquad x=-\nu_2(t+1),\quad y=-\nu_2(t+2)\pmod m,
\]
where at least one of \(x,y\) is \(0\). The reversed difference triple of the even form is \((-1,-x,-1)\): it cannot be an even form, which requires two outer \(+1\)'s, or an odd form, which has an outer \(0\). The reversal of an odd form has central difference \(-1\) and an outer \(0\), so it cannot be odd (center \(+1\)) or even (both outer \(+1\)). These comparisons use \(m\ge3\) and prove the claim in base two as well.

Any reflected factor of length \(h>4\) would contain a reflected length-four factor by taking its length-four prefix and the corresponding suffix of its reversal. Thus no reversible factor has length at least \(4\).

### 3. Reversible length-three factors are precisely palindromes
For \(b\ge3\), at least one of the two differences of every length-three factor is \(+1\). If its reverse also occurs, the original differences must contain \(-1\) as well. Because \(+1\ne-1\) modulo \(m\ge3\), the differences are \((+1,-1)\) or \((-1,+1)\), precisely the condition that the first and third letters agree.

For \(b=2\), a length-three factor at an even start has differences \((1,x)\), and one at an odd start has \((x,1)\). A reversed even-start factor cannot occur with odd-start parity, as its last difference would be \(-1\) rather than \(+1\); if it occurs with even-start parity, the condition forces \(x=-1\). The argument with parities interchanged applies to odd-start factors. Hence every reversible length-three factor is a palindrome for \(b=2\) too.

If \(g\ge3\), the length-two result already precludes such factors. When \(g\mid2\), choose an integer \(j\ge1\) satisfying
\[
j(b-1)\equiv2\pmod m.
\]
The three positions \(b^j-2,b^j-1,b^j\) then have digit sums
\[
j(b-1)-1,\quad j(b-1),\quad1,
\]
so their residues form a palindrome of type \((a,a+1,a)\). The three positions \(b^j-1,b^j,b^j+1\) likewise form a palindrome of type \((a,a-1,a)\). By alphabet-translation invariance from Part 1, both types occur for every \(a\in\mathbb Z_m\). For \(m\ge3\) these \(2m\) words are distinct, and the first paragraph proves that there are no other length-three palindromes. This establishes sharpness of the cutoff \(4\) and the exact palindrome count.

Because the only reversibly matched length-three factors are self-reversing and no length-four-or-longer factor is reversible, reversing factors makes no identifications at any length \(h\ge3\). Consequently \(r_{b,m}(h)=\rho_{b,m}(h)\) for all such \(h\).

### 4. Reversal closure for \(m\le2\)
For \(m=1\) the sequence is constant. For \(m=2\) and odd \(b\), the congruence \(s_b(n)\equiv n\pmod2\) makes the word alternating. Both languages are reversal-closed.

For \(m=2\) and even \(b\), let \(\tau(0)=(01)^{b/2}\) and \(\tau(1)=(10)^{b/2}\). The word is the fixed point of \(\tau\), and the exchange morphism \(E\) commuting with \(\tau\) satisfies \(\tau(c)^R=\tau(E(c))\) for both letters. Hence \(\tau^2\) commutes with reversal, and each \(\tau^{2j}(0)\) is a palindromic prefix. Every factor of the infinite word occurs in one of these prefixes, so its reversal occurs there too. The language is reversal-closed, completing the trichotomy.

## Relationship to prior work
Allouche, Campbell, Li, Shallit, and Stipulanti introduced reflection complexity and explicitly asked how their Thue–Morse results extend to generalized Thue–Morse sequences (Question 64 of the first version; Question 62 of the later version). They observe equality of reflection and factor complexity for a ternary-valued base-two example but do not state an arbitrary-base classification or a sharp arithmetic cutoff.

Allouche and Shallit previously proved that generalized digit-sum words have arbitrarily long ordinary palindromes if and only if the modulus is at most two; for larger moduli their proof excludes ordinary palindromes of length four or five and above. That theorem alone does **not** exclude a nonpalindrome occurring together with its reversal. The present local-pattern argument excludes both simultaneously for every base.

Starosta proved that the generalized Thue–Morse language is invariant under a full dihedral family of **letter-negating antimorphisms**, gave its exact two-letter language (Property II), and calculated factor complexity. Those antimorphisms are not ordinary reversal for moduli exceeding two. The present theorem credits that two-letter description, then uses ordinary reversal to distinguish the sharp cutoffs \(2\) and \(4\), and gives the resulting reflection count and reflected-palindrome stratum.

An earlier fixed-base-two calculation determines the equality \(r=\rho\) for modular binary digit sums at lengths at least three. The contribution here is the two-parameter sharp cutoff classification, including the gcd-controlled transition absent in base two.

## Limitations
No new formula is asserted for the factor complexity \(\rho_{b,m}\), which has already been determined in the literature. For \(m=2\), the theorem establishes reversal closure rather than rederiving every reflection-complexity value.

The result concerns ordinary reversal only; it is not a classification for all the generalized letter-negating antimorphisms present in the dihedral symmetry group of the generalized Thue–Morse language.

Although comprehensive targeted primary-source and semantic searches found no stronger ordinary-reversal theorem, an unindexed calculation could conceivably contain the same cutoff classification.
