# Endpoint potentials and the exact determinant of quantum average mixing on weighted paths

## Finding

**Theorem.** Fix an integer \(n\geq 2\), positive real edge couplings \(a_1,\ldots,a_{n-1}\), and a real endpoint potential \(\tau\). Let \(H_\tau\) be the symmetric tridiagonal \(n\)-by-\(n\) matrix with
\[
(H_\tau)_{11}=\tau,\qquad
(H_\tau)_{j,j+1}=(H_\tau)_{j+1,j}=a_j
\quad(1\leq j<n),
\]
and all other entries zero. Define the continuous-time average mixing matrix
\[
\widehat M_\tau=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac1T\int_0^T
 e^{itH_\tau}\circ e^{-itH_\tau}\,dt,
\]
where \(\circ\) denotes entrywise multiplication. Put \(m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor\), \(\chi_\tau(z)=\det(zI-H_\tau)\), and
\[
\Delta_\tau=\operatorname{Disc}_z(\chi_\tau)
   =\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)^2,
\qquad
L=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_j^{2(n-j)}.
\]
Define \(D_0\) by
\[
D_0=\begin{cases}
\prod_{j\,\text{odd}}a_j^2,&n\text{ even},\\
\prod_{j\,\text{even}}a_j^2,&n\text{ odd},
\end{cases}
\]
with products restricted to \(1\leq j<n\), and with empty products equal to \(1\). Then \(\Delta_\tau>0\) and
\[
\boxed{\det\widehat M_\tau=\frac{L}{D_0}\,
                \frac{\tau^{2m}}{\Delta_\tau}.}
\]
In particular,
\[
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}\widehat M_\tau=
\begin{cases}
n,&\tau\ne0,\\
\lceil n/2\rceil,&\tau=0.
\end{cases}}
\]
Thus a nonzero potential at only one end of **every** positively weighted path makes its infinite-time quantum average mixing positive definite. The zero-potential rank defect is restored for arbitrarily small nonzero \(\tau\), without restrictions on the couplings.

## Assumptions and scope

The walk is generated by the real symmetric Hamiltonian \(H_\tau\), not the combinatorial or normalized Laplacian. There is a potential only at the first endpoint; all remaining diagonal entries are zero. Edge couplings are arbitrary fixed positive real numbers. Changing their signs by a diagonal orthogonal gauge does not change the conclusion, provided none vanishes. The result is exact for all integers \(n\ge2\) and all real \(\tau\), including negative values and zero. The determinant and ranks refer to the **Cesàro** infinite-time average, not a finite-time transition matrix or state-transfer fidelity.

## Proof

**1. Simple spectrum and the mixing Gram factor.** Every irreducible real symmetric tridiagonal matrix has distinct real eigenvalues: the eigenvector recurrence shows that an eigenvector is determined by one nonzero endpoint coordinate, hence each eigenspace is one-dimensional. Write these eigenvalues as \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n\), and choose unit eigenvectors \(v^{(j)}\). The rank-one spectral projectors are \(E_j=v^{(j)}(v^{(j)})^{\mathsf T}\), so averaging the oscillating cross terms gives
\[
\widehat M_\tau=\sum_{j=1}^n E_j\circ E_j=B B^{\mathsf T},
\qquad B_{kj}=(v_k^{(j)})^2.
\]
In particular, \(\widehat M_\tau\) is positive semidefinite and has the same rank as \(B\).

**2. The squared-eigenvector determinant.** Normalize an eigenvector initially by assigning its final component the value \(1\). Backward substitution along the path gives components \(r_{n-k}(z)\), where \(r_0(z)=1\) and the polynomials \(r_s(z)\), for \(0\le s<n\), have parity \(s\) and leading coefficient
\[
\operatorname{lc}(r_s)=
   (a_{n-s}\cdots a_{n-1})^{-1},
\]
interpreted as \(1\) when \(s=0\). These properties follow by induction from the eigenvector recurrence at every vertex other than the first. The boundary condition at the first vertex is equivalent to \(\chi_\tau(z)=0\). The actual normalized eigenvector at \(\lambda_j\) therefore has components \(r_{n-k}(\lambda_j)/\sqrt{S_j}\), where
\[
S_j=\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}r_s(\lambda_j)^2>0.
\]
The polynomials \(r_s(z)^2\) are polynomials in \(z^2\), of respective degrees \(s\) and with nonzero top coefficient. Evaluating them at \(\lambda_j^2\) produces a Vandermonde determinant. Define
\[
K=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_j^{2j}.
\]
The product of squared leading coefficients is \(K^{-1}\), giving
\[
(\det B)^2=
\frac{\prod_{i<j}(\lambda_i^2-\lambda_j^2)^2}
     {K^2\prod_{j=1}^n S_j^2}.
\]
Furthermore, the Vandermonde evaluation immediately implies
\[
\operatorname{rank}B=\#\{\lambda_1^2,\ldots,\lambda_n^2\}.
\]

**3. Normalization by the characteristic discriminant.** Let \(p_{n-1}(z)\) be the characteristic polynomial of the leading principal submatrix on vertices \(1,\ldots,n-1\). The diagonal resolvent identity and spectral residues at each simple eigenvalue show
\[
\frac1{S_j}=
\frac{p_{n-1}(\lambda_j)}{\chi_\tau'(\lambda_j)}.
\]
For completeness, the leading-principal continuants \(p_k\) obey
\[
p_k(z)=(z-d_k)p_{k-1}(z)-a_{k-1}^2p_{k-2}(z),
\]
where \(d_1=\tau\) and \(d_k=0\) thereafter. Repeatedly applying this recurrence in the resultant \(\operatorname{Res}(p_n,p_{n-1})\), with \(p_0=1\), yields the exact product identity
\[
\prod_{j=1}^n p_{n-1}(\lambda_j)
 =(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}K.
\]
The characteristic polynomial is monic, hence
\[
\prod_{j=1}^n \chi_\tau'(\lambda_j)
 =(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}\Delta_\tau.
\]
Combining the two identities gives \(\prod_j S_j=\Delta_\tau/K\). Consequently
\[
\det\widehat M_\tau=(\det B)^2
=\frac{\prod_{i<j}(\lambda_i+\lambda_j)^2}
 {\Delta_\tau}.
\tag{1}
\]

**4. Endpoint perturbation and the opposite-root product.** Let \(P(z)=\chi_0(z)\) and let \(Q(z)\) be the characteristic polynomial of the trailing principal submatrix on vertices \(2,\ldots,n\). Expanding the determinant at the first vertex gives
\[
\chi_\tau(z)=P(z)-\tau Q(z).
\]
The zero-diagonal path is bipartite, so \(P\) has parity \(n\) and \(Q\) has parity \(n-1\). Since \(P\) and \(Q\) have no common zero (irreducible Jacobi interlacing), for \(\tau\ne0\) neither zero nor opposite pairs can cause denominator difficulties in the following product. For each root \(\lambda_i\) of \(\chi_\tau\), parity gives
\[
\chi_\tau(-\lambda_i)
=(-1)^n2\tau Q(\lambda_i)
=(-1)^n2\lambda_i\prod_{j\ne i}(\lambda_i+\lambda_j).
\]
Multiplying over \(i\) yields
\[
\prod_{i<j}(\lambda_i+\lambda_j)^2
=\tau^n\frac{\prod_i Q(\lambda_i)}{\prod_i\lambda_i}.
\tag{2}
\]
Apply the same continuant-resultant recurrence from the opposite endpoint to obtain
\[
\prod_iQ(\lambda_i)
=(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_j^{2(n-j)}
=(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}L.
\]
An even-sized zero-diagonal path has constant term \((-1)^m\prod_{j\,\mathrm{odd}}a_j^2\), whereas an odd-sized one has zero constant term. Expanding \(\chi_\tau(0)=P(0)-\tau Q(0)\) therefore gives
\[
\prod_i\lambda_i=
\begin{cases}
(-1)^m D_0,&n=2m,\\
(-1)^m\tau D_0,&n=2m+1.
\end{cases}
\]
The signs in (2) cancel, leaving the uniform exact identity
\[
\prod_{i<j}(\lambda_i+\lambda_j)^2
=\frac{L}{D_0}\tau^{2m}.
\tag{3}
\]
Equations (1) and (3) prove the determinant formula for \(\tau\ne0\); it also holds at \(\tau=0\), because both sides are continuous in \(\tau\) and \(\Delta_0>0\).

**5. Sharp rank classification.** For \(\tau\ne0\), (3) is strictly positive and the Gram factorization gives \(\widehat M_\tau\succ0\), proving full rank. For \(\tau=0\), the zero-diagonal path anticommutes with the bipartition-sign matrix, so its simple eigenvalues occur in pairs \(\lambda,-\lambda\), together with exactly one zero when \(n\) is odd. The squared eigenvalues consequently take exactly \(\lceil n/2\rceil\) distinct values. The Vandermonde rank identity in Step 2 proves the claimed rank at zero, completing the theorem.

## Verification

The accompanying `verify.py` implements an independent exact rational reconstruction, without computing numerical eigenvectors. For a simple-spectrum symmetric matrix, the powers \(I,H_\tau,\ldots,H_\tau^{n-1}\) span its commutant. Let \(V\) have column \(j\) equal to the diagonal of \(H_\tau^{j-1}\), and let \(G\) be the Gram matrix \(G_{ij}=\operatorname{tr}(H_\tau^{i+j-2})\). Then
\[
\widehat M_\tau=VG^{-1}V^{\mathsf T}.
\]
Using SymPy exact arithmetic, the script checks this identity's resulting matrix for determinant, rank, symmetry and row sums on 48 cases (orders \(2\) through \(7\), two families of integer couplings, and potentials \(0,1,-1,1/2\)), comprising 1,112 exact matrix entries. Run `python3 verify.py` with Python 3 and SymPy at least 1.12. The expected terminal line starts `VERIFY_OK`. This finite checker corroborates but does not establish the theorem for arbitrary real couplings and all orders.

## Relationship to prior work

Godsil [1], Sections 4–5, establishes the basic spectral-projector formula, computes the zero-potential **unweighted** path mixing matrix, and studies symmetric loops at **both** endpoints; these do not determine one-endpoint potential mixing for arbitrary weights. Coutinho, Godsil, Guo and Zhan [2] characterize average mixing using commutants and establish general rank bounds, including special zero-diagonal bipartite cases, but do not state the present universal rank transition or determinant. Coutinho and collaborators [3] provide exact computational methods for quantum average mixing; their generic finite-Hamiltonian methods do not alone provide the closed all-order discriminant formula here.

Kirkland and collaborators [4] study **weighted paths with potentials** by orthogonal-polynomial methods, but their main claims concern state-transfer fidelity and perfect state transfer, not the Cesàro average-mixing rank. Kirkland and van Bommel [5] study state transfer with **matching loops at both** endpoints, again a different quantity and perturbation. Eisenberg, Kempton and Lippner [6] use potentials to induce pretty good state transfer and do not give a corresponding average-mixing determinant. These are close in object or technique but do not state the present claim; the possibility of an implicit or broader result elsewhere is a residual originality risk.

## Limitations

The theorem assumes a path with nonzero edge couplings and **only one** nonzero allowable diagonal site, at an endpoint. Arbitrary on-site potentials at additional vertices, more general graphs, and finite-time mixing are not covered. The determinant can become very small near \(\tau=0\) even when full rank holds; the result does not assert a uniform spectral gap independent of \(\tau\). The exact-arithmetic checker only tests finitely many integer/rational instances. Novelty is assessed against the inspected literature, not certified by exhaustive bibliographic coverage; an unlocated stronger Jacobi-matrix theorem could imply the formula.
