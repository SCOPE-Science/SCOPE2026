# Mathematical review

## Correctness — PASS

The integral equations reproduce the two arrangements given in arXiv:2403.16870v1. Every line meets the common conic in two distinct rational points in characteristic zero: the conic parametrization reduces this assertion to twelve explicitly checked positive-square discriminants. Together with the fifteen rational line-pair intersections, this yields eleven distinct singular points in each arrangement with multiplicity counts \((6,3,2)\). Exact cross-product content calculations over all unordered pairs of those points and all pairs of line coefficients give respectively \(\{2,3,5,7,11\}\), \(\{2,3,5\}\) and \(\{2,3,7\}\), \(\{2,3\}\). These are global, finite arithmetic certificates for every prime \(p\ge7\), not empirical prime ranges. Recomputed finite-field profiles establish the two exceptional fibers; the gradient calculation proves the unique tangency. The reduced-support convention at \(p=7\) and branch-count convention at \(p=11\) are explicit.

## Originality — PASS (subject to stated bibliographic risks)

The original 2024 preprint establishes weak-combinatorial equality and different complex Jacobian syzygies; it does not classify arithmetic reduction of its integral equations. The later work DOI:10.1007/s10711-025-01063-w develops distinct weak/strong Ziegler examples but does not provide these two prime sets or the special-fiber incidence profiles. An earlier independently recorded result on this pair distinguishes its complex strong combinatorics; that conclusion does not imply the reduction prime sets, the line coalescences, or the tangency. Targeted literature and mathematical-record searches did not identify an equivalent classification. Unindexed computations or alternate presentations can still exist, and absence of a search hit alone is not evidence of novelty.

## Value — PASS

The original example is a distinguished, explicitly rational pair with the same weak combinatorics but different homological behavior. For arithmetic uses of these specific integral realizations, it matters exactly which characteristics preserve the arrangements' components and intersection geometry. The classification covers *every* prime at least seven and reveals two different failures—loss of line components at \(7\), and a nonordinary four-branch tangency at \(11\)—for one member, while the other is uniformly stable. This is a complete natural reduction invariant of the published models, rather than an arbitrary isolated finite computation. The claim does not infer syzygy behavior, and the exceptional set is not asserted to be invariant under changing integral coordinates.
