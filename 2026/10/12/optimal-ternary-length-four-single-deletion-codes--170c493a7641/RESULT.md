# Optimal ternary length-four single-deletion codes

## Finding
Let \(\Sigma=\{0,1,2\}\). For \(x\in\Sigma^4\), write \(D_1(x)\) for the set of distinct words in \(\Sigma^3\) obtained by deleting one coordinate of \(x\). A code \(C\subseteq\Sigma^4\) corrects one deletion exactly when the sets \(D_1(x)\), for \(x\in C\), are pairwise disjoint.

The exact maximum is
\[
\max \lvert C\rvert=11.
\]
There are exactly six labeled maximum codes. All six contain the same nine-word core
\[
K=\{(a,a,b,b):a,b\in\Sigma\}.
\]
Let
\[
E=\{(a,b,c,a):a,b,c\in\Sigma\text{ are pairwise distinct}\}.
\]
There are six words in \(E\). Put an edge between two of them when adjoining that pair to \(K\) gives a one-deletion-correcting code. The resulting graph is a six-cycle, and the six maximum codes are exactly \(K\) together with one edge of this cycle.

Under the natural action generated by arbitrary permutations of the three symbols and reversal of the four coordinates, a group of order \(12\), all six maximum codes lie in one orbit and each has stabilizer order \(2\). Every maximum has exactly three codewords with one distinct deletion descendant, six with two, and two with four. Hence each maximum covers exactly \(23\) of the \(27\) possible length-three received words and is not perfect.

## Assumptions and scope
The alphabet is the ordered set of three distinct symbols only for notation; the channel itself is invariant under relabeling. Deleting any one coordinate is allowed, repeated deletion outcomes from the same codeword are counted once, and decoding requires disjoint sets of distinct one-deletion descendants. The result is finite and exact for length four and alphabet size three. It does not assert a formula for other alphabet sizes or lengths.

## Proof
Form the compatibility graph \(G\) whose \(81\) vertices are the words of \(\Sigma^4\), with two vertices adjacent exactly when their deletion shadows are disjoint. Then one-deletion-correcting codes are exactly cliques of \(G\). Direct construction gives \(2370\) edges.

An exhaustive exact clique search finds six cliques of size \(11\), while a separate fixed-size clique enumeration finds six \(11\)-cliques and no \(12\)-clique. Since every larger clique would contain a \(12\)-clique, this proves the upper bound \(11\) and proves that the six enumerated codes exhaust all equality cases.

Intersecting the six equality cases gives exactly
\[
K=\{(a,a,b,b):a,b\in\Sigma\},
\]
which has nine words. Their union has fifteen words, so the six words outside \(K\) are exactly \(E\). Each equality case contains exactly two words of \(E\). The six selected pairs give a connected two-regular graph on six vertices, hence a six-cycle. This proves the stated structural parametrization.

Finally, applying all six symbol permutations and reversal or non-reversal to one maximum produces exactly the six maxima. Orbit-stabilizer therefore gives stabilizer size \(12/6=2\). Direct counting of distinct descendants inside every maximum yields the common shadow-size distribution \(3,6,2\) at shadow sizes \(1,2,4\), respectively, and therefore \(3+12+8=23\) covered received words.

## Verification
The bundled `verify.py` uses only the Python standard library. It constructs every deletion shadow in two independent ways: by deleting each coordinate and by choosing the three surviving coordinates. It then reconstructs all \(81\) vertices and \(2370\) compatibility edges.

For exhaustiveness it runs a Bron--Kerbosch maximal-clique enumeration and, independently, a fixed-cardinality increasing-vertex clique counter. The second calculation returns six cliques of size \(11\) and zero of size \(12\). The script then reconstructs the nine-word core, the six outside words, the six-cycle of admissible outside pairs, the complete declared symmetry orbit, and the common deletion-shadow profile. All assertions must pass before the script emits `VERIFY_OK`.

## Relationship to prior work
The length-four single-deletion problem has a sharp formula for every even alphabet size in work of Kim, Lee, and Oh; their result explicitly leaves the odd-alphabet side outside that formula. Thus it does not imply the ternary equality classification here. Kulkarni and Kiyavash formulate deletion codes as hypergraph matchings and give nonasymptotic bounds and integer-programming formulations, but their general framework does not supply this ternary length-four optimum or its equality cases.

Li and Houghten study ternary Tenengolts codes in detail. In their length-four table, the largest member of that structured family has eight codewords. Their full paper studies properties and extensions of that family rather than proving the unrestricted ternary length-four optimum. Perfect-code results for odd alphabets are also not implication-equivalent: the six maxima classified here cover only \(23\) of the \(27\) length-three outputs, so none is perfect.

Targeted searches using the exact parameters, the single-deletion-shadow formulation, equality-case language, and equivalent code terminology found no published statement of the six-code classification or the six-cycle structure.

## Limitations
The proof is computer-assisted, although finite and fully exhaustive, and the verifier is intentionally self-contained. A relevant 2011 M.Sc. thesis by Zhiyuan Li on ternary one-deletion-correcting codes could not be inspected in full; indexed excerpts and the later complete 2012 paper from the same research program were checked, but an obscure finite enumeration in that thesis remains a bounded priority risk. More generally, an unindexed historical computation could exist. No claim is made for other odd alphabet sizes.
