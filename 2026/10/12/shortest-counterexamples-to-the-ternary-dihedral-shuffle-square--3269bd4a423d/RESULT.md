# Exact length-eight census for ternary dihedral shuffle squares

## Finding

Among the 1,641 labeled even words of length eight on `012`, exactly 48 are not dihedral shuffle squares. They are precisely the cyclic shifts and alphabet relabelings of `00011022`. Under the smaller equivalence relation generated by alphabet relabeling and reversal, they form four classes of size twelve, with lexicographic representatives

`00011022`, `00110220`, `00111221`, and `01112210`.

Length eight is the first failing length. The contribution is the exhaustive classification at that length, not priority for finding a counterexample. The exact permitted-permutation table in the companion seven-permutation covering result [2] already entails the failure of `00011022` and, by rotation and relabeling, gives all 48 words as failures. The census proves there are no other failures at length eight.

## Assumptions and scope

A word is even if each letter occurs an even number of times. In a word of length \(2n\), a shuffle split partitions its positions into two sets of size \(n\); each set is read in increasing position order to give a subword. The word is a dihedral shuffle square if some split gives subwords in the same \(D_n\)-orbit, consisting of rotations and rotations of the reversal. The census labels the alphabet and takes reversal and relabeling, but not rotation, when reporting the four classes. It makes no claim for larger lengths.

## Proof

At each positive even length below eight, distribute half the occurrences of each letter to each subword. The subwords then have equal multisets. For subwords of lengths one, two, and three, rotations and reflections realize every position permutation, so such a split is dihedral.

For the witness `00011022`, every potentially successful split must give each half two zeros, one one, and one two. The half that contains the fourth zero (position 6) reads `0102`; its complement reads `0012`. In the latter the zeros are adjacent in the cycle of positions, and in the former they are not. No rotation or reflection can relate them. This direct check is also consistent with [2]: its complete inverse-class list for this word, `1324` and `2314`, has empty intersection with \(D_4\), including when inverses are taken.

Failure is invariant under alphabet relabeling and reversal. It is also invariant under rotation of the entire word: moving an initial segment to the end rotates each induced subword, possibly by different amounts. If a split after rotation succeeds, rotating back yields a successful split before rotation. In the witness the four occurrences of the most frequent letter have a unique isolated occurrence on the cycle, so a rotation fixing their position set must fix that position and be trivial. Consequently the eight rotations and six relabelings yield 48 distinct failing words. This lower bound follows already from the known witness.

For the converse, enumerate ordered eight-letter words on `012`, keeping only those with even counts for all three letters. For each retained word, iterate through the \(\binom84=70\) four-position subsets. Read each subset and its complement in index order; test whether the complementary subword is in the set of four rotations of the chosen subword and four rotations of its reversal. A word fails exactly if no subset passes this test. The exhaustive run returns 1,641 even words and 48 failures, so the rotation and relabeling orbit above is the full failure set. For each failing word, take the lexicographic minimum of its six relabelings and their reversals; grouping by these minima gives the four listed classes, each with twelve words.

## Verification

The accompanying `verify.py` and `certificate.json` check the full labeled failure list, the four classes and the witness. The verifier enumerates lengths 2, 4, 6, and 8, with even-word counts \(3,21,183,1641\) and failure counts \(0,0,0,48\). For `00011022` it checks all 70 subsets and finds 24 oriented balanced splits, all giving the same unordered pair `0012`/`0102`. This is a finite exhaustive calculation over the claimed domain, not evidence about larger lengths.

## Relationship to prior work

Grytczuk, Pawlik, and Pleszczyński formulated the two-subword dihedral conjecture in [1, §2.3, Conjecture 2]. Their ternary cyclic obstruction and their example for a whole-word symmetry variant are not obstructions to this two-subword property. The distinct covering result [2] determines the minimum number of arbitrary permutations needed to cover all even ternary length-eight words. Its lower-bound table certifies the same witness used here, which already refutes the conjecture, but the seven-permutation cover uses non-dihedral permutations and its reported tables do not establish exhaustiveness of the 48 dihedral failures. The separate framework in [3] concerns constraints and cutting distance rather than this census. The bibliography does not exclude an unindexed or differently formulated complete census.
