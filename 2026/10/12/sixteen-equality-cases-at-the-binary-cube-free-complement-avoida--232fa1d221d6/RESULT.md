# Sixteen equality cases at the binary cube-free complement-avoidance boundary
## Finding
Consider finite binary words satisfying both conditions:

1. the word is cube-free;
2. no factor of length at least \(6\) occurs together with its bitwise complement.

The published sharp maximum length is \(50\). Exactly \(16\) words attain that maximum. Under the group generated by reversal and bitwise complementation, they form exactly four orbits, each of size \(4\), represented by

## Assumptions and scope
A cube is a nonempty factor \(uuu\). For a binary word \(x\), its complement \(\overline{x}\) is obtained by interchanging \(0\) and \(1\).

The complement-avoidance condition can be checked solely at length \(6\). Indeed, if both \(x\) and \(\overline{x}\) occur for some \(|x|\ge6\), then the length-\(6\) prefixes of those two occurrences are complementary factors. Conversely, a complementary pair of length \(6\) is already forbidden.

This is the finite obstruction used for the first nontrivial cube-free entry in the complement-avoidance threshold theorem. The maximum length \(50\) itself is prior work; the new statement is the complete equality classification.

## Proof
Both constraints are factorial, so every prefix of an admissible word is admissible. Exhaustive binary prefix extension is therefore complete.

Suppose a valid prefix is extended by one bit. Any newly created cube must be a suffix, so every suffix of length \(3p\) is tested for the form \(uuu\). Any newly created complementary-factor violation must involve the new length-\(6\) suffix; its complement is searched among the factors already present in the extended word.

The exact survivor counts for lengths \(0\) through \(51\) are
\[
1,2,4,6,10,16,24,36,52,72,92,108,132,160,190,218,200,206,202,180,168,176,180,192,208,224,240,220,216,204,188,184,184,180,192,196,200,204,208,208,220,228,232,236,228,152,108,72,36,28,16,0.
\]
Thus there are exactly \(16\) survivors at length \(50\) and none at length \(51\). Factoriality excludes every longer word.

A second enumerator independently rescans each candidate for every cube factor and reconstructs the full set of length-\(6\) factors before checking complement pairs. It produces exactly the same survivor set at every length through \(51\). A direct final scan also checks the complement condition at every factor length from \(6\) through \(50\).

Finally, reversal and complementation preserve both defining constraints. Closing the \(16\) extremizers under these two operations yields four disjoint orbits, all of size \(4\).

## Relationship to prior work
Currie, Dvořáková, Ochem, Opočenská, Rampersad, and Shallit prove that the first nontrivial complement-avoidance threshold is attained by infinite \(3^+\)-free binary words with no complementary pair of length at least \(6\). For optimality they state that backtracking gives maximum finite length \(50\) under the stronger cube-free condition and print one example:
\[
\mathtt{00101001001101001001101001101001001101001101001001}.
\]

The paper states the scalar maximum and one witness, but it does not enumerate the equality layer or classify it under reversal and complementation. The companion code page exposes the morphism-search program used for the positive constructions, not a table of the \(16\) finite extremizers.

Searches using the source title, the exact printed witness, the phrases “cube-free complementary factors length 6,” “length 50 extremizers,” and three non-source orbit representatives located the primary article but no published equality classification.

## Limitations
This result concerns only the cube-free finite obstruction for the length-\(6\) complement-avoidance threshold. It does not classify equality cases for the paper's other exponent/complement thresholds or for the separate bounded-number-of-complemented-words problem.

The source authors used backtracking to establish the scalar maximum, so unpublished search output could contain the same \(16\)-word layer. No such published or indexed equality list was located.
