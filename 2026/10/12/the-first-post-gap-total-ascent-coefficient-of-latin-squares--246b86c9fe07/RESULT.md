# The first post-gap total-ascent coefficient of Latin squares

## Finding

Let \(\mathcal L_n\) be all labeled Latin squares of order \(n\), with their symbols ordered as \(0,1,\ldots,n-1\). For \(L\in\mathcal L_n\), define its total number of vertical ascents by
\[
\Sigma(L)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf 1\{L_{i,j}<L_{i+1,j}\},\qquad T_n(m)=\#\{L\in\mathcal L_n:\Sigma(L)=m\}.
\]
For **every integer \(n\ge3\)**, the first attainable coefficient after the isolated lower extreme is exactly
\[
\boxed{T_n(n+1)=T_n((n-1)^2-2)=n!\sum_{\substack{d\mid n\\3d\le n}}d.}
\]
The summation includes \(d=1\). In particular \(T_6(7)=2160\), \(T_8(9)=3\cdot8!\), and \(T_{12}(13)=10\cdot12!\). The formula determines every order rather than extrapolating the previously tabulated small orders.

Stronger classification: every square with \(\Sigma=n+1\) is uniquely determined by (i) its arbitrary first-row permutation of \(\mathbb Z_n\); (ii) a divisor \(d\mid n\) with \(3d\le n\); and (iii) a residue class \(r\bmod d\). The next paragraphs give an explicit bijective construction. Row reversal gives the opposite-boundary classification.

## Assumptions and scope

Rows and columns are labeled and distinct arrangements are counted separately. All comparisons \(<\) use the ordinary order \(0<1<\cdots<n-1\), while modular arithmetic is used only to describe the symbols. Every row and column is a permutation of \(\mathbb Z_n\). The theorem concerns only \(m=n+1\) and its row-reversal partner, not the other coefficients of the full Latin Eulerian polynomial.

For the construction, fix \(n\ge3\), \(d\mid n\), \(3d\le n\), a residue \(r\in\{0,\ldots,d-1\}\), and any permutation \((p_1,\ldots,p_n)\) of \(\mathbb Z_n\). Put \(b=n-2d\). For the column whose first symbol is \(p\), use the following three downward cyclic runs (each entry reduced modulo \(n\)):
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\text{first run (length }d\text{):}&p,p-1,\ldots,p-d+1,\\
\text{middle run (length }b\text{):}&q,q-1,\ldots,q-b+1,\\
\text{last run (length }d\text{):}&s,s-1,\ldots,s-d+1,
\end{array}
\]
where \((q,s)=(p-2d,p-d)\) if \(p\equiv r\pmod d\), and \((q,s)=(p-d,p+d)\) otherwise. The formula creates a Latin square with precisely \(n+1\) ascents. No two choices give the same square.

## Proof

**1. Adjacent rows and rigidity.** Let \(A_i\) count columns with \(L_{i,j}<L_{i+1,j}\). Since two consecutive rows are permutations of the same symbols, cannot agree anywhere, and have equal sums, \(1\le A_i\le n-1\). Furthermore \(A_i=1\) forces the lower row to be the upper row minus one modulo \(n\), coordinatewise. To prove this, write \(\Delta_j=L_{i,j}-L_{i+1,j}\) as ordinary integers. Exactly one \(\Delta_j\) is negative; the other \(n-1\) are positive integers, and their sum is the absolute value of the negative term, at most \(n-1\). Therefore all positive terms equal one and the negative term is \(-(n-1)\), which is exactly the claimed cyclic shift. This argument also shows a cyclic downward unit shift has exactly one ascent.

If all but one adjacent row pair are unit downward shifts, the rows above and below the exceptional boundary form two complementary cyclic arcs of symbols in every column. The second arc is forced to follow the first around the symbol circle, so the supposedly exceptional boundary is also a unit shift, a contradiction. The same argument applies when either arc has length one. Therefore if \(\Sigma(L)=n+1\), the identity \(\sum_i(A_i-1)=2\) forces precisely **two** exceptional boundaries, each with exactly two ascents.

**2. Three-arc decomposition.** The two boundaries divide the rows into three consecutive blocks of positive lengths \(a,b,c\) with \(a+b+c=n\). Within each block, successive rows are unit downward shifts. Fix a column, writing \(p\) for its first-row symbol and \(q,s\) for its first symbol in blocks two and three. The three symbol sets are disjoint contiguous arcs of lengths \(a,b,c\) partitioning \(\mathbb Z_n\). Once the first arc is fixed, the remaining arcs can occur in only two orders, giving
\[
(q,s)=(p-a,p-a-b)\quad\text{(type 0)},\qquad(q,s)=(p-a-c,p-a)\quad\text{(type 1)}.
\]
Let \(S\subseteq\mathbb Z_n\) consist of the first-row symbols \(p\) whose columns have type 1. The first row is a permutation; hence columns are indexed exactly once by \(p\).

**3. Permutation constraints on \(S\).** The first row of the middle block sends \(p\) to \(p-a\) off \(S\) and \(p-a-c\) on \(S\). A map that is identity outside a set and translation by \(-c\) on the set is bijective precisely when the set is invariant under that translation. Thus the middle block begins with a permutation exactly when \(S-c=S\). Analogously the last-block start is \(p+c\) off \(S\) and \(p-a=(p+c)+b\) on \(S\); it is a permutation exactly when \(S+b=S\). As the subgroup generated by \(b,c\) in \(\mathbb Z_n\) consists of the multiples of \(g=\gcd(n,b,c)=\gcd(a,b,c)\), the set \(S\) must be the union of \(t\) residue classes modulo \(g\), where \(0\le t\le g\). Conversely these conditions make the two starting rows, and thus all rows, permutations; the three-arc partition makes every column a permutation.

**4. Exact exceptional-boundary ascent counts.** At the first exceptional boundary, the upper symbol is \(p-a+1\). The lower symbol equals the upper minus \(1\) in type 0, and the upper minus \(c+1\) in type 1. If every column used a unit downward shift, exactly one column would ascend. Changing a type-1 column contributes one additional ascent precisely when its upper symbol is one of the \(c\) residues \(1,\ldots,c\). Because \(c\) is divisible by \(g\) and \(S\) occupies \(t\) classes modulo \(g\), exactly \(tc/g\) such symbols occur. The first exceptional count is therefore \(1+tc/g\).

At the second exceptional boundary, the upper row is a permutation, and its lower symbol is the upper minus \(1\) in type 0 or the upper minus \(a+1\) in type 1. In type 1 the upper symbol is \(p+1\), so the same residue-counting argument gives \(1+ta/g\) ascents. Thus **every** three-block Latin square under these conditions has
\[
\Sigma(L)=(n-1)+\frac{t(a+c)}g.
\]
The requirement that each exceptional boundary have exactly two ascents gives \(ta/g=tc/g=1\). Since \(t\) and \(a/g,c/g\) are positive integers, necessarily \(t=1\) and \(a=c=g\). The remaining block length \(b=n-2g\) must be a positive multiple of \(g\), so \(g\mid n\) and \(3g\le n\). Conversely each such divisor and each one of its \(g\) residue classes gives exactly the Latin-square construction above, with its two exceptional boundaries and no others.

**5. Uniqueness and enumeration.** The two exceptional row boundaries recover the block lengths and hence \(d=g\). The first row recovers its permutation. Inspecting the order of arcs in each column then recovers the unique chosen residue class \(S=r+d\mathbb Z_n\). Therefore there are exactly \(n!\sum_{d\mid n,\,3d\le n}d\) squares. Reversing the row order bijectively replaces every ascent with a descent, sending \(\Sigma\) to \(n(n-1)-\Sigma\), and gives the upper coefficient \((n-1)^2-2\). This completes the proof.

## Verification

`verify_latin.cpp` is an independent exhaustive search over all possible subsequent row permutations, with the first row normalized to \((0,1,\ldots,n-1)\) by column permutation. It enforces the Latin column constraint and prunes only once the excess over one ascent per row boundary exceeds two. It checks orders \(3\) through \(6\) and obtains normalized coefficients \(1,1,1,3\), agreeing with the theorem; the order-six full count is \(6!\cdot3=2160\). This is exhaustive for the targeted coefficient, not for all order-six Latin squares. Compile with `g++ -std=c++17 -O2 verify_latin.cpp -o verify_latin`, then execute `./verify_latin`, comparing standard output to `verification_output.txt` and standard error to `verification_examples.txt`.

`verify_construction.py` independently builds all divisor/class constructions with identity first row for orders \(3\) through \(50\). It checks each row and column, the exact ascent total, and pairwise distinctness at each order, reproducing `construction_output.txt`. Execute `python3 verify_construction.py`. These finite tests corroborate the complete symbolic argument; they do not establish it at untested orders.

## Relationship to prior work

Mirzavaziri and Yaqubi introduced the full Latin Eulerian statistic, proved the extreme support gaps, the one-ascent adjacent-row rigidity, and row-reversal symmetry, and tabulated the full distribution through order five in *Latin Eulerian Numbers* (arXiv:2609.25100v1, first posted September 19, 2026). Their companion *Cyclic Latin Eulerian Numbers* (arXiv:2609.28808v1, first posted September 23, 2026), Theorem 3.4, gives the analogous \(n\)-element count for the special family of row-reorderings of a fixed cyclic square; its computational Table in Section 8 also records full distributions through order six, including the previously known coefficient \(2160\). Neither restricted cyclic counting nor the finite table determines the **all-orders, unrestricted** divisor-sum formula and the three-arc classification above. The proof here extends the published rigid-row method by analyzing two exceptional boundaries and arbitrary Latin squares.

## Limitations

This theorem concerns two coefficients, not the complete distribution or any general unimodality conjecture. Its finite verification covers normalized orders through six and constructed examples through fifty. Originality comparisons cover two especially close preprints and related indexed literature; unindexed equivalent results cannot be completely excluded. No independent external mathematical audit has been performed.
