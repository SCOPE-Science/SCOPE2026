# A binary counterexample to the singular-substitution state-complexity product bound

## Finding

Let \(\Sigma=\{a,b\}\), and write \(\downarrow w\) for the finite language of all **scattered subsequences** of a word \(w\in\Sigma^*\), including the empty word. Define
\[
L=\downarrow(a^{7})\;\cup\;\downarrow(ba^{5}ba),
\qquad K=\downarrow(aababa).
\]
For the singular language substitution \(L_{a\leftarrow K}\), replace each occurrence of \(a\) in a word of \(L\) independently with any word of \(K\), retaining each \(b\) unchanged. Let \(\kappa(X)\) be the number of states of the minimal **complete** deterministic automaton for \(X\), including the dead state. Then
\[
\kappa(L)=15,\qquad \kappa(K)=8,\qquad
\boxed{\kappa(L_{a\leftarrow K})=127>120=\kappa(L)\kappa(K).}
\]
Thus the unrestricted bound in Conjecture 3.25 of Guyot (2024) is false even on a common binary alphabet with \(K\) generated by a single maximal word and \(L\) generated by two maximal words.

## Assumptions and scope

The downward arrow always refers to the scattered-subword (subsequence) order, not contiguous factors. Both languages are nonempty, finite, downward-closed subsets of \(\Sigma^*\). The substitution replaces occurrences of the distinguished source letter \(a\) by **languages**, not by a single chosen string. The target and source alphabets overlap: each is \(\{a,b\}\). All automata are complete over precisely this alphabet.

Set \(z=aababa\) and define the ordinary word morphism \(h\) by \(h(a)=z\), \(h(b)=b\). The exact substituted language is
\[
L_{a\leftarrow K}=\downarrow(z^{7})\;\cup\;\downarrow(bz^{5}bz).
\]
The two displayed maximal generators have lengths \(42\) and \(38\), respectively. They are incomparable in the scattered-subword order. The resulting bound failure is an **absolute difference of seven states**; it is not a claim about asymptotic growth of state complexity.

## Proof

**Substitution identity.** For any word \(w\), one has
\[
(\downarrow w)_{a\leftarrow\downarrow z}=\downarrow h(w).
\]
Every substituted output embeds into \(h(w)\): first embed its source word into \(w\), then embed each replacement into its corresponding copy of \(z\). Conversely, given any scattered subsequence of \(h(w)\), divide it among the copies of \(z\) and the retained one-letter \(b\) blocks. Delete from \(w\) exactly those \(b\)'s not selected, choose the appropriate subsequences of \(z\) for the \(a\) blocks, and obtain the selected output. Substitution distributes over finite unions, proving the displayed formula for \(L_{a\leftarrow K}\).

**Canonical quotients.** Every finite downward-closed language \(X\) has a unique antichain \(\mathrm{Max}(X)\) of maximal scattered subwords, and \(X=\bigcup_{w\in\mathrm{Max}(X)}\downarrow w\). If \(c\in\Sigma\), let \(s_c(w)\) be the suffix strictly after the **first** occurrence of \(c\) in \(w\). Then, interpreting a missing occurrence as the empty language,
\[
 c^{-1}(\downarrow w)=
 \begin{cases}
 \downarrow s_c(w),&\text{if }c\text{ appears in }w,\\
 \varnothing,&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\]
Indeed, a scattered embedding of \(cv\) into \(w\) can always use the earliest occurrence of \(c\); that choice leaves the longest possible suffix. For a finite antichain \(W\), the canonical antichain of \(c^{-1}(\bigcup_{w\in W}\downarrow w)\) is obtained by taking these first-occurrence suffixes and deleting all members contained as proper subsequences in other members. The empty antichain represents the empty quotient; the antichain consisting of the empty word represents the singleton language containing the empty word.

**Finite exhaustive certificate.** The file `certificate.json` lists every reachable canonical residual antichain and both letter transitions, starting from the initial generators, separately for \(L\), \(K\), and \(L_{a\leftarrow K}\). It contains respectively \(15\), \(8\), and \(127\) states. The standalone `check_certificate.py` checks that every antichain is canonical, every transition is exactly the mathematically derived first-occurrence quotient, every listed state is reachable, no transition leaves the list, and no two states have the same canonical antichain. By the uniqueness of the maximal-generator antichain, any two distinct listed states recognize distinct quotient languages. By the Myhill–Nerode theorem these are exactly the minimal complete DFA state counts. The comparison \(127>15\cdot8=120\) proves the claim.

This is an exhaustive verification of the **entire finite quotient automaton**, not a sample of input words. The correctness of the finite state count follows from the quotient identity and from checking every state and edge, not from a search timeout or an assumed upper bound.

## Verification

Using Python 3 without third-party packages, from the directory containing the embedded files:

```text
python3 check_certificate.py
python3 nfa_crosscheck.py
```

The first program validates the complete quotient-state certificate and prints:

```text
VERIFY_OK complete_reachable_quotients L=15 K=8 substitution=127 product=120 excess=7
```

The second program constructs both input finite languages independently, builds a substitution automaton with epsilon transitions, performs subset construction and Moore partition refinement, and prints:

```text
VERIFY_OK independent_epsilon_NFA=337 deterministic_subsets minimum=127 originals=15,8
```

The NFA calculation is an independent algorithmic cross-check rather than a substitute for the canonical-quotient proof. Both programs and the certificate are included in full.

## Relationship to prior work

Guyot, *On the State Complexity of Square Root and Substitution for Subword-Closed Languages*, arXiv:2407.10355v1, first submitted 14 July 2024, states the bound \(\kappa(L_{a\leftarrow K})\le\kappa(L)\kappa(K)\) for arbitrary downward-closed \(L,K\) as Conjecture 3.25. Theorem 3.22 proves it when the two alphabets are disjoint. Theorem 3.27 covers directed languages expressible as products of atoms; Proposition 3.7 treats the case where both inputs are principal downward closures. Our alphabets overlap, and the two generators of \(L\) are incomparable, so none of those positive statements contains this counterexample. The paper's Appendix B gives the standard substitution-NFA construction, independently reflected in `nfa_crosscheck.py`.

The theorem **refutes the exact product bound**. It does not refute the existence of a different quadratic upper bound with a constant factor, nor the special-case theorems with their additional hypotheses.

## Limitations

No minimality claim is made about the word lengths, the number of states, or the size of the excess among all counterexamples. Only two specific finite languages are considered; no unbounded or exponential family is constructed. The proof uses a complete finite, machine-checkable transition certificate plus elementary language identities. Independent external validation has not been performed. An equivalent counterexample appearing in unindexed or subsequently updated literature cannot be excluded solely by the recorded literature searches.
