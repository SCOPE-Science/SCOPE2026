# Scientific assessment: singular-substitution product-bound counterexample

## Correctness — PASS

The exact claim concerns finite binary downward-closed languages, with singular substitution defined by replacing each source \(a\) with an independently chosen member of \(K\). The equality between substitution of a principal subword ideal and the subword ideal generated by word substitution is proved explicitly. The first-occurrence suffix rule computes left quotients of finite subword ideals without approximation. Every canonical residual and transition in the complete certificate is independently checked; the states are reachable and pairwise inequivalent because canonical maximal antichains identify languages uniquely. Thus the counts \(15\), \(8\), and \(127\) are exact, not extrapolated from bounded word tests. An independent epsilon-NFA, subset-construction and Moore-minimization implementation corroborates the count \(127\). The proof does not depend on any timeout or unverified statistical inference.

## Originality — PASS, with residual priority risk

The directly relevant 2024 preprint of Guyot explicitly formulates the very inequality as Conjecture 3.25. The same source proves it only under different assumptions: disjoint alphabets in Theorem 3.22, product-of-atoms source languages in Theorem 3.27, and two principal ideals in Proposition 3.7. The present source language has two incomparable maximal words and both alphabets overlap, so the positive results do not imply the contested bound. A newer author-hosted version was located through an indexed excerpt, which still prints Conjecture 3.25, but its entire text was not accessible for direct comparison. Searches for equivalent counterexamples and updates found no covering result. Priority outside indexed literature remains uncertain; discovery failure alone is not a proof of novelty.

## Mathematical value — PASS

The result resolves a concrete published universal conjecture negatively with a small, exact, reproducible two-generator binary counterexample. It isolates precisely why the cited disjoint-alphabet and directed-source hypotheses cannot simply be dropped. The failure survives even with a principal substitute language. This is a substantive structural boundary rather than a routine enumeration or an arbitrarily selected numerical slice. It does not establish a larger asymptotic complexity class or refute all possible quadratic bounds.
