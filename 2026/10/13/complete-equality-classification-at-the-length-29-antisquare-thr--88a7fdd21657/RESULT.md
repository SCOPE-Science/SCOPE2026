# Complete equality classification at the length-\(29\) antisquare threshold obstruction
## Finding
Consider binary words with both of the following properties:

1. every nonempty factor has exponent strictly less than \(\frac{8}{3}\);
2. no factor is an antisquare \(x\overline{x}\) of order \(|x|\ge 4\), where \(\overline{x}\) is obtained by exchanging \(0\) and \(1\).

The published sharp upper bound is length \(29\). At that boundary there are exactly eight words. Under the group generated by reversal and binary complementation, they form exactly two orbits, represented by
\[
\mathtt{00100101001100101001100110100}
\]
and
\[
\mathtt{00100101001100101001100110110}.
\]
Thus the equality cases are completely classified by two symmetry types. The first representative is the example displayed in the source; the second differs from it only at the penultimate position.

The full set of eight extremizers is:
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\mathtt{00100101001100101001100110100},\\
&\mathtt{00100101001100101001100110110},\\
&\mathtt{00101100110010100110010100100},\\
&\mathtt{01101100110010100110010100100},\\
&\mathtt{10010011001101011001101011011},\\
&\mathtt{11010011001101011001101011011},\\
&\mathtt{11011010110011010110011001001},\\
&\mathtt{11011010110011010110011001011}.
\end{aligned}
\]

## Assumptions and scope
A finite word \(u\) has period \(p\) when \(u_i=u_{i+p}\) whenever both positions exist. Its exponent is \(|u|/p_{\min}\), where \(p_{\min}\) is the least period. The phrase \(\frac{8}{3}\)-free therefore means that every nonempty factor has exponent strictly below \(\frac{8}{3}\).

An antisquare is a word \(x\overline{x}\); its order is \(|x|\). The antisquare restriction here forbids orders \(4,5,\ldots\), while allowing smaller antisquares.

Reversal and binary complementation preserve both restrictions. The claim classifies all equality cases for the \((\ell,\beta)=(4,\frac{8}{3})\) finite obstruction appearing in the optimality argument of Baranwal, Currie, Mol, Ochem, Rampersad, and Shallit.

## Proof
Both restrictions are factorial: every factor, and in particular every prefix, of an admissible word is admissible. Hence exhaustive extension from the empty word is a complete search.

Suppose an admissible prefix \(w\) is extended by one bit. Any newly created forbidden factor must end at the new last position. For an antisquare, it is therefore enough to test every suffix of length \(2h\), \(h\ge4\), and reject when the first half is the binary complement of the second.

For the repetition restriction, for every suffix \(s\) of length \(L\) and every positive \(p<L\), test whether \(p\) is a period of \(s\). The suffix violates the condition exactly when
\[
\frac{L}{p}\ge\frac{8}{3},
\]
equivalently \(3L\ge8p\). Testing all \(p\) includes the least period, so this is exact and uses integer arithmetic only.

The exhaustive survivor counts for lengths \(0\) through \(30\) are
\[
1,2,4,6,10,16,24,36,42,52,60,72,86,96,94,90,92,90,92,96,102,110,102,102,78,60,36,32,24,8,0.
\]
Thus there are exactly eight admissible words of length \(29\) and none of length \(30\). Because the language is factorial, absence at length \(30\) also excludes every longer word.

Direct whole-word verification of the eight length-\(29\) survivors, independent of the incremental pruning argument, checks every factor for every possible antisquare split and every possible period. Closing the set under reversal and complementation gives two disjoint four-element orbits, exactly those recorded above.

## Relationship to prior work
Baranwal, Currie, Mol, Ochem, Rampersad, and Shallit determine repetition thresholds for binary words with antisquare restrictions. In the optimality argument for their first nontrivial antisquare-order threshold, their Table 3 states that a longest \(\frac{8}{3}\)-free binary word containing no antisquare of order at least \(4\) has length \(29\), and displays
\(\mathtt{00100101001100101001100110100}\)
as one example.

That statement proves the maximum length but does not enumerate the equality cases or identify their symmetry classes. The present result keeps the published maximum and strengthens the finite boundary description to a complete classification: eight equality cases in two reversal/complement orbits.

Searches for the exact second representative, for the displayed source representative together with the \(\frac{8}{3}\) condition, and for classifications of the length-\(29\) antisquare obstruction located the source but no published equality-case list.

## Limitations
This result classifies only the equality cases for the \((4,\frac{8}{3})\) row of the source's finite optimality table. It does not classify the equality cases in the other rows, nor does it strengthen the source's infinite-word threshold theorem.

The originality search cannot exclude an unpublished computation or an unindexed auxiliary data file retained by the source authors. The maximum length \(29\) itself is prior work; the new claim is the complete eight-word, two-orbit equality classification.
