# Scientific review

## Correctness — PASS

The divisibility \(k\mid n\) follows from the defining equation, not from numerical heuristics. The divisor injection proves the key inequality in the composite-parameter case. Its least-prime factor gives the sharp-in-form bound \(n\le r^2\). In the prime case the displayed exact prime-power identity separates \(a=1\), \(a\ge2\) with positive proper-divisor sum, and the prime-power branch. The latter gives the cubic bound and its attainment by infinitely many prime cubes. Small offsets are exhaustively checked only after the symbolic upper bounds make their ranges finite. The independent arithmetic regression matches the theorem within its declared range. The proof has no unproved primality claims and uses infinitude of ordinary primes only.

## Originality — PASS

The closest full-text source, Sándor (2022), considers the identical shifted divisor-sum equation and gives a qualitative fixed-offset finiteness-or-prime-family theorem (Theorem 5). It does not provide the exact criterion \(r-1\) prime, the all-support explicit sharp cubic cutoff, or the prime-cube extremal mechanism. Its Theorem 4 also omits the genuine \(r=3\) solution \((2,8)\). The sequel treats different arithmetic functions. A known affine divisor-sum classification of the \(\Omega(n)\le2\) layer covers the familiar semiprime phenomenon but cannot yield the all-support global size cutoff; the present claim acknowledges this overlap. Targeted semantic and literature searches did not yield a stronger applicable theorem. Unindexed literature remains a residual originality risk.

## Value — PASS

An explicit, best-possible polynomial cutoff turns a qualitative infinite-versus-finite dichotomy into a finite search domain for every fixed offset, without restricting prime support or multiplicity. The exact infinitude criterion is structurally motivated by the initial equation, and the cubic boundary is both universal (for fixed \(r\ge4\)) and attained at infinitely many offsets. The complete small-offset classification also identifies a missed boundary case, though that correction is not the primary value claim. The result does not pretend to solve the remaining finite instances below the cutoff.
