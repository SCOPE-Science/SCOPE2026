# Review

## Correctness
PASS. The proof first disposes of one-prime support, where the equation forces exponent \(2\). With at least two distinct primes, all exponents at most \(2\) give the strict inequality \(B(n)<\beta(n)^2\), so some exponent is at least \(3\). Under \(\Omega(n)\le5\), this leaves only the three-prime pattern \((3,1,1)\) and the two-prime patterns \((3,2)\), \((3,1)\), and \((4,1)\). The three-prime case is eliminated by reducing an exact factor equation modulo the cubed prime; the two-prime cases reduce to explicit contradictions. The witness \(144\) is checked exactly and proves sharpness.

## Originality
PASS. The primary literature defines the same equation, identifies the prime-square family, and proves global counting bounds, but the inspected full text does not state an \(\Omega\)-layer classification. A 2009 public source explicitly cites the then-forthcoming equation paper, establishing the problem's public presence before the 2010 volume. Targeted searches using the exact equation, \(\Omega\), prime-square language, \(144\), and low-support aliases found no same or stronger theorem. Database absence is treated only as a residual search fact, not as novelty proof.

## Value
PASS. The prime squares are the obvious infinite family in the source. Determining the exact first multiplicity at which any other solution can occur is a natural structural refinement of that problem. The result gives an unbounded exclusion through five total prime factors and a sharp transition at \(6\), witnessed by the small natural example \(144\); it is not a finite-table increment.
