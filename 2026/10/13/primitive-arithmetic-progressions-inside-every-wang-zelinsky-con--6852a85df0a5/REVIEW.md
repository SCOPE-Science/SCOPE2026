# Self-review

## Correctness
PASS. The proof reconstructs the divisor representation from the stated parameters. The congruence \(a+1\equiv0\pmod{b+1}\) forces \(\gcd(p,q)=1\); the displayed divisor box is therefore valid and distinct, and the omitted complementary-pair identity gives exact sum \(2N_{b,t}\). The Mersenne perfect-power lemma excludes nontrivial perfect powers, which is stronger than needed for primitivity. The fixed-row counting formula follows from the explicit exponential growth. Finite replay is corroborative only.

## Originality
PASS. Full-text inspection of Wang–Zelinsky's Theorem 9 and their later primitive definition found the ambient strong-pseudoperfect construction but not the fixed-row primitive progression or its logarithmic counting law. The closest public result, the full dyadic-\(60\) theorem, already gives a different infinite primitive family, so the present claim explicitly avoids asserting first global infinitude. The low-\(\Omega\) classification and OEIS A334405 do not imply the claim.

## Value
PASS. The result connects two central components of the recent paper—the two-parameter construction and the newly defined primitive subclass—uniformly in every fixed parameter row. It works even when \(p\) and \(q\) are composite and supplies a quantitative subfamily count. The \(b=1\) row is completely primitive.

## Closest literature and limitations
The direct source is Audrey Wang and Joshua Zelinsky, “Strongly Pseudoperfect Numbers,” arXiv:2609.36068v1. The closest public comparison is “All dyadic multiples \(60\cdot2^n\) are strongly pseudoperfect,” which concerns another family and already blocks any novelty claim based merely on primitive infinitude. The theorem here does not classify primitive strongly pseudoperfect numbers, prove maximality of its exponent progression, or settle odd primitive infinitude. Unindexed prior work remains a residual originality risk.
