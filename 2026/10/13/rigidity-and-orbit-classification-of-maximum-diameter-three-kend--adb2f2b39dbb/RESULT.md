# Rigidity and orbit classification of maximum diameter-three Kendall anticodes
## Finding
Let \(S_n\) be the permutations of \(\{1,\ldots,n\}\) written as one-line orderings and let \(d_K\) be the Kendall \(\tau\) distance (minimum number of adjacent-position swaps). Write \(B_1(\sigma)=\{\rho:d_K(\sigma,\rho)\le1\}\), and call a subset an anticode of diameter at most \(3\) if every two of its members have distance at most \(3\).

**Rigidity theorem.** For every integer \(n\ge5\), an anticode attains the known optimal size \(2(n-1)\) if and only if it equals
\[
A=B_1(\sigma)\cup B_1(\pi),\qquad d_K(\sigma,\pi)=1.
\]
Moreover, the unordered center pair \(\{\sigma,\pi\}\) is uniquely determined by \(A\). Thus the number of labeled maximum anticodes is exactly
\[
\frac{n!(n-1)}{2}.
\]

For the explicit order-\(2n!\) isometry group generated by common alphabet relabeling and optional reversal of every one-line word, two maximum anticodes are equivalent exactly when the adjacent swap positions of their unique center edges satisfy \(i=j\) or \(i=n-j\). Consequently, for odd \(n\) there are \((n-1)/2\) orbits, each of size \(n!\) and stabilizer order \(2\). For even \(n\) there are \(n/2\) orbits: one of size \(n!/2\) and stabilizer order \(4\), plus \((n-2)/2\) orbits of size \(n!\) and stabilizer order \(2\).

## Assumptions and scope
For each ordering \(p\), let
\[
I(p)=\{(u,v):1\le u<v\le n,\ v\text{ precedes }u\text{ in }p\}.
\]
Then \(d_K(p,q)=|I(p)\mathbin{\triangle}I(q)|\). Left relabeling of every symbol by a common bijection preserves this distance and can send any chosen permutation to the identity ordering. Reversing the coordinate positions is also an isometry.

Let \(N(p)=\{q:d_K(p,q)=1\}\) be the open distance-one neighborhood, of size \(m=n-1\). The parity of \(|I(p)|\) makes the adjacent-swap graph bipartite. All group actions in the statement are the explicitly described subgroup; no claim about a larger full isometry group is needed. The restriction \(n\ge5\) is essential to the proof and is not extended to \(n=4\).

## Proof
**1. Two-inversion structure.** Set \(m=n-1\). Write \(e_i=(i,i+1)\) for the adjacent-value inversion coordinate, \(1\le i\le m\), and \(h_i=(i,i+2)\), \(1\le i\le n-2\). Exactly the following sets can occur as \(I(p)\) when \(|I(p)|=2\):
\[
\{e_i,e_j\}\quad (|i-j|\ge2),\qquad
\{e_i,h_i\},\quad\{h_i,e_{i+1}\}.
\]
Indeed, every two-inversion permutation is obtained from the identity by two adjacent swaps: disjoint swaps give the first kind, and overlapping swaps in the two possible orders give the last two kinds. In the graph \(H\) whose vertices are inversion coordinates and whose edges are these two-element sets, every \(e_i\) has degree \(m-1\), every \(h_i\) has degree \(2\), and no triangle of \(H\) contains an \(h_i\). For \(n=5\), \(H\) has no triangles at all: three indices in \(\{1,2,3,4\}\) cannot be mutually at least two apart.

**2. Parity-class lemma and equality.** Suppose \(U\subseteq S_n\) has all pairwise Kendall distances at most \(2\) and all its members have the same inversion parity. Choose \(p_0\in U\) and relabel symbols to send \(p_0\) to the identity. Every other member has precisely two inversions. Its inversion set is an edge of \(H\). Because the distance between any two such members is at most \(2\), the corresponding distinct two-element sets pairwise intersect. Every family of distinct pairwise-intersecting two-element sets is either a star or the three edges of a triangle. A star gives at most \(m-1\) edges because the maximum degree in \(H\) is \(m-1\). A triangle yields at most three edges; for \(n\ge6\), this is strictly below \(m-1\), and for \(n=5\) no triangle exists. Thus \(|U|\le m\). Equality is possible only when the inversion sets form the entire star at some \(e_i\); together with the identity this is exactly \(N(s_i)\), where \(s_i\) is the ordering obtained from the identity by swapping positions \(i,i+1\). Undoing the relabeling gives:
\[
|U|=m\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad U=N(a)\text{ for some opposite-parity }a.
\]

**3. Uniqueness of the parity center.** Suppose \(N(a)=N(b)\), and normalize \(a\) to the identity. Every \(s_i\), \(1\le i\le m\), belongs to the common neighborhood. Therefore \(b\) is at distance \(1\) from every \(s_i\). Since \(b\) has even inversion parity, either \(|I(b)|=0\) or \(|I(b)|=2\). In the latter case \(d_K(b,s_i)=1\) forces \(e_i\in I(b)\) for every \(i\). That is impossible for \(m\ge4\). Hence \(b=a\): the center is unique.

**4. Extremal diameter-three structure.** For an arbitrary diameter-three anticode \(A\), split \(A=A_{\mathrm e}\sqcup A_{\mathrm o}\) by inversion parity. Each part has diameter at most \(2\), so Step 2 gives \(|A|\le2m\). If equality holds then both parity parts have cardinality \(m\) and therefore
\[
A_{\mathrm e}=N(a),\qquad A_{\mathrm o}=N(b)
\]
for uniquely determined opposite-parity centers \(a,b\).

Normalize \(a\) to the identity. The inversion number \(t=|I(b)|\) is odd. If \(t\ge5\), any immediate neighbor \(y\in N(b)\) has inversion number at least \(4\). If \(t=3\), the permutation \(b\) is not the full reverse ordering (whose inversion number is \(\binom n2\ge10\)), so some adjacent increasing pair can be swapped to produce \(y\in N(b)\) with inversion number \(4\). Either way there is \(y\in N(b)\) with \(r=|I(y)|\ge4\). Some adjacent-value inversion \(e_i\) is absent from \(I(y)\): otherwise all adjacent values would appear in decreasing order, forcing the complete reverse ordering and \(r=\binom n2\ge10\), in which case \(d_K(s_i,y)=r-1>3\). If \(e_i\notin I(y)\), then \(d_K(s_i,y)=r+1>3\). Both conclusions violate the assumed diameter because \(s_i\in N(a)\subseteq A\) and \(y\in N(b)\subseteq A\). Therefore \(t=1\) and \(d_K(a,b)=1\).

Conversely, when \(d_K(a,b)=1\), \(N(a)\) and \(N(b)\) lie in opposite parity classes, each has size \(m\), and every cross distance is at most \(1+1+1=3\). Moreover, \(a\in N(b)\) and \(b\in N(a)\), so \(N(a)\cup N(b)=B_1(a)\cup B_1(b)\) has exactly \(2m\) members. This proves both the sharp classification and the uniqueness of the two centers.

**5. Counting and orbits.** The adjacency graph of \(S_n\) is the permutohedron: every permutation has exactly \(m\) neighbors. It has \(n!m/2\) unordered edges. By Step 3 and Step 4, distinct edges give distinct maximal diameter-three anticodes, proving the labeled count. Common relabeling is transitive on edges obtained by swapping a fixed pair of adjacent positions \(i,i+1\); reversing the one-line word sends that swap position to \(n-i\). Thus the orbit types are precisely \(i\leftrightarrow n-i\). For each paired position type \(i\ne n-i\), the orbit has \(n!\) members and stabilizer \(2\) in the order-\(2n!\) group. When \(n\) is even, the central position \(i=n/2\) supplies one orbit of size \(n!/2\) and stabilizer \(4\). This gives the formulas stated in the Finding.

## Verification
The supplied standalone `verify.py` uses only the Python standard library. It independently reconstructs Kendall distance from breadth-first adjacent-swap paths and from inversion-set symmetric differences, checking the two full distance matrices for \(n=5\) and \(n=6\). A complete Bron--Kerbosch maximal-clique search yields exactly \(240\) optima of size \(8\) in \(S_5\) and exactly \(1800\) optima of size \(10\) in \(S_6\). Rebuilding every adjacent-center double ball independently produces precisely those optimum sets with unique center edges. Full label-relabeling/reversal group enumeration gives two orbits of size \(120\) at \(n=5\) and three orbits of sizes \(360,720,720\) at \(n=6\). A separate length-five-through-seven enumeration checks the complete list of two-inversion patterns and their degrees.

The replay ends with:

`VERIFY_OK n5_max=8 n5_count=240 n6_max=10 n6_count=1800 rank2_n5_to_n7=true`

The computations are **finite corroboration only**; the proof in Steps 1–5 supplies the unbounded \(n\)-quantifier.

## Relationship to prior work
Buzaglo and Etzion established that the largest Kendall diameter-three anticode has \(2(n-1)\) permutations and exhibited the double ball of two adjacent centers. Their 2014 conference Theorem 7 states the construction and optimum, and their later full-length Theorem 8 proves the upper bound using parity and the known diameter-two case. Their Theorem 6 (conference) and Theorem 7 (later treatment) classify unrestricted diameter-two anticodes as radius-one balls for \(n\ge5\).

Those sharp bounds and existence constructions are treated as **known**, not as new results. The added statement is a rigidity classification of **every** diameter-three equality case, uniqueness of its adjacent center edge, the exact labeled count, and the explicit isometry-orbit formulas for every \(n\ge5\). The inspected primary statements and proofs do not assert or imply those conclusions without the additional parity-class equality lemma proved here.

## Limitations
The theorem uses the ordinary, noncyclic Kendall \(\tau\) metric and covers diameter at most \(3\) for \(n\ge5\). It does not classify diameter-four anticodes, cyclic Kendall metrics, Ulam metrics, or the exceptional smaller symmetric groups. Only the explicitly generated label-relabeling/position-reversal isometry subgroup is used for the orbit statement.

A literature search cannot certify universal bibliographic uniqueness: an unindexed or obscure source might already establish equivalent equality rigidity.
