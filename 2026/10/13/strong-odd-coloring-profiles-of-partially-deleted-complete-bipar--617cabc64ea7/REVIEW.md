# Review: strong odd coloring profiles of partially deleted complete bipartite graphs

## Correctness
PASS. Because each side has an unmatched, universal-to-the-opposite vertex, every color class occurring on one bipartition side must have odd size. If such a class contained a matched endpoint together with any other vertex, it would have odd size at least three, but the paired excluded neighbor would see a positive even multiplicity, contradicting the strong odd condition. Thus all matched endpoints are singleton within their sides; unmatched vertices are freely partitioned into odd blocks. Properness allows the same color across the two sides exactly on a deleted matched pair, and any subset of those pairs can share colors. The reverse implication checks the odd neighborhood multiplicity at matched and unmatched vertices. Counting these disjoint structures gives the polynomial without overcounting. Minimum block counts yield the closed chromatic number and exact optimizer count. The single-edge increase for even crown graphs follows by direct comparison, with no unproved step. Independent definition-level finite checks match all 87,448 enumerated partitions in 16 representative parameter cases.

## Originality
PASS. The initiating 2024 paper gives the strong odd definition and sparse-graph bounds. The closest full-text primary comparison, Caro et al. (2024/2025), Theorem 4.4, solves strong odd *chromatic numbers* of complete-by-complete direct products, which includes the **full crown** graph but not the **partial matching deletion** graphs treated here. This theorem deliberately does not reclaim that known crown value; it derives the exact new value on irregular near-bicliques, their full coloring profile with odd-block Stirling numbers, and the unbounded single-edge jump. Its structural proof uses additional universal unmatched vertices absent at the crown endpoint. Targeted indexed-record and published-literature searches under partial matching, near-complete bipartite, all-coloring enumerator, and color-neighborhood parity formulations found no coverage. A residual risk from differently indexed literature remains.

## Value
PASS. A natural extremal operation—deleting part but not all of a perfect matching from a biclique—has a complete closed-form chromatic profile, not just a scalar evaluation. The proof exposes a previously unaccounted transition between permissible odd monochromatic blocks and forced singleton endpoints. For even sizes, adding a single missing matching edge to a crown graph increases the strong odd chromatic number from \(2\) to \(n+1\), an unbounded edge-insertion instability. This explains why endpoint direct-product values alone do not describe nearly identical dense bipartite graphs. The polynomial also counts all colorings for arbitrary palettes, and its coefficients enumerate every feasible color-class count.
