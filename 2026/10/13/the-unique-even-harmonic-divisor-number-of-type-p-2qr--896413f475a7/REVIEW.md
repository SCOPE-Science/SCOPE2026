# Review

## Correctness
PASS. The proof partitions the even case into the only two possible placements of the prime \(2\). In the \(4qr\) branch, exact bounds force the harmonic mean to be \(5\) or \(6\), and the resulting rational formulas for \(r\) leave only \(q=5,r=7\). In the \(2p^2r\) branch, exact bounds force harmonic mean \(5,6\), or \(7\); the \(5\)-case reduces to \(p\in\{3,5\}\), while the other two cases use the congruences \(p^2-3p-3\equiv1\pmod{p+1}\) and \(p^2-7p-7\equiv1\pmod{p+1}\) to force impossible divisibility by \(12\) or \(56\). Direct substitution gives \(H(140)=5\).

## Originality
PASS. The focal 2020 paper treats relationships between Zumkeller and harmonic mean numbers but not the exact even \(p^2qr\) layer. MathWorld records the classical two-prime-support classification and the example \(140\), while OEIS A001599 records the sequence; neither checked source states uniqueness of \(140\) in this three-prime exponent pattern. Exact-form, alias, implication, and broader-coverage searches did not locate a covering theorem. The principal residual risk is older or poorly indexed Ore-number literature.

## Value
PASS. The exponent pattern \(p^2qr\) is the first nonsquarefree multiplicity layer with three distinct prime divisors. Giving a complete, bound-free classification on its even stratum is a natural structural step beyond the classical two-prime-support theory, and it explains why the first non-perfect harmonic divisor number \(140\) is isolated in this boundary family rather than merely appearing in a table.
