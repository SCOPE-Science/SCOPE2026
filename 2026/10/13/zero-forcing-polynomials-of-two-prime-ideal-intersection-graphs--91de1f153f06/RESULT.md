# Zero forcing polynomials of two-prime ideal-intersection graphs

## Finding

Let \(p\ne q\) be primes and let \(\alpha,\beta\ge1\). For the intersection graph \(G_{\alpha,\beta}=G(\mathbb Z/(p^\alpha q^\beta))\) on the nonzero proper ideals, put \(N=\alpha\beta+\alpha+\beta-1\). If \((\alpha,\beta)=(1,1)\), then \(G_{1,1}=2K_1\), so \(\mathcal Z(G_{1,1};u)=u^2\), \(Z=M=2\), and \(\operatorname{mr}=0\). Otherwise \[G_{\alpha,\beta}\cong K_{\alpha\beta-1}\vee(K_\alpha\dot\cup K_\beta),\]and, with \(E=\alpha\beta+(\alpha+\beta)(\alpha\beta-1)\), \[\mathcal Z(G_{\alpha,\beta};u)=E u^{N-2}+N u^{N-1}+u^N,\qquad Z=M=N-2,\qquad \operatorname{mr}=2.\] The minimum zero forcing sets are exactly the complements of two vertices chosen from two different canonical ideal classes. Among all exponent pairs with \(\alpha\beta>1\), the zero forcing polynomial determines the graph up to isomorphism except for exactly two collisions: \(\{\alpha,\beta\}=\{1,9\}\) versus \(\{3,4\}\), and \(\{1,11\}\) versus \(\{2,7\}\).

Thus this natural algebraic family supplies exactly two pairs of nonisomorphic ideal-intersection graphs with the same zero forcing polynomial.

## Assumptions and scope

Let
\[
n=p^\alpha q^\beta,
\]
where \(p\ne q\) are primes and \(\alpha,\beta\ge1\). Write
\[
G_{\alpha,\beta}=G(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)
\]
for the intersection graph whose vertices are the nonzero proper ideals and whose distinct vertices are adjacent exactly when their intersection is nonzero.

Every ideal is generated by
\[
p^i q^j,
\qquad
0\le i\le\alpha,\quad 0\le j\le\beta.
\]
The pairs
\[
(0,0),\qquad(\alpha,\beta)
\]
correspond respectively to the whole ring and the zero ideal and are omitted from the graph.

For a graph \(G\), \(\mathcal Z(G;u)\) denotes its zero forcing polynomial, \(Z(G)\) its zero forcing number, \(M(G)\) its real symmetric maximum nullity, and \(\operatorname{mr}(G)\) its real symmetric minimum rank.

## Proof

For ideals
\[
I_{i,j}=(p^i q^j),
\qquad
I_{k,\ell}=(p^k q^\ell),
\]
their intersection is
\[
I_{i,j}\cap I_{k,\ell}
=
(p^{\max(i,k)}q^{\max(j,\ell)}).
\]
Therefore the intersection is zero exactly when
\[
\max(i,k)=\alpha
\quad\text{and}\quad
\max(j,\ell)=\beta.
\tag{1}
\]

Partition the vertices into three classes:
\[
A=\{I_{\alpha,j}:0\le j<\beta\},
\]
\[
B=\{I_{i,\beta}:0\le i<\alpha\},
\]
and
\[
C=\{I_{i,j}:0\le i<\alpha,\ 0\le j<\beta,\ (i,j)\ne(0,0)\}.
\]
Their sizes are
\[
|A|=\beta,\qquad |B|=\alpha,\qquad |C|=\alpha\beta-1.
\tag{2}
\]

By (1), every pair of distinct vertices is adjacent except an \(A\)-vertex paired with a \(B\)-vertex. Hence
\[
G_{\alpha,\beta}
\cong
K_{\alpha\beta-1}\vee(K_\alpha\dot\cup K_\beta).
\tag{3}
\]

If \(\alpha=\beta=1\), then \(C\) is empty and (3) is \(2K_1\). The only zero forcing set is the full vertex set, so
\[
\mathcal Z(G_{1,1};u)=u^2,
\qquad
Z=M=2,
\qquad
\operatorname{mr}=0.
\]

Assume now
\[
\alpha\beta>1.
\]
Then \(C\) is nonempty, so the graph is connected and noncomplete.

Each of \(A,B,C\) is a true-twin class: vertices in the same class have identical closed neighborhoods. A zero forcing set can therefore leave at most one white vertex in each class. If one vertex from each of \(A,B,C\) is left white, no blue vertex has a unique white neighbor: a blue \(A\)-vertex sees the white vertices in \(A\) and \(C\), a blue \(B\)-vertex sees the white vertices in \(B\) and \(C\), and a blue \(C\)-vertex sees all three. Thus
\[
Z(G_{\alpha,\beta})\ge N-2.
\tag{4}
\]

Conversely, leave two white vertices in two different classes. If they lie in \(A\) and \(C\), any blue \(B\)-vertex forces the white \(C\)-vertex, after which a blue universal \(C\)-vertex forces the white \(A\)-vertex. The \(B,C\) case is symmetric. If the white pair lies in \(A,B\), at least one of \(A,B\) has a blue member because \((\alpha,\beta)\ne(1,1)\); that blue vertex forces the white member of its own class, and then a \(C\)-vertex forces the other. Hence every cross-class white pair is forcing.

No same-class white pair is forcing because the two vertices are twins. Therefore the minimum zero forcing sets are exactly the complements of cross-class pairs, and
\[
Z(G_{\alpha,\beta})=N-2.
\]
Their number is
\[
E
=
|A||B|+|A||C|+|B||C|
=
\alpha\beta+(\alpha+\beta)(\alpha\beta-1).
\tag{5}
\]

Every set of size \(N-1\) is zero forcing because the graph has no isolated vertices, and the full vertex set is zero forcing. There are no smaller zero forcing sets by (4). Thus
\[
\mathcal Z(G_{\alpha,\beta};u)
=
Eu^{N-2}+Nu^{N-1}+u^N.
\tag{6}
\]

For minimum rank, assign to every vertex in \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\), respectively, the vectors
\[
(1,0),\qquad(0,1),\qquad(1,1)
\]
in \(\mathbb R^2\), and take their ordinary Gram matrix. Its off-diagonal entries vanish exactly between \(A\) and \(B\), so it has precisely the graph pattern in (3), and its rank is \(2\). Thus
\[
\operatorname{mr}(G_{\alpha,\beta})\le2.
\]
A rank-one real symmetric graph-pattern matrix can have a nonisolated support only on a complete graph. Since \(G_{\alpha,\beta}\) is connected and noncomplete,
\[
\operatorname{mr}(G_{\alpha,\beta})=2.
\]
Hence
\[
M(G_{\alpha,\beta})=N-2=Z(G_{\alpha,\beta}).
\]

It remains to determine when the polynomial recognizes the graph inside this two-prime family.

Put
\[
s=\alpha+\beta,
\qquad
t=\alpha\beta.
\]
Then
\[
N=t+s-1
\]
and
\[
E=t(s+1)-s
=
N+1+(N-1)s-s^2.
\tag{7}
\]
Suppose another exponent pair \((\gamma,\delta)\) with sum \(s'\) and product \(t'\) gives the same polynomial. Equality of degrees gives the same \(N\), and equality of the coefficient in degree \(N-2\), using (7), gives
\[
(s-s')(N-1-s-s')=0.
\tag{8}
\]

If \(s=s'\), then \(t=t'\), so \(\{\alpha,\beta\}=\{\gamma,\delta\}\).

Assume instead \(s\ne s'\). Then (8) yields
\[
s+s'=N-1.
\]
Using \(N=t+s-1=t'+s'-1\),
\[
s'=t-2,
\qquad
t'=s+2.
\tag{9}
\]

If all four exponents are at least \(2\), then
\[
(\gamma-2)(\delta-2)\ge0
\]
implies
\[
s+2=t'\ge2s'-4=2t-8,
\]
so
\[
2t-s\le10.
\]
If \(\alpha\ge3\) and \(\beta\ge\alpha\), then
\[
2\alpha\beta-\alpha-\beta\ge12,
\]
a contradiction. Hence \(\alpha=2\), and then
\[
3\beta-2\le10,
\]
so \(\beta\le4\); direct substitution gives no distinct pair.

Therefore one exponent in one of the two pairs equals \(1\). Write
\[
\alpha=1,\qquad \beta=r.
\]
Then (9) gives
\[
\gamma+\delta=r-2,
\qquad
\gamma\delta=r+3,
\]
hence
\[
(\gamma-1)(\delta-1)=6.
\]
The only unordered positive factor pairs of \(6\) give
\[
\{\gamma,\delta\}=\{3,4\}
\quad\text{or}\quad
\{2,7\},
\]
corresponding respectively to
\[
r=9
\quad\text{or}\quad
r=11.
\]
Thus the only distinct polynomial collisions are
\[
\{1,9\}\leftrightarrow\{3,4\},
\qquad
\{1,11\}\leftrightarrow\{2,7\}.
\]

The 2009 classification of intersection graphs of \(\mathbb Z_n\) shows that different unordered exponent factorizations give nonisomorphic graphs. Equivalently here, the complements have unique nontrivial components
\[
K_{\alpha,\beta},
\]
so the two collision pairs are visibly nonisomorphic.

## Verification

The accompanying `verify.py` constructs the ideal graph directly from exponent pairs and the lcm/intersection rule.

For all
\[
1\le\alpha\le\beta\le4,
\]
it checks the complete graph pattern, verifies the three canonical class sizes, and, whenever the graph has at most \(15\) vertices, exhaustively enumerates every vertex subset to reproduce the full zero forcing polynomial.

The checker builds the explicit rank-two Gram witness and verifies its off-diagonal pattern entry-by-entry using exact rational rank computation.

It also verifies the two polynomial collisions
\[
(1,9)\leftrightarrow(3,4),
\qquad
(1,11)\leftrightarrow(2,7),
\]
checks that the corresponding degree multisets differ, and exhaustively scans all unordered exponent pairs up to \(79\) to corroborate that no other collision occurs in that range.

## Relationship to prior work

Chakrabarty, Ghosh, Mukherjee, and Sen study the exact intersection graphs \(G(\mathbb Z_n)\), derive their vertex counts and degrees, and prove that the isomorphism type is determined by the unordered exponent factorization of \(n\). Their full paper does not study zero forcing or minimum rank.

Pucanović, Radovanović, and Erić study intersection graphs of ideals from the commutative-algebra side; their paper has primary Mathematics Subject Classification \(13A15\) and establishes ring-theoretic structural results for these graphs. Its full text contains no occurrence of “zero forcing” or “minimum rank.”

A broader minimum-rank result for finite principal ideal rings already determines the values \(Z=N-2\) and \(\operatorname{mr}=2\) in the two-local-factor nonreduced case. The new content here is the complete zero forcing polynomial, the exact classification of all minimum zero forcing sets, and especially the complete polynomial-recognizability theorem with exactly two nonisomorphic collision pairs.

Boyer and collaborators introduced the zero forcing polynomial and characterize its extremal coefficients. Their general theory is compatible with (6), but the inspected full text does not state this ideal-intersection family or its two exceptional polynomial collisions.

## Limitations

The theorem treats rings of the form
\[
\mathbb Z/(p^\alpha q^\beta)
\]
with exactly two distinct prime divisors.

The polynomial depends only on the exponent pair, not on the primes \(p,q\).

The minimum-rank statement is over real symmetric graph-pattern matrices.

The rank and zero forcing number alone are broader prior coverage for two-factor principal ideal rings; the strengthened contribution is the complete counting polynomial, all minimizers, and the exact recognizability/collision classification.

No claim is made here for three or more distinct prime factors, where the ideal-intersection graph has more than three canonical support types.
