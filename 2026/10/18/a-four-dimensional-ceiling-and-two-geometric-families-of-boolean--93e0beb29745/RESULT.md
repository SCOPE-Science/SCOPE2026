# A four-dimensional ceiling and two geometric families of Boolean scales in binary Hamming contexts

## Finding
For every integer \(d\ge2\), form the finite formal context \(K_d=(W,W,I)\) on the binary Hamming cube \(W=\{0,1\}^d\), with \(xIy\) if and only if the Hamming distance \(\operatorname{dist}(x,y)\le1\). A contranominal scale of dimension \(k\) is a pair of \(k\)-element object and attribute subsets that admit simultaneous orderings \((x_1,\ldots,x_k)\), \((y_1,\ldots,y_k)\) with \(x_iIy_j\) exactly when \(i\ne j\). Two orderings producing the same object set and attribute set are counted as the same subcontext.

**Theorem.** The maximum scale dimension is exactly \(4\), independently of \(d\). Every dimension-four scale is uniquely of one of the following two types:

* **Square type:** its four objects are exactly the four vertices of a coordinate \(2\)-face; its four attributes are the same four vertices, paired with objects by opposite corners.
* **Parity-tetrahedron type:** its four objects are one parity class of a coordinate \(3\)-face; its attributes are the other parity class of that face, paired with objects by Hamming distance \(3\).

In both cases, the attribute set and its pairing with the object set are uniquely determined. Thus the exact number of distinct maximal-dimensional contranominal subcontexts is
\[
N_d=2^{d-2}\binom d2+2^{d-2}\binom d3=2^{d-2}\binom{d+1}{3}.
\]
The two summands count square and parity-tetrahedron types, respectively. No dimension-five scale exists. In particular, the general contranominal-subcontext criterion for Boolean suborders of concept lattices bounds the dimension of any Boolean suborder in the concept lattice of \(K_d\) by four.

## Assumptions and scope
The alphabet is binary; all coordinates, objects, and attributes range over the full finite set \(W\). Incidence includes equality and Hamming-distance-one adjacency. The result counts **induced contranominal subcontexts** (both object and attribute sets); it does not count all Boolean suborders or Boolean sublattices of the full concept lattice. It concerns maximum-dimension scales, not a classification of lower-dimensional scales.

Use \(B(v)=\{w:\operatorname{dist}(v,w)\le1\}\), and write \(v\oplus e_i\) for flipping the \(i\)-th coordinate of \(v\). A coordinate \(j\)-face means a subcube obtained by freeing exactly \(j\) coordinates and fixing all others. Parity refers to the parity of the \(j\) free bits; the two parity classes have equal size for \(j=3\).

## Proof
**1. A universal upper bound.** For two distinct cube vertices \(u,v\), the intersection \(B(u)\cap B(v)\) has at most two elements. Indeed, for distance one, the intersection consists of \(u,v\); for distance two, it consists of the two intermediate vertices; for larger distance it is empty. In a contranominal scale with \(k\) objects and attributes, fix two distinct attributes \(y_i,y_j\). Each of the other \(k-2\) distinct objects lies in both radius-one balls. Hence \(k-2\le2\) and \(k\le4\).

**2. Geometric lemma for four objects.** In a four-scale, every pair of distinct objects is at Hamming distance at most two: an attribute associated to either of the *other* two objects is incident with both. We now classify any four distinct vertices \(S\) whose pairwise distances are at most two.

Translate a chosen member of \(S\) to the zero vector using coordinatewise binary addition. The other three nonzero vectors have Hamming weight one or two. If at least two of these have weight one, say \(e_a,e_b\), then the third is either a further unit vector (all four points lie in \(B(0)\)), or it is \(e_a\oplus e_b\) (the four points form a coordinate square). If exactly one has weight one, say \(e_a\), both weight-two vectors must contain coordinate \(a\), because their distance from \(e_a\) is at most two; hence all four vertices lie in \(B(e_a)\). If all three have weight two, their two-element supports intersect pairwise. Three pairwise-intersecting two-element sets either have a common coordinate or are the three pairs \(\{a,b\},\{a,c\},\{b,c\}\). In the first case all four points lie in \(B(e_a)\) for the common coordinate \(a\); in the second they are precisely one parity class of a coordinate three-face. Thus every such \(S\) either lies in a radius-one ball, is a coordinate square, or is a parity tetrahedron. These cases may be regarded as a covering classification at this stage; the scale condition makes them disjoint.

**3. No radius-one-ball configuration supports a scale.** Suppose \(S\subseteq B(z)\) consists of four distinct vertices. For any omitted \(x\in S\), the remaining three contain at least two leaves of \(B(z)\); they contain three leaves if \(z\notin S\) or if \(x=z\). The common intersection of the radius-one balls of three distinct leaves \(z\oplus e_a,z\oplus e_b,z\oplus e_c\) is \(\{z\}\). If \(z\in S\) and is omitted, that is immediately the only possible witness, but it is incident with itself. If \(z\in S\) and a leaf is omitted, the three remaining points consist of \(z\) and two distinct leaves; their common radius-one intersection is also \(\{z\}\). If \(z\notin S\), all four are leaves and the preceding three-leaf argument applies. In all cases no attribute can be incident with the other three while nonincident with \(x\), because \(z\in B(x)\). Thus a four-scale cannot have its objects inside a single radius-one ball.

**4. Existence and uniqueness for squares.** Let \(S=\{v,v\oplus e_a,v\oplus e_b,v\oplus e_a\oplus e_b\}\). For each \(x\in S\), the diagonally opposite corner \(y=x\oplus e_a\oplus e_b\) has distance two from \(x\) and distance at most one from each other member of \(S\). Thus these pairs give a four-scale. If any other attribute \(y'\) could witness the same omitted \(x\), then the intersection of the closed neighborhoods of the other three corners is the singleton consisting of the opposite corner: this follows directly by intersecting the two radius-one balls of the two neighbors of \(x\), whose intersection is \(\{x,x\oplus e_a\oplus e_b\}\), and then imposing adjacency to the opposite corner. Hence every witnessing attribute is unique. This argument also works at \(d=2\).

**5. Existence and uniqueness for parity tetrahedra.** Translate and permute coordinates to \(S=\{000,110,101,011\}\) in three varying coordinates, with all other coordinates fixed. The unique witnesses are \(111,001,010,100\), respectively: for each omitted object the corresponding attribute is its bitwise complement in the three-face, at distance three, and is at distance one from the other three. Uniqueness follows because the intersection of the radius-one balls centered at any three of these even-parity vertices is precisely their fourth, opposite-parity neighbor (respectively \(111,001,010,100\)); the two-center intersections each have exactly two points, and the third removes the other. Coordinate changes and translations preserve this property.

**6. Counting without duplication.** Each coordinate square is specified uniquely by its two varying coordinates and the fixed bits in the remaining \(d-2\) coordinates. The number is \(2^{d-2}\binom d2\). Each parity tetrahedron is specified uniquely by its three varying coordinates, the fixed \(d-3\) coordinates, and a parity choice, yielding \(2^{d-2}\binom d3\) (understand the latter as zero for \(d=2\)). The two types are distinguishable by their internal Hamming distances: squares contain distance-one pairs whereas parity tetrahedra have pairwise distance exactly two. Since witnesses are unique in both classes, every object set produces exactly one unordered pair of four-element object/attribute sets. This establishes the formula and completes the theorem.

## Verification
The included standard-library checker `verify.py` independently enumerates all four-point object sets of pairwise Hamming diameter at most two and computes all possible attributes for each omitted object. For dimensions \(2\) through \(8\), it checks witness uniqueness, the complete square/parity dichotomy, exact incidence, and both counting formulas. It reports respectively \(1,8,40,160,560,1792,5376\) maximal scales. The infinite upper bound, classification, and counts are proved above without relying on computational exhaustion.

## Relationship to prior work
Koyda and Stumme (2021) establish general relationships between Boolean suborders of formal-concept lattices and contranominal subcontexts, including an implication from Boolean suborders to contranominal scales. Dürrschnabel, Koyda, and Stumme (2021) give general algorithms for enumerating such scales and an induced-matching viewpoint. Their general machinery motivates studying exact extremal scales within a highly structured incidence relation. The present result supplies the binary Hamming-radius-one-specific constant maximum, geometric equality cases, unique witnesses, and closed count; the cited general results alone do not state this classification. An existing general-purpose enumeration algorithm can find the cases individually but does not by itself imply the formula.

## Limitations
We have not classified lower-dimensional scales, full Boolean suborders, or larger-radius/nonbinary Hamming contexts. Literature-wide originality and publication priority are not established by this mathematical proof. Contranominal enumeration is a well-studied area; an equivalent geometric description in induced-matchings, coding, or graph-neighborhood language may exist. Finite test cases do not certify the infinite statement; the argument above provides that proof.
