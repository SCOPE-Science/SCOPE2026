## Originality — PASS (subject to independent bibliographic verification)

The closest work is the *general* induced-subgraph component polynomial of Tittmann, Averbouch, and Makowsky: it captures the full random induced-component distribution and must not be presented as a newly discovered distributional formula. Its Proposition 2 concerns the ability of the full polynomial to distinguish tree isomorphism types through nine vertices, not the failure of equal-degree trees to be distinguished by the first two moments or the sharp transition at the third. The finite extremal variance law is likewise not stated there. Janson's later limiting covariance theorem is substantially related and establishes a degree-moment dependence asymptotically, but does not give the exact all-finite-tree path/star gap or six-vertex third-moment identity. Hackl–Panholzer–Wagner average over random labelled trees rather than optimize over fixed trees. Habitat graphs as ecological models are reviewed by Urban et al. Published index results about bond-component/triangle covariance and random-tree degree covariances do not imply this vertex-deletion theorem. Equivalent results hidden in derivative formulas or older reliability literature remain a real residual risk.

## Mathematical value — PASS

Conservation assessments frequently use counts of surviving connected habitat fragments. The exact variance extremizers quantify the structurally maximal and minimal uncertainty at *any* fixed survival fraction, while the first same-degree skewness separation shows a concrete limitation of relying only on degree-based risk summaries. The six-patch onset, unique degree-twin pair, all-parameter positive skewness separation, and complementary fixed-budget/Bernoulli protocols provide a motivated structural boundary rather than a computed arbitrary isolated case. No general claim about ecological impact, stochastic domination, heterogeneous losses, or non-tree habitat networks is made.
