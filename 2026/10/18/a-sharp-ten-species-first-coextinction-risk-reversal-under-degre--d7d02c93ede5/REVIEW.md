# Scientific review

**Correctness — PASS.** Every finite connected bipartite network through nine vertices is represented by a sorted tuple of nonempty plant-neighborhood bitmasks (and all guild splits are examined). Degree matching is checked separately in both guilds; every degree-class comparison of first-loss subset-count vectors is exact and coordinatewise comparable. The Bernstein mixture makes the resulting sign order valid for every independent pollinator-loss probability. The connected ten-vertex witness is checked by direct subset enumeration and separately by rational Bernoulli outcome enumeration; its two risk count vectors give the displayed polynomial and strict sign reversal. Repeating an existing singleton neighborhood leaves the first-loss event unchanged, proving the unrestricted extension. Exact finite experiments do not certify broader census claims, which are not made.

**Originality — PASS, with bounded bibliographic uncertainty.** The closest ecological literature analyzes species coextinction models, nestedness, mean richness-loss robustness, or stochastic cascades. A plant-degree formula for expected *counts* does not imply the tail risk of any plant coextinction; two networks here have identical mean counts but crossing first-loss probabilities. Searches for equivalent terms—first coextinction, any secondary extinction, hypergraph union-event reliability, random deletion and degree-preserving rewiring—found no covering theorem in the inspected sources. An earlier equivalent result in hypergraph reliability or ecological extinction modeling remains possible; this finding does not assert that every such source has been exhaustively searched.

**Mathematical value — PASS.** Expected secondary-extinction count is a standard but incomplete risk summary. The theorem pinpoints the smallest connected degree-matched ecological system where *event risk* changes order with the background threat intensity, separates expected counts from probability of the first secondary extinction, and gives an exact unbounded family rather than an isolated numerical example. The nine-vertex cutoff is a natural sharp boundary imposed by preserving both guilds' degree profiles, not an arbitrary parameter slice.

The closest full-text studies checked were Memmott, Waser & Price (2004), Burgos et al. (2007), Vieira & Almeida-Neto (2015), and the analytical 2023 treatment by Jones, Zurell & Wiesner. Residual risks include an unlocated equivalent hypergraph reliability statement and possible earlier treatment of first-extinction tails in the ecology literature. The verification applies only to the models and sizes explicitly stated in the finding. No external independent audit was performed.
