# Scientific review — path closure and epidemic duration

**Correctness — PASS.** With fixed endpoint source, synchronous one-generation transmission is coupled exactly to independent bond percolation: infection times equal shortest open-path distances. The path and cycle tail events are explicitly partitioned at \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor\), including the all-open-cycle exception. Their sums yield the displayed all-\(n\) polynomial. Dividing by the positive power \(p^{n-1}\) makes the crossover equation strictly monotone, proving uniqueness. The positive finite-size-scaling root follows by a convergent Riemann sum and a two-sided root bracket. The independent edge-subset program checked 32 exact identities at \(3\le n\le10\). Residual mathematical risk is limited to possible modeling distinctions from other epidemic conventions; the definitions are explicit.

**Originality — PASS, subject to bibliographic risk.** The closest examined primary accounts are Sharkey et al. (arXiv:1212.2172: exact *continuous-time* tree SIR state equations), Lashari et al. (arXiv:1805.05117: duration asymptotics on random configuration-model graphs), Shivam et al. (arXiv:2103.06427: finite network Reed–Frost contact tracing and scaling), and Alarcón González–Pérez (DOI:10.1101/2023.04.24.23289026: expected-duration computation without a fixed graph topology). None of their inspected theorem statements implies the present exact path-to-cycle crossover, the strict simultaneous mean-size increase, or the exact \(1/n\) crossover scale. An existing finite-graph result about the change in *outbreak-size variance* when a contact is added does not entail a comparison of epidemic duration. Searches for cycle/path duration, fixed-root infection generation, and percolation eccentricity did not identify an equivalent identity, but search absence is not proof of uniqueness. A possible older treatment under graph-theoretic eccentricity or chain-binomial language remains a substantive literature risk.

**Mathematical value — PASS.** A one-contact modification in an epidemiological graph produces opposed qualitative outcomes: expected total infections always increase, while expected duration lengthens at low transmissibility and shortens at high transmissibility. The exact crossover exists and is unique for every path size, and its large-network scale has a universal, nontrivial constant. This gives a structural, parameter-complete boundary for a natural network intervention rather than a single ad hoc numerical example. It concerns an idealized one-generation process, not observed epidemics or arbitrary graph edits.
