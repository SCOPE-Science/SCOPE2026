# Scientific assessment

## Correctness — PASS

The theorem is uniform over every finite hereditary family containing singletons, with real scalars and surjective linear isometries. Each inclusion-maximal facet supplies an open set where its associated quadratic form uniquely attains the squared norm; equality of maximum-of-quadratics norms forces equality of the constituent forms after a bijection. Their sum produces a positive diagonal incidence metric. Simultaneous diagonalization recovers twin classes by joint eigenvalue signatures, including their incidence multiplicities; this proves that each isometry is Euclidean orthogonal and a permutation plus block-orthogonal factors. The inverse/converse and group description are explicit in the proof. `check_examples.py` gives additional exact small-instance sanity checks but is not substituted for the theorem.

## Originality — PASS

Fakhoury, arXiv:2305.13125v1, studies the same quadratic construction, exhibits rotations for \(p=2\), and gives a criterion for *only* signed-permutation isometries. The complete group in the nonrigid finite real case, including between-family isometries and the connected symmetry dimension, is not stated in the inspected results. Brech, Ferenczi and Tcaciuc, arXiv:1907.06815, concern the original \(p=1\)-type combinatorial norm. Kalton and Wood (1976) discuss complex Hilbert components and are a substantial but differently scoped comparison. Public claim-level index results include finite-cube reachability and graph-generated combinatorial norms, but no directly dominating p=2 theorem. Remaining bibliographic risk: unpublished Brech–Tcaciuc material credited by Fakhoury may contain additional relevant results; absence from the consulted index is not a proof of global priority.

## Mathematical value — PASS

The quadratic exponent is the geometrically exceptional case in which the earlier general signed-permutation theorem fails. This result gives a complete computable classification of all finite real isometric types and their full symmetry groups from the maximal-set hypergraph; it identifies exactly when a continuous factor exists, its dimensions, and how discrete automorphisms act on it. The example of two overlapping three-point facets demonstrates why distinguishing common membership from identical facet incidence matters.

## Scope and scientific limitations

Finite dimensions; real scalars; onto linear isometries. No independent audit has been conducted. Finite computational checks are illustrative, not substitutes for the proof. The accessible general theory and unpublished manuscript pose residual priority risks as described above.

Self-review: passed. Independent audit: not yet performed.
