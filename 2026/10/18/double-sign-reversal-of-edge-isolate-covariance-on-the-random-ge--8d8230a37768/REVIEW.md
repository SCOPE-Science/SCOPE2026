# Self-review

## Correctness — PASS

The proof conditions on a specified isolated vertex; the complement of its forbidden neighborhood is an arc of length \(1-2r\), so the remaining vertices are independent uniform points there. The conditional pair-edge probability is the triangular-law expression used in the mixed-moment calculation. Subtracting the product of the marginal means gives an exact finite-\(n\) covariance; the quadratic and second branch give a complete sign classification, and Taylor expansions give the stated sparse limit. Rational arithmetic and separately sampled geometric graphs check relevant finite cases. Boundary conventions and all quantified parameter ranges are explicit. The proof, not the computation, covers infinitely many sizes and radii.

## Originality — PASS (with residual risk)

The inspected primary work of Dall–Christensen (2002), Decreusefond–Ferraz (2010), and Penrose (2015) addresses random geometric graphs, component counts, marginal degree and isolate statistics, or asymptotic Poisson approximations; it does not state the finite-sample cross-covariance's double sign reversal. A related monograph chapter on subgraph and component covariance was not fully accessible; it could contain a stronger implicit calculation. In contrast to limiting covariance constants, the present classification works for every fixed size, every admissible radius, and locates an exact minimum size of six for positive covariance. No claim of priority is made beyond the sources and comparisons documented.

## Mathematical value — PASS

The same pair of natural network observables changes dependence direction twice as interaction radius grows. This supplies a structural finite-size phase diagram rather than another estimate of mean edge or isolate counts, and gives a sharp sparse-degree threshold \(8/3\) that can inform interpretation of small spatial networks.

## Limits

No generalization to other geometries or random vertex counts is established. The unavailable monograph material remains an originality risk; numerical checks do not certify the general theorem.
