# Exact equality classes for odd Fourier uncertainty on the fifteen-cycle

## Finding

Let \(G=\mathbb Z/15\mathbb Z\), and use the unnormalized Fourier transform
\[\widehat f(y)=\sum_{x\in G} f(x)e^{2\pi ixy/15}.\]
For every nonzero complex function satisfying \(f(-x)=-f(x)\),
\[|\operatorname{supp} f|+|\operatorname{supp}\widehat f|\ge10.\]
The bound is attained in exactly **four projective classes**, represented by \(h_1,h_2,\widehat h_1,\widehat h_2\), where for \(t\in\{1,2\}\),
\[h_t(3k)=e^{2\pi itk/5}-e^{-2\pi itk/5}\quad(k\in\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z),\qquad h_t(x)=0\quad(x\notin3\mathbb Z/15\mathbb Z).\]
Here projective equivalence means multiplication by any nonzero complex scalar. These four classes are distinct; their time/Fourier support-size pairs are \((4,6)\) and \((6,4)\), respectively.

More precisely, for every odd function with exactly four nonzero values, its Fourier transform has at least ten nonzero values unless its support is \(\{3,6,9,12\}\). On that exceptional support, its Fourier transform has either six or twelve nonzero values; the six-value case consists exactly of the two lines generated by \(h_1\) and \(h_2\).

## Assumptions and scope

All supports are sets of positions on the fifteen-cycle, not supports of a sampled or normalized sine transform. Oddness forces the value at zero to vanish and forces every nonzero support to be a union of pairs \(\{u,-u\}\), \(1\le u\le7\). This result concerns one specific squarefree cyclic group and arbitrary complex coefficients. No theorem for all composite orders is claimed.

## Proof

Write \(\zeta=e^{2\pi i/15}\), \(c_u=f(u)\), and
\[S_{y,u}=\zeta^{yu}-\zeta^{-yu},\qquad1\le y,u\le7.\]
Then \(\widehat f(y)=\sum_{u=1}^7 S_{y,u}c_u\). Let \(a\) and \(b\) count the nonzero positive representatives in \(f\) and \(\widehat f\), respectively. Consequently the support-size sum is \(2(a+b)\). Fourier inversion preserves oddness and exchanges the roles of \(a,b\).

**One occupied time pair.** For a single active \(u\), \(S_{y,u}=0\) exactly when \(15\mid yu\), since \(15\) is odd. Among \(1\le y\le7\), there are at most two such zeros (two only when \(u=5\)). Thus \(a=1\) implies \(b\ge5\). Fourier duality gives the symmetric assertion for \(b=1\).

**Two occupied time pairs.** Fix \(1\le u<v\le7\). The vector \((c_u,c_v)\ne(0,0)\) annihilates a nonzero row \((S_{y,u},S_{y,v})\) only if the row lies on the unique one-dimensional complex annihilating direction of the coefficient vector. Any completely zero row is automatically annihilated. In particular, if \(z(u,v)\) is the number of completely zero rows and \(m(u,v)\) the largest number of nonzero rows with one projective direction, the number of positive zero Fourier frequencies cannot exceed \(z(u,v)+m(u,v)\).

This bound can be **certified by a finite-field calculation without reducing the unknown complex coefficients**. For \(p=31\) (or \(61\)), choose a primitive fifteenth root \(w\in\mathbb F_p\), and form \(\overline S_{y,u}=w^{yu}-w^{-yu}\). Because \(p\) does not divide \(15\), evaluation \(\zeta\mapsto w\) defines a homomorphism from \(\mathbb Z[\zeta]\) to \(\mathbb F_p\). Therefore every zero entry or vanishing two-by-two minor of \(S\) remains zero after reduction. Hence each complex zero-row set and each complex proportional-row class is contained in the corresponding modular class; counting modular classes produces **rigorous upper bounds** on complex Fourier zeros. No converse from modular zero to complex zero is asserted.

The accompanying exact checker visits all \(\binom72=21\) unordered pairs \(\{u,v\}\), forms their seven modular rows and groups them by projective direction. In each of the two fields its histogram of the modular upper bound is: eight pairs have bound one, twelve pairs have bound two, and the unique pair \(\{3,6\}\) has bound four. For this exceptional pair, the universally zero positive row is \(y=5\) and the remaining rows split into precisely two groups \(\{1,4,6\}\) and \(\{2,3,7\}\), each with one projective direction. Thus every two-pair function satisfies \(b\ge3\), with \(b=3\) possible only when its time support is \(\{\pm3,\pm6\}\) and its coefficient vector annihilates one of these two three-row groups. The group kernels are at most one-dimensional because their rows are nonzero. This finite enumeration is exhaustive and requires only elementary arithmetic modulo \(31\); the second prime is an independent reproducibility check.

**Exact attainment and uniqueness.** For \(t\in\{1,2\}\), writing \(\xi=e^{2\pi i/5}\), direct geometric summation gives
\[\widehat h_t(y)=\sum_{k=0}^4 (\xi^{tk}-\xi^{-tk})\xi^{ky}
=5\bigl(\mathbf1_{y\equiv-t\ (\mathrm{mod}\,5)}-\mathbf1_{y\equiv t\ (\mathrm{mod}\,5)}\bigr).\]
Each \(h_t\) is nonzero precisely on \(\{3,6,9,12\}\), while each \(\widehat h_t\) is supported at six positions. Their positive zero-frequency rows are, respectively, \(\{2,3,5,7\}\) and \(\{1,4,5,6\}\), realizing both exceptional one-dimensional kernels. Consequently these are all two-pair functions with \(b=3\). For every other nonzero function on the exceptional four-point support, exactly the common zero row \(y=5\) vanishes, so \(b=6\).

Finally, if \(a+b<5\), one of \(a,b\) is at most two. The one-pair case forces the other to be at least five, and the two-pair case forces the other to be at least three. Thus \(a+b\ge5\). If equality holds, one-pair possibilities are excluded; the only pairs of positive support counts are \((2,3)\) and \((3,2)\). The first gives precisely \(h_1,h_2\); applying the Fourier transform gives precisely \(\widehat h_1,\widehat h_2\) in the second case, because \(\widehat{\widehat f}(x)=15f(-x)\). The four classes are distinct by their support sizes and different positive-frequency supports. This proves the assertions.

It checks primitive-root orders, all 21 two-column projective-row configurations over \(\mathbb F_{31}\) and \(\mathbb F_{61}\), and all singleton zero rows. Expected output is included in `check_output.txt`. The geometric-sum formula above is the exact complex existence and nondegeneracy argument; modular experiments alone are not substituted for it.

## Relationship to prior work

Garcia, Karaali, and Katz develop the odd-function discrete sine-transform framework and show that its all-minors-nonvanishing property characterizes prime cyclic order. Their Theorem 1.3 does not determine the singular-minor pattern or equality classes at composite order \(15\). Bonami and Ghobber determine the unrestricted Meshulam support function on products of two prime cyclic groups and classify many equality cases. For \(G\cong\mathbb Z_3\times\mathbb Z_5\), their unrestricted minimum-frequency counts at time supports \(4\) and \(6\) are, respectively, \(5\) and \(3\). The present odd-class support pairs \((4,6)\) and \((6,4)\) are therefore not the unrestricted equality configurations classified there, and inversion oddness imposes an additional condition. The numerical support-sum inequality by itself is not asserted to be novel; the four-ray equality classification and special-support dichotomy are the distinct statements.

## Limitations

This is a finite, computer-assisted, exact certificate for the fifteen-cycle. Exhaustiveness is established by enumerating precisely 21 support pairs and proving that the modular upper bounds imply the corresponding complex exclusions; it does not depend on numerical floating-point thresholds. No claim of independent verification or of an all-modulus theorem is made. The principal bibliographic risk is a prior enumeration of the same fifteen-cycle sine-matrix proportional-row classes under different terminology; the primary sources inspected do not state it.
