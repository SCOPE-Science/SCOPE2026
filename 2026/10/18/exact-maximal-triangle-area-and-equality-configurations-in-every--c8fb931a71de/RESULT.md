# Exact maximal triangle area and equality configurations in every symmetric circular lens

## Finding
Let \(R>0\) and \(0<d<R\). Set
\[
L_{R,d}=\overline B((-d,0),R)\cap\overline B((d,0),R),
\qquad \theta=\arccos(d/R)\in(0,\pi/2).
\]
Among all Euclidean triangles \(T\subseteq L_{R,d}\), including triangles with arbitrary interior vertices, the exact sharp maximum is
\[
\boxed{\max_{T\subseteq L_{R,d}}\operatorname{area}(T)
=2R^2\sin^3(2\theta/3).}
\]
Moreover, precisely two geometric triangles attain equality. Define
\[
H=R\sin\theta,\quad a=R\sin(\theta/3),\quad
r=R\bigl(\cos(\theta/3)-\cos\theta\bigr)>0.
\]
For \(\sigma\in\{+1,-1\}\), they are
\[
T_\sigma=\operatorname{conv}\{(0,\sigma H),\ (-r,-\sigma a),\ (r,-\sigma a)\}.
\]
The two triangles are related by central inversion. There are no other maximizers, even allowing triangle vertices in the interior of the lens. The endpoints \(d=0\) and \(d=R\) are excluded; at \(d=0\) the disk has a continuum of rotated equilateral maximizers, while at \(d=R\) the intersection degenerates.

## Assumptions and scope
The disks are closed Euclidean disks of equal radius \(R\), with centers separated by \(2d\), where \(R>0\) and \(0<d<R\). Triangles may be arbitrarily placed inside their intersection; no symmetry constraint is placed on a candidate triangle. The formula is invariant under Euclidean similarity. We normalize \(R=1\) in the proof and write \(h=d/R\), \(H=\sqrt{1-h^2}\), \(\theta=\arccos h\), and
\[
f(y)=\sqrt{1-y^2}-h \quad (-H\le y\le H).
\]
Then the lens has horizontal sections \(\{(x,y):|x|\le f(y)\}\) and arc tips \((0,\pm H)\). The two smooth boundary arcs each subtend angle \(2\theta<\pi\) at their generating circle centers.

## Proof
**1. No maximizer has three smooth boundary vertices.** Compactness gives an area-maximizing triangle, and its area is strictly positive. Each vertex is on the lens boundary: with the other two fixed, interior displacement normal to the opposite side would strictly increase area. At a smooth boundary vertex, a maximum requires that the supporting tangent line be parallel to the opposite triangle side. Equivalently, the radius joining that vertex to the center of its generating circle lies along a triangle altitude.

Suppose all three vertices are smooth. If they belong to the same generating circle, its center lies on all three altitudes, and is also their circumcenter. Therefore the triangle must be equilateral. But no arc of angular length less than \(\pi\) contains three vertices of an inscribed equilateral triangle (its smallest containing circular arc has length \(4\pi/3\)). This case is impossible.

Otherwise, by relabeling and reflection, two vertices lie on the right arc \(x=f(y)\), generated by the circle centered at \((-h,0)\), and the third lies on the left arc \(x=-f(y)\), generated by the circle centered at \((h,0)\). The altitudes from the two right-arc vertices concur at \((-h,0)\). The altitude from the left-arc vertex must pass through both generating-circle centers, hence is horizontal. Its vertex is therefore the leftmost point \((h-1,0)\), and the opposite side is vertical. The two right-arc vertices consequently have coordinates \((u-h,\pm a)\), where \(a>0\), \(u=\sqrt{1-a^2}\in(h,1)\). Their triangle area is
\[
A_{\mathrm{sm}}(u)=(u+1-2h)\sqrt{1-u^2}.
\]
Stationarity in the smooth one-parameter family gives
\[
2u^2+(1-2h)u-1=0.
\]
The positive root satisfies \(u>\max\{h,1/2\}\), since the left side is negative at both \(u=h\) and \(u=1/2\), and positive at \(u=1\).

The full boundary variation, rather than just the symmetric family, now rules out a maximum. Let
\[
y_1=-a+\varepsilon,\quad y_2=-2\varepsilon,\quad
 y_3=a+\varepsilon,
\]
and take successive vertices \((f(y_1),y_1)\), \((-f(y_2),y_2)\), \((f(y_3),y_3)\). For sufficiently small \(\varepsilon\), they remain on the same smooth arcs. Their unsigned area near \(\varepsilon=0\) equals
\[
A(\varepsilon)=\frac12\bigl[(y_3-y_2)f(y_1)
+(y_3-y_1)f(y_2)+(y_2-y_1)f(y_3)\bigr].
\]
Direct differentiation using \(f'(y)=-y/\sqrt{1-y^2}\) and \(f''(y)=-(1-y^2)^{-3/2}\) yields
\[
A'(0)=0,\qquad
A''(0)=\frac{a}{u^3}\bigl(-1+6u^2-4u^3\bigr)>0.
\]
Indeed the parenthesized polynomial vanishes at \(u=1/2\) and has strictly positive derivative \(12u(1-u)\) on \((1/2,1)\). Thus this stationary smooth triangle has an increasing area under one of the two small perturbations; it is not a local maximum. Consequently every global maximizer has a nonsmooth vertex, necessarily one of the two arc tips.

**2. Optimization conditioned on an upper arc tip.** By central symmetry it suffices to suppose one vertex is \((0,H)\). For the other two points \((x,y)\), \((z_x,z)\) in the lens, their vertical coordinates belong to \([-H,H]\), and
\[
2\operatorname{area}(T)\le f(y)(H-z)+f(z)(H-y).
\]
Equality requires the horizontal coordinates to be opposite extremal points of their sections, whenever both heights are below \(H\) and the triangle has positive area.

Write \(H=\sin\theta\), \(h=\cos\theta\), \(y=\sin\alpha\), \(z=\sin\beta\), where \(\alpha,\beta\in[-\theta,\theta]\). Put \(m=(\alpha+\beta)/2\), \(q=(\alpha-\beta)/2\). Expanding the previous right side gives exactly
\[
2\sin(\theta+m)\cos q-\sin(2m)-2hH.
\]
Because \(\theta+m\in[0,2\theta]\subset[0,\pi)\), the coefficient of \(\cos q\) is nonnegative. Replacing \(q\) by zero cannot reduce area. At a positive maximum equality forces \(q=0\) (the vanishing-coefficient endpoint would give a degenerate triangle). Thus \(y=z\), and both horizontal endpoints are \(\pm f(y)\). Their area is \(f(y)(H-y)\).

For \(y>0\) and \(f(y)>0\), reflection to \(-y\) increases this area strictly. Hence the optimum has \(y=-a\), with \(a\in[0,H]\), maximizing
\[
F(a)=(\sqrt{1-a^2}-h)(H+a).
\]
Its second derivative is
\[
F''(a)=-\frac{H+a}{(1-a^2)^{3/2}}
-\frac{2a}{\sqrt{1-a^2}}<0 \quad (0\le a\le H).
\]
Moreover \(F'(0)=1-h>0\) and \(F'(H)<0\), so there is a unique maximizer in \((0,H)\).

Put \(a=\sin t\), \(0<t<\theta\). Elementary sum-to-product identities give
\[
F(\sin t)=2\sin^2\left(\frac{\theta+t}{2}\right)
\sin(\theta-t).
\]
At the unique stationary point, differentiating its logarithm shows
\[
\cot\left(\frac{\theta+t}{2}\right)=\cot(\theta-t).
\]
Both arguments lie strictly between \(0\) and \(\pi/2\), so injectivity of cotangent gives \((\theta+t)/2=\theta-t\), hence \(t=\theta/3\). Substituting yields
\[
F\bigl(\sin(\theta/3)\bigr)=2\sin^3(2\theta/3).
\]
All equality requirements are strict apart from the specified coordinate reflections, so the unique maximizing triangle with upper tip is
\[
\operatorname{conv}\{(0,H),(-r,-\sin(\theta/3)),
(r,-\sin(\theta/3))\},
\quad r=\cos(\theta/3)-h.
\]
Central inversion gives the sole maximizer with lower tip. Restoring the scale \(R\) multiplies all vertices by \(R\) and all areas by \(R^2\), proving the stated result.

## Verification
The standalone `verify.py` uses only the Python standard library. Run `python3 verify.py` in the directory containing this document. It compares the closed formula with the determinant of its claimed triangle, tests the unsquared stationarity equation, and checks the positive smooth-variation curvature for twelve values of \(h\) across \((0,1)\). It additionally performs 60,000 seeded triangle trials, sampling both boundary arcs and the interior. It checks the previously solved special case \(h=1/2\). A successful run prints `VERIFY_OK lens cases=12 numerical_diagnostics=60036 prior_special_case=matched`. These are finite numerical diagnostics only: the continuum theorem and uniqueness are proved by the preceding analytic arguments.

## Relationship to prior work
Kallus's *Inextensible domains* [1] discusses maximal inscribed triangles in centrally symmetric planar convex bodies and proves characterizations connected to planar lattice covering and a rotating family of such triangles. Its general theorems do not provide an exact formula for intersections of disks. Jerónimo-Castro [2] establishes sharp area-ratio bounds under an additional rotating-maximal-triangle hypothesis, not a lens-specific all-separation optimizer.

The online discussion *Largest Area Triangle in the Vesica Piscis* [3] **already solves the case \(d/R=1/2\)**, obtaining \(2R^2\sin^3(2\pi/9)\) and exhibiting the corresponding isosceles triangle. The same discussion mentions the possibility of treating unequal center-distance-to-radius ratios but does not state or prove the full formula and equality classification for every \(0<d/R<1\). That solved special case is not claimed as new. A public mathematical record [4] addresses mean width of congruent-disk intersections, a distinct invariant which does not determine maximal inscribed triangle area.

The contribution asserted here is the uniform sharp all-separation formula, its complete two-triangle equality classification, and the all-parameter exclusion of smooth local maximizers through a positive second variation. An equivalent result under older terminology may exist; no historical-priority claim beyond the inspected comparisons is made.

## Limitations
The theorem concerns two *congruent* disks in the Euclidean plane, with a nonzero, non-tangent center separation strictly smaller than twice their radius. No extension to unequal radii, higher-dimensional simplices, multiple intersecting disks, or general convex lenses is proved. The cited 2024 special case is prior coverage, and older equivalent statements cannot be excluded by search. Reproducible numerical testing does not replace the proof, and independent mathematical audit has not been performed.
