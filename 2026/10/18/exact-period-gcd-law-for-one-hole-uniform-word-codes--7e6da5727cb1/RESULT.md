# Exact period–GCD law for one-hole uniform word codes

## Finding

Let \(\Sigma\) be any finite alphabet with at least two letters, let \(k\ge 2\), and let \(u\in\Sigma^k\). Define the fixed-length code
\[
X(u)=\Sigma^k\setminus\{u\}
\]
and its shortest uncompletable-factor length
\[
U(u)=\min\big\{|z|:z\notin\operatorname{Fac}(X(u)^*)\big\},
\]
where \(\operatorname{Fac}\) means contiguous factors of arbitrary finite concatenations. Write the distinct **proper periods** of \(u\) in increasing order as \(p_1<\cdots<p_m\): thus \(1\le p_i<k\) and the prefix of length \(k-p_i\) equals the suffix of that length. Define
\[
g_0=k,\qquad g_i=\gcd(g_{i-1},p_i)\quad(1\le i\le m).
\]
If there are no proper periods, take \(m=0\), \(g_m=g_0=k\), and the sum below to be empty. Then **for every** such alphabet and word,
\[
\boxed{\displaystyle U(u)=k+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(g_{i-1}-g_i)p_i+(g_m-1)(k+1).}
\]
The proof constructs a word of exactly this length missing from the factor language; its shortestness follows from a weighted spanning-tree lower bound. The formula is computable from the period list using only \(O(k)\) greatest-common-divisor evaluations. It does not require explicitly listing the exponentially many codewords of \(X(u)\).

For an unbordered word (empty proper-period list), this specializes to the previously known value \(k^2+k-1\). Conversely, every bordered word has a strictly smaller value, so unbordered deleted words are exactly the maximizers in this one-hole class. For a constant word it gives \(2k-1\).

A genuinely multiple-period example is \(u=\mathtt{010010}\), with proper periods \(3\) and \(5\) and \((g_0,g_1,g_2)=(6,3,1)\). Its exact shortest missing-factor length is \(6+3\cdot3+2\cdot5=25\). One shortest uncompletable word is `0100100101001001010010010`; the six copies of the deleted block begin at zero-based positions \(0,3,8,11,16,19\), one in each residue class modulo \(6\).

## Assumptions and scope

The alphabet is finite of cardinality at least two. The deleted block has length \(k\ge2\); all *allowed* codewords have exactly length \(k\). There is exactly one forbidden length-\(k\) codeword, which may be periodic or bordered. The factor language allows arbitrary starting and ending positions inside codeword concatenations; it is not just the set of full concatenations. A proper period \(p\) means \(u[j]=u[j+p]\) whenever both indices lie in \(0,\ldots,k-1\). All residue classes are taken modulo \(k\).

## Proof

**1. Alignment criterion.** An arbitrary word \(z\) is a factor of \(X(u)^*\) if and only if at least one of the \(k\) possible codeword-boundary alignments contains no full occurrence of \(u\) among its length-\(k\) blocks lying wholly inside \(z\). Indeed, in such an alignment every wholly observed block is allowed. Each partially observed end block can be completed to a block different from \(u\), because at least one of its symbols is free and the alphabet has at least two symbols. Conversely, any embedding into an allowed concatenation supplies such an alignment. Consequently,
\[
z\notin\operatorname{Fac}(X(u)^*)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\{j\bmod k:z[j:j+k]=u\}=\mathbb Z/k\mathbb Z,
\]
where the set ranges over valid starting indices \(0\le j\le|z|-k\).

**2. A weighted residue graph and the lower bound.** Suppose \(z\) is uncompletable. Choose exactly one occurrence of \(u\) from each residue class and order their starting positions as \(s_0<\cdots<s_{k-1}\). The starting residues are distinct. If a gap \(d=s_{j+1}-s_j\) is smaller than \(k\), the two copies overlap, so \(d\) is necessarily a proper period of \(u\). Otherwise their distinct residues force \(d\ge k+1\).

Construct an undirected weighted graph on \(\mathbb Z/k\mathbb Z\). For each proper period \(p_i\), put an edge of weight \(p_i\) between each residue \(r\) and \(r+p_i\). Also put an edge of weight \(k+1\) between every two distinct residues. Parallel edges are permitted; the lighter one is effective. Each of the \(k-1\) consecutive gaps above corresponds to an edge of weight **at most** that gap. These edges form a spanning path, so their total cost is at least the minimum-spanning-tree weight \(W\), and \(|z|\ge k+W\).

Kruskal's algorithm computes \(W\) exactly. After all edges for periods \(p_1,\ldots,p_i\) have been made available, connected components are exactly the cosets of the subgroup generated by those periods in \(\mathbb Z/k\mathbb Z\). Their number is \(g_i\). Processing edges in increasing proper-period order therefore chooses precisely \(g_{i-1}-g_i\) edges of weight \(p_i\). Finally, \(g_m-1\) complete-graph edges of weight \(k+1\) join the remaining components. It follows that
\[
W=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(g_{i-1}-g_i)p_i+(g_m-1)(k+1),
\]
proving the displayed lower bound.

**3. A path achieving the spanning-tree weight.** We now build a path through every residue exactly once using only the allowed steps \(p_i\), plus steps of length \(k+1\), and with total length \(W\). Start with the one-vertex path at residue \(0\), which traverses the subgroup \(H_0=\{0\}\). Inductively suppose there is a path through exactly the subgroup \(H_{i-1}=g_{i-1}\mathbb Z/k\mathbb Z\). To traverse \(H_i\), concatenate \(g_{i-1}/g_i\) translated copies of that path. Join the endpoint of each copy to the beginning of the next with a forward step of size \(p_i\). Modulo \(H_{i-1}\), the successive copies lie in distinct cosets generated by \(p_i\), so they partition \(H_i\). This also works when \(g_i=g_{i-1}\), with just one copy.

Finally concatenate \(g_m\) translated copies of the path through \(H_m\), using a forward gap of length \(k+1\) between copies. Its residue is \(1\) modulo \(k\), so these copies traverse all cosets of \(H_m\) exactly once. Thus all \(k\) residues occur exactly once. Each level's connecting gaps are copied at subsequent levels, giving exactly \(g_{i-1}-g_i\) final steps of length \(p_i\) and \(g_m-1\) steps of length \(k+1\). Their total span is \(W\).

**4. Realize the occurrence positions.** Place copies of \(u\) starting at the nonnegative integer positions of this forward path, with first start zero. A connecting gap \(p_i<k\) is a period, so consecutive copies agree on their overlap. A gap \(k+1\) has no overlap. Nonconsecutive copies are compatible as well: if their starts differ by less than \(k\), their difference is a sum of successive proper-period steps and is itself a period, because the sum of two periods of a word is a period whenever the sum is shorter than the word. Free positions may be filled arbitrarily. This produces a word \(z\) of length \(k+W\) having an occurrence of \(u\) in every residue class. By Step 1 it is uncompletable. Together with Step 2, this proves the exact formula for all \(u\).

**5. Extremal corollaries.** When no proper period exists, every spanning-path gap costs at least \(k+1\), and the formula gives \(k^2+k-1\). If a proper period exists, already processing its first edges replaces one or more edges of weight \(k+1\) by strictly cheaper edges, and hence \(U(u)<k^2+k-1\). If \(u\) is constant, the period \(1\) joins all residues at unit cost, giving \(U(u)=2k-1\).

## Verification

`verify_onehole.py` implements two logically different finite checks. Its KMP-based breadth-first search reads candidate missing factors character by character and tracks the residues of complete forbidden-block occurrences. Its independent prefix-flower nondeterministic automaton directly recognizes factors of concatenations of allowed words and breadth-first searches for the first empty path-set. Neither implementation uses the period–GCD expression to determine the search answer.

Replaying `python3 verify_onehole.py` gives `PASS`: exact agreement for all \(508\) binary deleted blocks of lengths \(2\) through \(8\), with additional direct flower-automaton agreement for all \(124\) binary blocks of lengths \(2\) through \(6\). `verification_output.txt` contains the recorded complete per-length distributions. An independent directed Held–Karp Hamilton-path checker in `verify_residue_dp.py` also verifies the weighted-residue construction for all 1,022 formal period subsets at \(2\le k\le10\); see `residue_dp_output.txt`. The infinite-domain theorem rests on the proof above, not on these finite experiments.

## Relationship to prior work

Pribavkina, *Slowly Synchronizing Automata with Zero and Incomplete Sets* (2009), Proposition 2, establishes \(U(u)=k^2+k-1\) for **unbordered** missing words \(u\), and connects these one-hole codes to synchronizing automata. Fici, Pribavkina, and Sakarovitch (2010), Proposition 2.3, independently presents the same unbordered boundary and Section 3 investigates minimal-word structure assuming unborderedness. Chandelkar and Chadha, *Quadratic Bounds for Uncompletable Words and Matrix Mortality* (2026), Proposition 5.1, uses the unbordered instance \(u=a^{k-1}b\), and Theorem 1.1 gives the general upper bound \(4k^2-3k\) for arbitrary incomplete uniquely decipherable codes. The new statement is the **exact closed formula for every bordered as well as unbordered missing block**, with all simultaneous periods accounted for through a gcd filtration. It neither improves nor replaces the published general-code bound, because it concerns the more structured single-hole fixed-length class.

The alignment criterion in Step 1 is related to the elementary residue-obstruction argument used in the cited papers. The weighted spanning-tree formula and a matching Hamilton-path realization are the additional mathematical content. The novelty comparison is limited to examined sources and indexed searches; equivalent unindexed work cannot be excluded.

## Limitations

Only a code containing **every** length-\(k\) word except one is covered; deleting two blocks or adding shorter codewords changes the problem. The expression computes the shortest missing-factor **length**, not the number or complete language of shortest witnesses. Algorithmic finite checks cover the binary cases indicated above, and cannot by themselves establish the result for unbounded \(k\) or larger alphabets. The proof is a mathematical argument requiring independent expert assessment; none has yet occurred.
