# Exact two- and three-state separation of nearest binary conjugates

## Finding

Let \(\Sigma\) be any alphabet of cardinality \(q\ge 2\), let \(n\ge 2\), and for \(1\le k<n\) let \(\rho_k\) denote left cyclic rotation of a length-\(n\) word by \(k\) symbols. Write \(\operatorname{sep}(u,v)\) for the minimum number of states of a **complete deterministic one-way finite automaton** accepting exactly one of the distinct words \(u,v\). Put
\[
R_{\Sigma}(n,k)=\max_{w\in\Sigma^n,\ w\ne\rho_k(w)}\operatorname{sep}(w,\rho_k(w)).
\]
For every such alphabet, the exact profile at the first two nontrivial cyclic shifts is
\[
\boxed{R_{\Sigma}(n,k)=2\quad\text{if }\min\{k,n-k\}=1,}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{R_{\Sigma}(n,k)=3\quad\text{if }\min\{k,n-k\}=2.}
\]
The second case necessarily has \(n\ge4\). An equivalent constructive statement underlying the upper bound is: **for any binary words \(A,B\) with \(|A|=2\) and \(AB\ne BA\), there is a complete three-state DFA separating \(AB\) and \(BA\)**. The three states suffice irrespective of the length of \(B\).

## Assumptions and scope

All automata have a single initial state, arbitrary accepting-state sets, no epsilon transitions, and total transitions on the specified alphabet. An automaton may be selected separately for each pair of words. The initial state is not assumed to be a prescribed named state. The result concerns ordinary deterministic separation, **not** separation by permutation-only automata or nondeterministic state complexity. Rotation is on finite words; the maximum excludes equal rotations. Word concatenation acts on state transformations in input order: if \(T_u\) is the action of \(u\), then \(T_{uv}=T_v\circ T_u\).

## Proof

**1. Alphabet reduction and inversion of shifts.** Let \(w\ne\rho_k(w)\). At some position the symbols differ. Map these two symbols to \(0\) and \(1\), respectively, and map all remaining symbols arbitrarily to one of these binary symbols. The projected words remain distinct, and projection commutes with rotation. A binary DFA separating the projected pair lifts unchanged to \(\Sigma\). Thus \(R_\Sigma(n,k)=R_{\{0,1\}}(n,k)\). Also \(R_\Sigma(n,k)=R_\Sigma(n,n-k)\), since the two rotations are inverses and deterministic separation is symmetric.

**2. One-symbol rotations.** Let \(w\) be nonconstant and \(v=\rho_1(w)\ne w\). If the first and last bits of \(w\) differ, the two last bits of \(w,v\) differ, so a two-state last-symbol recorder separates them. If they agree, call their value \(c\). The final run of \(c\)'s in \(v\) has length exactly one greater than the final run in \(w\), because the removed first \(c\) is appended. Define a two-state DFA whose transition on \(c\) swaps its states and whose transition on the other bit sends both states to state \(0\). Since each word contains the other bit, the final states record the parity of these trailing run lengths, which differ by one. Thus \(\operatorname{sep}(w,v)\le2\). A one-state DFA cannot distinguish any pair, yielding equality.

**3. Two-symbol rotations: equal-symbol prefix.** Write \(w=AB\), \(v=BA\), \(|A|=2\). If \(A=00\), then \(AB\ne BA\) implies \(B\) contains a \(1\). Take three states \(\{0,1,2\}\), make the transition on \(0\) a three-cycle, and make the transition on \(1\) constant to \(0\). In \(AB=00B\) and \(BA=B00\), the number of zeroes after the **last** \(1\) differs by exactly two. The three-cycle therefore yields different terminal states. If \(A=11\), exchange the symbols. Final accepting states may be chosen to distinguish the resulting states.

**4. Two-symbol rotations: mixed prefix.** It suffices by exchanging symbols to treat \(A=01\). Use the following transformations on the three states \(\{0,1,2\}\), written as their images of \(0,1,2\):
\[
T_0=(0,0,1),\qquad T_1=(2,0,2).
\]
Set \(P=T_{01}=T_1\circ T_0=(2,2,0)\). The crucial structural lemma is
\[
\boxed{T_{01}T_B=T_BT_{01}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad B\in(01)^*.}
\]
Here the displayed products denote composition of transformations (their order is immaterial for the commutativity equation). To establish the lemma for **every possible length of \(B\)**, consider the finite transformation monoid generated by \(T_0,T_1\). Its complete reachable closure from the identity has exactly twelve elements. The table lists each transformation's image triple, the index reached by appending \(0\) or \(1\), and whether it commutes with \(P\):

| index | image triple | append 0 | append 1 | commutes with \(P\) |
|---:|:---:|---:|---:|:---:|
| 0 | `(0,1,2)` | 1 | 2 | yes |
| 1 | `(0,0,1)` | 3 | 4 | no |
| 2 | `(2,0,2)` | 5 | 6 | no |
| 3 | `(0,0,0)` | 3 | 6 | no |
| 4 | `(2,2,0)` | 7 | 6 | yes |
| 5 | `(1,0,1)` | 3 | 8 | no |
| 6 | `(2,2,2)` | 9 | 6 | no |
| 7 | `(1,1,0)` | 3 | 10 | no |
| 8 | `(0,2,0)` | 11 | 6 | no |
| 9 | `(1,1,1)` | 3 | 3 | no |
| 10 | `(0,0,2)` | 1 | 6 | yes |
| 11 | `(0,1,0)` | 3 | 2 | no |

Each row is checked directly by composing triples. The set of indices \(\{2,3,5,6,8,9,11\}\) is closed under both appended letters and has no commuting state. The only way to travel from the identity through index \(1\) or \(7\) into a commuting state is by the next expected letter \(1\); the sole permissible continuing alternating path is
\[
0\xrightarrow{0}1\xrightarrow{1}4\xrightarrow{0}7\xrightarrow{1}10\xrightarrow{0}1\xrightarrow{1}4\xrightarrow{0}\cdots.
\]
Its commuting endpoints are exactly the endpoints of \((01)^*\). The finite, closed transition table proves the lemma for arbitrarily long words; it is **not** a bounded experimental search.

If \(AB\ne BA\) for \(A=01\), then \(B\notin(01)^*\): indeed, the elementary equation \(01B=B01\) forces nonempty \(B\) to begin with \(01\), and cancellation iterates. By the lemma, the transformations \(T_{01B}=T_B\circ P\) and \(T_{B01}=P\circ T_B\) differ. Choose a state on which they differ as the initial state and one of the distinct images as the sole accepting state. This gives a complete three-state DFA separating the pair. For \(A=10\), interchange the binary letters in the same construction.

**5. Sharpness for every length.** For each \(n\ge4\), consider
\[
w=0^{n-2}10,\qquad \rho_2(w)=0^{n-4}1000.
\]
Both end in \(0\) and have the same number of each symbol. Any complete two-state transition on \(\{0,1\}\) is either a permutation (identity or swap) or a constant map. If the transition on \(0\) is constant, the final \(0\) makes the actions of the two words identical. Otherwise the transition on \(0\) is a permutation. If the transition on \(1\) is also a permutation, the two actions agree because all permutations on two points commute and the words have the same Parikh vector. If the transition on \(1\) is constant, there are respectively one and three \(0\)'s after the last \(1\), and a two-point permutation has order dividing two; the actions again agree. Hence **all** two-state automata finish in identical states on these words, regardless of their initial state. At least three states are necessary, and the upper bound supplies three.

The binary upper and lower bounds, alphabet reduction, and inversion of rotations together prove the formulas.

## Verification

The bundled `verify.py` generates the full finite transformation closure from the specified two generators, checks every transition and commutativity bit, and uses a product-state reachability argument to verify language equality with \((01)^*\), including all word lengths. It also checks all distinct one-step and two-step binary conjugates of lengths \(2\) through \(12\) against the constructions and verifies the two-state lower-bound family for \(4\le n\le400\). Run `python3 verify.py`. The exact recorded output is `verification_output.txt`.

The infinite upper bound follows from the closed twelve-state transformation graph and the direct case analysis; small-length enumeration is solely a consistency check. The infinite lower-bound family has the separate analytic argument above.

## Relationship to prior work

Demaine, Eisenstat, Shallit, and Wilson (2011) give the explicit example \(\operatorname{sep}(0010,1000)=3\), establishing a single-length lower witness, but no exact all-length two-shift classification. Nicol (2026), in Section 3, studies the extremal separation of conjugate words and proves the general \(R(n,k)\le 2\min\{k,n-k\}+1\), which gives upper bounds \(3\) and \(5\) at the first and second shifts. The present formulas sharpen these to **exactly \(2\) and \(3\)** for every admissible length and every finite alphabet, and the uniform centralizer lemma proves the second upper bound. The published general lower-embedding and arithmetic bounds do not imply these exact constants.

For comparison, existing formulas concerning *permutation-only* separators for words such as \(a^r b^s\) versus \(b^s a^r\) restrict the machine class and do not cover the unrestricted complete-DFA statement here. A separate result elsewhere using the same parameters is not enough for coverage unless its conclusion implies these bounds.

## Limitations

Only cyclic shifts by one or two symbols (or their inverse shifts) are classified. The proof does not claim the bound three for arbitrary shifts, arbitrary nonconjugate pairs, NFAs, or permutation-only DFAs. The twelve-state calculation proves a finite transition **closure**, not a classification of the full three-state transformation monoid. Priority over unindexed publications is not certified, and independent mathematical audit remains outstanding.
