# Exceptional-prime singular schemes of cyclic Klein hypersurfaces

## Finding
Let \(k\) be algebraically closed of characteristic \(p>0\), \(s\ge4\), and \(d\ge3\) with \(p\nmid d\). Define the cyclic Klein hypersurface
\[
X_{s,d}=V(F_{s,d})\subset\mathbb P_k^{s-1},\qquad
F_{s,d}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb Z/s}x_i^{d-1}x_{i+1},
\]
where the indices are cyclic, and set
\[
Q_{s,d}=\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}(1-d)^j
       =\frac{(1-d)^s-1}{-d} \in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}.
\]
The hypersurface is smooth exactly when \(p\nmid Q_{s,d}\). In the other case write \(|Q_{s,d}|=p^v m\), with \(v\ge1\) and \(p\nmid m\). Then its scheme-theoretic singular locus is a torsor under \(\mu_{|Q_{s,d}|}\) over \(k\). It is supported on exactly \(m\) distinct points, all in the projective torus with nonzero coordinates. At every support point its local Tjurina algebra is
\[
k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^{p^v}),
\]
and the ambient projective Hessian of the local equation has rank \(s-2\) (corank one). Each singularity therefore has hypersurface multiplicity two. The variety is geometrically integral, normal, Cohen–Macaulay, and Gorenstein.

For instance, at \((s,d,p)=(4,3,5)\) and \((5,3,11)\) there is exactly one singular point, with respective Tjurina lengths \(5\) and \(11\). At \((6,3,7)\) there are three singular points, each of Tjurina length \(7\).

## Assumptions and scope
The field is algebraically closed. The number of variables is \(s\ge4\), the homogeneous degree is \(d\ge3\), and the prime characteristic satisfies \(p\nmid d\). The group scheme \(\mu_r\) is the kernel of the \(r\)-th power map on \(\mathbb G_m\); it can be nonreduced when \(p\mid r\). The term *Tjurina algebra* means the local quotient of the hypersurface ring by the first partial derivatives of an affine local defining equation. The Hessian rank is computed at a geometric singular point on a smooth projective ambient chart. The theorem does not address \(p\mid d\) or degree two.

## Proof
**1. The singular locus avoids the boundary.** Put \(a=d-1\ge2\) and \(t_i=x_i^a x_{i+1}\). The homogeneous partial derivatives satisfy
\[
x_i\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_i}=a t_i+t_{i-1}.
\]
If \(x_i=0\), then \(\partial F/\partial x_i=x_{i-1}^a=0\) at any singular point, hence \(x_{i-1}=0\). Iteration around the cycle would force all coordinates to vanish, impossible in projective space. This argument holds also when \(p\mid a\). Thus every singular point lies in the dense torus \(T=(\mathbb G_m)^s/\mathbb G_m\).

**2. An integral torus isogeny and its kernel.** On \(T\) define
\[
\Phi:T\longrightarrow T,\qquad [x_0:\cdots:x_{s-1}]\longmapsto[t_0:\cdots:t_{s-1}].
\]
This is well-defined since all \(t_i\) have the same degree \(d\). The character lattice of \(T\) is generated by \(\alpha_i=e_i-e_{i+1}\), with the sole relation \(\sum_i\alpha_i=0\). The pullback of \(t_i/t_{i+1}\) is the character \(a\alpha_i+\alpha_{i+1}\). In the cokernel of the character map, these relations give \(\alpha_{i+1}=-a\alpha_i\) and consequently \(Q_{s,d}\alpha_0=0\). The final cyclic relation gives \(((1-d)^s-1)\alpha_0=-dQ_{s,d}\alpha_0=0\) and is redundant. No smaller relation arises because the presentation consists precisely of these relations. Therefore
\[
\operatorname{coker}(\Phi^*)\cong\mathbb Z/|Q_{s,d}|\mathbb Z,
\quad \ker(\Phi)\cong\mu_{|Q_{s,d}|}.
\]
In particular \(\Phi\) is a finite surjective isogeny of degree \(|Q_{s,d}|\), including when it is inseparable.

**3. Exact singular scheme.** Since \(p\nmid d\), Euler's identity \(dF=\sum_i x_i\partial_iF\) makes \(F\) redundant in the Jacobian ideal. On the torus every \(x_i\) is invertible, and the Jacobian equations are exactly \(t_{i-1}=-a t_i\), scheme-theoretically. If \(p\mid a\) these equations have no torus solution and \(Q_{s,d}\equiv1\pmod p\), as claimed. Otherwise put \(c=-a=1-d\in k^*\). Compatibility around the cycle requires \(c^s=1\). Because \(c-1=-d\) is a unit, this holds precisely when \(p\mid Q_{s,d}\). In that case the target point \([t_i]\) is uniquely determined by the ratios \(t_{i-1}=c t_i\), and \(\sum_i t_i=0\) by the geometric-series identity. The Jacobian scheme is exactly its fiber under \(\Phi\). Any geometric fiber of a surjective torus isogeny is a torsor under its kernel, hence under \(\mu_{|Q_{s,d}|}\).

Now write \(|Q_{s,d}|=p^v m\), \(p\nmid m\). Over \(k\), the group scheme \(\mu_{|Q_{s,d}|}\) has \(m\) geometric points; its local algebra at each point is \(k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^{p^v})\), since \(u^{p^v}-1=(u-1)^{p^v}\). Via the Euler identity, this is exactly the local Tjurina algebra of \(X_{s,d}\). When \(p\nmid Q_{s,d}\), there is no singular point at all.

**4. Hessian rank.** At a singular point \(p\mid Q_{s,d}\) forces \(p\nmid a\); moreover \(p\) is odd because \(p\nmid a(a+1)\). Let \(E\) be the cyclic exponent matrix with rows having \(a\) in position \(i\) and \(1\) in position \(i+1\), and set \(D=\operatorname{diag}(t_i)\). In logarithmic tangent coordinates of \((\mathbb G_m)^s\), the second differential at a critical point is
\[
H=E^{\mathsf T} D E.
\]
The cyclic recurrence \(E v=0\) has a one-dimensional solution space because \(c^s=1\), and \(E^{\mathsf T}t=0\) by the Jacobian equations. Thus \(\operatorname{im}E=\{w:\sum_i t_i w_i=0\}\). Because \(\sum_i t_i=0\), the all-ones vector \(\mathbf1\) belongs to \(\operatorname{im}E\), and because \(E\mathbf1=d\mathbf1\) with \(d\ne0\) in \(k\), it has a preimage independent of \(\ker E\). For any \(v\),
\[
Hv=0\iff D Ev\in\ker(E^{\mathsf T})=kt
\iff Ev\in k\mathbf1.
\]
Hence \(\ker H\) in the \(s\)-dimensional logarithmic space is two-dimensional, spanned by the scalar/radial vector and a generator of \(\ker E\). After quotienting by the radial direction, the Hessian has a one-dimensional kernel in dimension \(s-1\), i.e. rank \(s-2\). This rank is at least two; since the linear term vanishes, the multiplicity of the local hypersurface equation is exactly two.

**5. Global properties.** The singular locus is finite, and the coordinate point \([1:0:\cdots:0]\in X_{s,d}\) is smooth because \(\partial F/\partial x_1=1\) there. Thus the equation is reduced. If the hypersurface had two distinct irreducible components, they would meet in dimension at least \(s-3\ge1\) and every point in their intersection would be singular, contradicting the finite singular locus. Therefore it is geometrically integral. A hypersurface is Cohen–Macaulay and Gorenstein; since its singular locus has codimension at least two within \(X_{s,d}\), Serre's \(R_1\) and \(S_2\) criteria give normality.

## Verification
The standalone program `verify_klein.py` uses exact arithmetic over prime fields. Direct enumeration on the dense projective torus for \((d,s,p)=(3,4,5),(3,5,11),(3,6,7)\) finds \(1,1,3\) singular points and confirms projective Hessian ranks \(2,3,4\), respectively. It separately checks a smooth case when the characteristic divides the degree, showing why the excluded case needs its own analysis. A local Gröbner calculation for the first two cases yields univariate equations equivalent to \((z-2)^5\) and \((z-4)^{11}\), consistent with Tjurina lengths \(5\) and \(11\). These finite checks are corroboration only; the lattice-isogeny and Hessian arguments prove the uniform theorem.

## Relationship to prior work
González-Aguilera and Liendo (2011, Example 3.5) introduce precisely this cyclic equation and prove smoothness over the complex numbers. Their recurrence isolates the same geometric-series factor, but their result does not state the positive-characteristic singular-scheme structure, exact support counts, local Tjurina algebras, or Hessian coranks. Roulleau (2010) studies the complex Klein cubic threefold and its Fano surface. Heijne (first posted 2012) studies Picard numbers and ADE singularities of complex Delsarte surfaces; the positive-characteristic degenerations addressed here are outside the inspected scope. The exceptional-prime criterion alone is an immediate adaptation of the classical recurrence, not an independently claimed contribution; the contribution is its uniform scheme-theoretic and local-geometric refinement.

## Limitations
No conclusion is drawn in characteristics dividing \(d\), where Euler's identity cannot eliminate the hypersurface equation from the Jacobian ideal. The statement does not identify the full formal analytic normal form beyond the Hessian and Tjurina algebra. An equivalently formulated classification could appear in other studies of invertible loop polynomials or Delsarte hypersurfaces; literature-search incompleteness is an originality risk. The finite checker is not an exhaustive proof of the all-degree statement.
