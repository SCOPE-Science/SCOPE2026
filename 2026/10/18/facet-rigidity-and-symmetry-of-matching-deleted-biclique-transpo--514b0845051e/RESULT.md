# Facet rigidity and symmetry of matching-deleted biclique transportation balls

## Finding
Let \(p,q\ge3\) and \(0\le k\le\min\{p,q\}\) be integers. Construct the connected graph \(G_{p,q,k}\) from the complete bipartite graph with parts \(X=\{x_1,\ldots,x_p\}\) and \(Y=\{y_1,\ldots,y_q\}\) by deleting precisely \(\{x_i y_i:1\le i\le k\}\). Endow its vertices with the unit-edge shortest-path metric. The unit ball of its real Lipschitz-free space, equivalently its symmetric-edge transportation polytope, has exactly
\[
\boxed{F(p,q,k)=2^p+2^q-2+2k}
\]
facets. Thus removing any matching of \(k\) edges from the complete biclique creates exactly \(2k\) additional facets, with no interaction terms.

More strongly, the facets are exhausted by these explicit types, with only the overlap noted below: (A) a supporting potential constant on \(X\), taking the two values one unit above or below that constant on each vertex of \(Y\); (B) the symmetric type constant on \(Y\); or (C) one of exactly two exceptional signed potentials associated with each deleted edge. The common intersection of families (A) and (B) consists of precisely two potentials; there is no other overlap. For the exceptional potential associated with the deleted edge \(x_i y_i\), one orientation has heights
\[
 f(x_i)=0,\quad f(y_i)=3,\quad f(x_j)=2\ (j\ne i),\quad f(y_j)=1\ (j\ne i),
\]
and the other is its negative, in each case understood modulo additive constants.

There is also a full classification under graph automorphisms and global sign reversal. Define
\[
a(s,k)=\frac{(k+1)(s-k+1)+\varepsilon(s,k)}2,\qquad
\varepsilon(s,k)=\begin{cases}1,& k\text{ and }s-k\text{ are both even},\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
\]
The exact number \(O(p,q,k)\) of symmetry classes of facets is
\[
\boxed{O(p,q,k)=\begin{cases}
a(p,k)+a(q,k)-1+\mathbf1_{k>0},&p\ne q,\\
a(p,k)+\mathbf1_{k>0},&p=q.
\end{cases}}
\]
The sizes and representatives of **every** orbit are given explicitly in the proof below. For crown graphs \(p=q=k=n\ge3\), the specialization is \(F(n,n,n)=2^{n+1}+2n-2\) and \(O(n,n,n)=\lfloor n/2\rfloor+2\). The crown special case is not independently claimed as a novel count: related earlier work already identifies its diameter-three facet patterns.

## Assumptions and scope
All vector spaces and norms are real. For a finite metric space \((V,d)\), its Lipschitz-free unit ball is
\[
 B_{\mathcal F(V)}=\operatorname{conv}\left\{\frac{e_u-e_v}{d(u,v)}:u,v\in V,\ u\ne v\right\}\subseteq H_V:=\left\{z\in\mathbb R^V:\sum_{v\in V}z_v=0\right\}.
\]
Here \(e_v\) are the standard coordinate vectors. When \(d\) is the shortest-path metric of a connected unit-edge graph, telescoping along shortest paths shows that this ball equals the convex hull of the signed edge vectors \(\pm(e_u-e_v)\). Its dimension is \(|V|-1\). A facet is a codimension-one exposed face of this ball. Potentials are considered modulo additive constants. The polar dual consists of centered functions \(f\) with \(|f(u)-f(v)|\le1\) along graph edges; its vertices correspond bijectively to facets of the primal ball.

The symmetry group used below is precisely the group generated by graph automorphisms and \(f\mapsto-f\). The assertion does not purport to classify arbitrary linear polytope automorphisms.

## Proof
**1. Tight-edge criterion and bipartite rigidity.** Fix one potential coordinate to zero. The rank of the active edge-difference equalities is \(|V|-c\), where \(c\) is the number of connected components of the spanning tight-edge graph. Indeed, this is the standard rank of a graph incidence matrix, obtained by choosing one spanning tree in every component. Thus a feasible potential is a polar vertex exactly when the tight-edge graph is connected and spanning. Along a tight spanning tree all potential increments are \(\pm1\), hence all potential differences are integral. If the original graph is bipartite, parity of the difference between any two vertices is determined by their bipartition classes: along any tight path, increments of \(\pm1\) have the same parity as its length. Every original edge joins opposite classes, so its difference is an odd integer; the Lipschitz inequality forces its absolute value to equal \(1\). Conversely, if all graph edges are tight then the tight-edge graph is the connected original graph, giving a facet. **Therefore, for these graphs, facets correspond exactly to integer potentials whose difference across every surviving edge is \(\pm1\), modulo constants.**

**2. What deleting a matching can change.** In \(G_{p,q,k}\) any two \(X\)-vertices have a common neighbor because each misses at most one \(Y\)-vertex and \(q\ge3\); analogously any two \(Y\)-vertices have a common neighbor. Every missing matched pair \((x_i,y_i)\) has distance precisely three: it is nonadjacent, its endpoints belong to opposite bipartition classes, and a length-three route exists by choosing intermediate indices outside the pair (possible since \(p,q\ge3\)). Thus the difference across a missing pair in any all-tight potential is an odd integer with absolute value at most three, hence either \(1\) or \(3\).

If all missing pairs also have difference of absolute value \(1\), the potential is all-tight on the *complete* bipartite graph. Its complete list is elementary: if there are two distinct values in \(X\), then they differ by \(2\) and every \(Y\)-value equals their midpoint, so \(Y\) is constant; otherwise \(X\) is constant and all \(Y\)-values are one unit above or below it. This gives families (A) and (B). Each family has respectively \(2^q\) and \(2^p\) potentials, while their common part contains exactly two, for a total of \(2^q+2^p-2\).

Otherwise there exists a missing edge \(x_i y_i\) with a gap of absolute value \(3\). After sign reversal and translation, take \(f(x_i)=0\) and \(f(y_i)=3\). All other \(X\)-vertices are adjacent to \(y_i\) and all other \(Y\)-vertices to \(x_i\). Since \(p,q\ge3\), for every \(x_j\) with \(j\ne i\) there is a surviving edge \(x_j y_\ell\) with \(\ell\ne i\), and similarly every \(y_\ell\) has one to an \(x_j\ne x_i\). The possibilities \(f(x_j)\in\{2,4\}\) and \(f(y_\ell)\in\{-1,1\}\) leave only \(f(x_j)=2\), \(f(y_\ell)=1\) on such an edge. Therefore these forced values hold for every other vertex. They give one valid facet potential, and negation gives the second. Distinct deleted edges give distinct exceptional potentials, none present in (A) or (B); there are exactly \(2k\). This proves the classification and facet formula.

**3. Symmetry group and orbit representatives.** For \(k>0\), automorphisms preserving each color class permute the \(k\) matched index pairs *together*, permute the \(p-k\) unmatched \(X\)-vertices freely, and permute the \(q-k\) unmatched \(Y\)-vertices freely. The degree distinction separates matched from unmatched vertices. The same description with \(k=0\) gives unrestricted independent permutations in the parts. Since the graph is connected and bipartite, every automorphism preserves or exchanges its unique bipartition; exchange is possible exactly when \(p=q\). When \(p=q\), a canonical exchange sends \(x_i\leftrightarrow y_i\).

For family (A), put all \(X\)-heights at zero; a representative has \(j\) of the \(k\) matched \(Y\)-vertices at \(+1\) and \(\ell\) of the \(q-k\) unmatched \(Y\)-vertices at \(+1\), all other \(Y\)-heights at \(-1\), where \(0\le j\le k\), \(0\le\ell\le q-k\). Up to part-preserving automorphisms and global negation, \((j,\ell)\) is identified only with \((k-j,q-k-\ell)\). Family (B) has the analogous representatives and identification with \(p\) in place of \(q\). The two constant-on-both potentials make a single shared orbit of size two. Every exceptional potential belongs to a single orbit of size \(2k\) (if \(k>0\)). When \(p=q\), part exchange identifies the remaining (A) and (B) orbits.

For completeness, a class indexed by \((j,\ell)\) on a varying side of size \(s\) has size, within one fixed family,
\[
 \lambda_s(j,\ell)=\binom kj\binom{s-k}{\ell}\left(2-\mathbf1_{\{2j=k,\ 2\ell=s-k\}}\right).
\]
Use exactly one of every complement-pair \((j,\ell)\sim(k-j,s-k-\ell)\). Exclude the shared all-constant pair \((0,0)\sim(k,s-k)\), whose actual orbit has size two. If \(p\ne q\), the remaining family-(A) orbits have sizes \(\lambda_q\) and remaining family-(B) orbits have sizes \(\lambda_p\). If \(p=q\), the corresponding merged nonconstant orbits have sizes \(2\lambda_p\). Add the exceptional orbit of size \(2k\) when \(k>0\). This provides exhaustive, disjoint orbit representatives **and sizes**, not just the orbit total.

There are \((k+1)(s-k+1)\) possible pairs for one family. The complement involution fixes exactly one pair when both group sizes are even, and none otherwise. Burnside's count gives \(a(s,k)\). Combining the two families, subtracting the shared constant orbit, adding the exceptional orbit, and (when \(p=q\)) identifying both families via part exchange yields the stated \(O(p,q,k)\). \(\square\)

## Verification
The standalone Python 3 script `check_matching_deleted_biclique.py` uses only the standard library. Its first enumeration constructs a spanning tree of each graph, independently assigns the two possible signed unit increments on each tree edge, and enforces *all* graph edges. Across \(72\) parameter triples with \(3\le p\le6\), \(p\le q\le7\), \(0\le k\le p\), it checks the facet formula and the exact \(2k\) exceptional patterns. For all cases with \(p+q\le8\), a second enumeration instead allows slack edge differences \(-1,0,1\), then independently checks the full tight-edge incidence rank by connectivity; the resulting polar vertices are identical. For \(52\) cases with \(p+q\le11\), a further calculation constructs automorphism and sign orbits using adjacent-transposition generators and checks the claimed orbit numbers *and every orbit size*. The script also checks crown counts through \(n=8\). Run `python3 check_matching_deleted_biclique.py` from its directory. These finite checks corroborate, but do not replace, the universal proof in the preceding section.

For illustration, \((p,q,k)=(3,4,2)\) gives \(26\) facets split into \(8\) orbits with sizes \(2,2,2,4,4,4,4,4\). The crown case \((4,4,4)\) gives \(38\) facets in \(4\) orbits of sizes \(2,8,12,16\).

## Relationship to prior work
Higashitani, Jochemko and Michałek (2018, Proposition 3.4) give the exact \(2^p+2^q-2\) facet count and the two-family potential classification for *complete* bipartite graphs. D'Alì, Delucchi and Michałek (2019, Lemma 4.5) prove that every facet of a connected bipartite symmetric edge polytope saturates all its edges. Chen, Davis and Korchevskaia (2021, Theorem 3) characterize facet subgraphs generally. Those are crucial previous ingredients and are not claimed here.

The public result *A sharp Euclidean inradius and tangent-facet transition for crown-graph symmetric edge polytopes* (2026) analyzes the fully deleted *balanced* crown \(p=q=k=n\), including its extremal length-three potentials and tangent-facet classes. Consequently the crown subcase of our count has substantial prior structural coverage and is **not** an independent novelty claim. The contribution here is the simultaneous complete enumeration, deletion additivity, explicit automorphism-and-sign orbit classification, and orbit sizes for **all rectangular sizes and every partial matching size** \(0\le k\le\min\{p,q\}\). The cited crown result neither asserts nor implies these arbitrary rectangular/partial-deletion orbit formulas without additional analysis. Braun and Bruegge (2023) tabulate extrema for graphs with few edges and study sparse graph families; their tables can contain isolated small numerical cases, but do not provide this dense matching-deletion family or its orbit structures. Neither the inspected full texts nor targeted mathematical-record searches presented an equivalent general theorem. This is an informed literature comparison, not a universal uniqueness guarantee.

## Limitations
The condition \(p,q\ge3\) is essential to the common-neighbor and length-three arguments; degenerate smaller bicliques, other deleted-edge patterns, and nonunit edge lengths are not covered. Only graph-automorphism-plus-sign orbits are classified, not the entire combinatorial or linear automorphism group of the polytope. These statements concern facet counts and symmetry, not all face numbers or metric inradii. The partial matching case is an elementary-looking but exact structural classification; the earlier crown special case and the existing complete-bipartite base formula must not be presented as novel standalone results. Unindexed equivalent literature remains possible, and independent scholarly assessment has not yet occurred.
