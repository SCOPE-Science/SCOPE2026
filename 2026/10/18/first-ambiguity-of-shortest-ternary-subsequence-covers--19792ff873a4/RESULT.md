# First ambiguity of shortest ternary subsequence covers

## Finding

Let a *subsequence cover* (s-cover) of a nonempty word \(S\) be a nonempty word \(C\) for which every position of \(S\) lies in at least one scattered-subsequence occurrence of \(C\). A *shortest s-cover* minimizes the length of \(C\), counting distinct words rather than occurrences.

**Theorem (sharp ternary threshold and equality classification).** Every nonempty word over \(\Sigma=\{0,1,2\}\) of length at most \(10\) has exactly one shortest s-cover. Among the \(3^{11}=177{,}147\) words of length \(11\), exactly six have more than one shortest s-cover. These are precisely the six alphabet relabelings of

\[
S=\mathtt{01012021010}.
\]

Each of these six words has exactly two shortest s-covers, both of length \(6\). In the displayed labeling they are

\[
C_1=\mathtt{010210},\qquad C_2=\mathtt{012010}=C_1^{\mathrm R}.
\]

Here \(C_1^{\mathrm R}\) denotes reversal. Thus there is a single exceptional orbit under alphabet permutations. Its representative is a palindrome, and its two minimum-length covers are mutual reversals. In particular, length \(11\) is the **first length permitting nonunique shortest s-covers over a ternary alphabet**.

## Assumptions and scope

Words are finite and nonempty. Their alphabet is a subset of the labeled set \(\{0,1,2\}\); use of all three letters is **not** required. A subsequence occurrence is a strictly increasing sequence of positions matching the candidate word. Different occurrences of the same candidate may overlap and may collectively cover all positions. The full word \(S\) itself is always an s-cover, so every nonempty word has a shortest one. The theorem quantifies over all words of lengths \(1\) through \(11\), with no assertion about the number of exceptions for larger lengths.

Two words are considered different s-covers if their letter sequences differ; the number of occurrences is immaterial. The six labeled exceptions are distinct even though they form one orbit under permutations of the three alphabet symbols. Reversal is not an additional equivalence relation in the enumeration.

## Proof

**1. Finite exhaustive search equals the definition.** Fix a word \(S=s_0s_1\cdots s_{n-1}\) of length \(1\le n\le11\). Identify its positions with bit indices \(0,\ldots,n-1\). For each nonzero bit mask \(b\) with \(1\le b<2^n\), form the subsequence \(S[b]\) by retaining exactly the positions whose bits are set. For each distinct nonempty candidate \(C\), set

\[
U_S(C)=\bigvee_{\substack{1\le b<2^n\\ S[b]=C}}b,
\]

where the operation is bitwise OR. Because each nonempty subsequence occurrence is represented by exactly one nonzero mask, the following equivalence is *exact*:

\[
C\text{ is an s-cover of }S
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
U_S(C)=2^n-1.
\]

Taking the minimum length among the keys satisfying this equality, and then retaining all keys at that length, therefore computes **all** shortest s-covers. There is no restriction to candidate covers below any preset length. All integer masks fit into a \(16\)-bit unsigned value when \(n\le11\). The full implementation is in `verify_direct.cpp`.

**2. Exhaustive alphabet-normalized word generation.** Every word over at most three symbols has a unique *restricted-growth representative*: rename its first symbol \(0\), the next previously unseen symbol \(1\), and the third previously unseen symbol \(2\). Hence such a representative starts with \(0\) and, whenever the greatest symbol already present is \(r\), its next symbol may be any value from \(0\) through \(\min(2,r+1)\). Conversely, every word generated by this rule is the unique representative of its alphabet-renaming class. Distinct-cover multiplicity and minimum-cover length are invariant under bijective letter relabeling. Thus complete enumeration of these canonical representatives proves the theorem for all labeled ternary words.

The `verify_direct.cpp` program performs precisely this recursion, with no pruning beyond the above canonicalization. The resulting exact counts of canonical words of lengths \(1,2,\ldots,11\) are

\[
1,\ 2,\ 5,\ 14,\ 41,\ 122,\ 365,\ 1094,\ 3281,\ 9842,\ 29525.
\]

For lengths \(1\) through \(10\), none has multiple shortest covers. At length \(11\), the *only* canonical representative with multiple shortest covers is \(\mathtt{01012021010}\), whose complete minimum-cover set is \(\{\mathtt{010210},\mathtt{012010}\}\). Its three symbols all occur, so it corresponds to exactly \(3!=6\) labeled words. The complete finite search processes exactly \(72{,}517{,}166\) nonempty subsequence occurrences over all the canonical representatives and is guarded by explicit full-census assertions in the supplied source code. This proves every case of the stated theorem, not a sample.

**3. Explicit upper-bound witnesses.** Both claimed covers can be checked without code: the following pairs of *one-based* subsequence-position lists are valid embeddings, and the union of the two lists in each pair is the full set \(\{1,\ldots,11\}\):

- For \(C_1=\mathtt{010210}\): \((1,2,3,5,8,9)\) and \((1,4,6,7,10,11)\).
- For \(C_2=\mathtt{012010}\): \((1,2,5,6,8,9)\) and \((3,4,7,9,10,11)\).

The exhaustive minimum-cover enumeration additionally proves that no word of length at most \(5\) covers this witness and that no third length-\(6\) word does. The exhaustive lower-length census excludes any earlier ambiguous word.

**4. Independent exhaustive cross-check.** The separate program `verify_greedy.cpp` uses a logically different membership test. Given \(S\) and \(C\), it computes the lexicographically *earliest* and *latest* subsequence embeddings \(F\) and \(L\). Position \(i\) is covered if and only if some \(j\) satisfies

\[
s_i=C_j,\quad (j=0\ \text{or}\ F_{j-1}<i),\quad
(j=|C|-1\ \text{or}\ i<L_{j+1}).
\]

Indeed, this permits an earliest prefix occurrence, position \(i\), and a latest suffix occurrence to be concatenated; any occurrence through position \(i\) also implies both inequalities by greedy minimality/maximality. To search all possible *shortest* ternary covers, this second program uses the established facts that a shortest cover is s-primitive, contains no adjacent identical letters, begins and ends with the same symbols as its host, and has length at most \(8\) on a ternary alphabet (the published identity \(\gamma(3)=8\)). It then enumerates **every labeled ternary word** of every length through \(11\), independently of the canonical word recursion.

This independent engine reports \(3^n\) words with unique shortest cover for each \(1\le n\le10\). At length \(11\), it reports \(177{,}141\) words with a unique shortest cover and exactly six with two. The six all normalize to the same word and cover pair found by the definition-based engine. The second certificate uses a published candidate bound; the first does not, and therefore independently establishes the complete finite theorem. \(\square\)

## Verification

Run from the directory containing the supplied source files:

```sh
c++ -std=c++17 -O2 verify_direct.cpp -o verify_direct
./verify_direct
c++ -std=c++17 -O2 verify_greedy.cpp -o verify_greedy
./verify_greedy
```

The programs require only a C++17 standard-library compiler. Both exit unsuccessfully if any expected census, minimum-cover set, or named word classification fails. `verification_output.txt` records the actual outputs. The first program exhausts \(29{,}525\) canonical words at length \(11\) and all shorter canonical words; the second independently inspects all \(177{,}147\) labeled words at length \(11\) and all shorter labeled words. The two distinct mechanisms match. The proof is exact but **finite**; no inference at lengths greater than \(11\) follows from these runs.

## Relationship to prior work

Charalampopoulos, Pissis, Radoszewski, Rytter, Waleń and Zuba introduced subsequence covers, proved the ternary s-primitivity bound \(\gamma(3)=8\), and gave an algorithm for finding a shortest s-cover. Their 2022 conference paper, Section 5, exhibits a four-letter word of length \(15\) with two *equally shortest* covers and a ternary word with two covers of *unequal* lengths. It does **not** state the sharp first ternary nonuniqueness threshold, the canonical length-\(11\) witness, or the six-word equality classification obtained here. The expanded 2025 paper, Section 7, retains the four-letter length-\(15\) first example and develops larger families; its inspected relevant statements do not give this finite ternary classification.

The published testing and shortest-cover algorithms are stronger in *algorithmic generality* but do not imply these exact counts without a new, complete enumeration. Conversely, the present finite census does not improve their algorithms or the known value of \(\gamma(3)\). The first online date of the original conference chapter was 1 November 2022; the subsequent journal/arXiv version is not used to determine eligibility.

## Limitations

This is a computer-assisted, exact finite classification for alphabets of size at most three and word lengths at most eleven. The code, its exhaustive-loop justification, and full outputs are provided for reproducibility, but no separate independent scholarly audit has been conducted. No result is asserted for larger word lengths, larger alphabets, or nonidentical definitions of cover (such as ordinary contiguous covers or disjoint shuffle covers). A mathematically equivalent classification might exist in literature not found by the inspected searches.
