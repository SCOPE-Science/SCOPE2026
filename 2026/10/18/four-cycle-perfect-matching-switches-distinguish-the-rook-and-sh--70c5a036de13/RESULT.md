# Four-cycle perfect-matching switches distinguish the rook and Shrikhande graphs

## Finding

Let \(R=K_4\square K_4\) be the sixteen-vertex rook graph and let \(S\) be the Shrikhande graph. Both have strongly regular parameters \((16,6,2,2)\). For any graph \(G\) with perfect matchings, let \(F_4(G)\) be the graph whose vertices are the perfect matchings of \(G\), adjacent exactly when their symmetric difference is a single alternating four-cycle.

There is an exact difference in the connectivity of the two switch spaces:

\[
|V(F_4(R))|=2016,\quad |E(F_4(R))|=6048,
\]
\[
F_4(R)\text{ has component sizes }1368,288,288,\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{72},
\]
\[
|V(F_4(S))|=1920,\quad |E(F_4(S))|=5280,
\]
\[
F_4(S)\text{ has component sizes }1908,\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{12}.
\]

In particular, \(F_4(R)\) has 75 connected components and 72 frozen perfect matchings, while \(F_4(S)\) has 13 components and 12 frozen perfect matchings. Here *frozen* means isolated under this one-step switch operation; it does not mean immovable under larger even-cycle exchanges. The 1368-vertex rook component has radius 7 and diameter 8; each 288-vertex rook component is three-regular, of radius and diameter 14. The 1908-vertex Shrikhande component has radius 10 and diameter 13.

Thus the strongly regular parameters do not determine the perfect-matching switch component profile, its frozen-state count, or its nontrivial component radii and diameters.

## Assumptions and scope

Use the common vertex set \(V=\mathbb Z_4\times\mathbb Z_4\), labeled by \(u=4a+b\) for \((a,b)\) in \(\{0,1,2,3\}^2\). In \(R\), two distinct vertices are adjacent exactly when their first coordinates or their second coordinates agree. In \(S\), two distinct vertices are adjacent exactly when their difference belongs to
\[
\{\pm(1,0),\ \pm(0,1),\ \pm(1,1)\}\pmod 4.
\]
Both graphs are simple and undirected and have 16 vertices and 48 edges. Their common-neighbor counts are two for every distinct vertex pair, and their neighborhoods distinguish their isomorphism types: a rook vertex has an induced neighborhood \(2K_3\), while a Shrikhande vertex has an induced neighborhood \(C_6\).

A perfect matching has eight pairwise vertex-disjoint edges, covering every vertex exactly once. An allowed switch replaces two edges \(\{a,b\}\) and \(\{c,d\}\) of a perfect matching by either \(\{a,c\},\{b,d\}\) or \(\{a,d\},\{b,c\}\), provided both replacement edges belong to \(G\). No alternating cycles longer than four are permitted. Component radii and diameters are taken in their own connected components; isolated vertices have radius and diameter zero.

## Proof

**Exhaustive state recurrence.** For every even-cardinality unused-vertex set \(U\subseteq V\), let \(\mathcal M_G(U)\) be the family of perfect matchings of \(G[U]\). Set \(\mathcal M_G(\varnothing)=\{\varnothing\}\). If \(U\ne\varnothing\), choose its smallest labeled vertex \(u\). Then
\[
\mathcal M_G(U)=\bigcup_{v\in N_G(u)\cap U}
\bigl\{\{uv\}\cup M:M\in\mathcal M_G(U\setminus\{u,v\})\bigr\}.
\]
Every matching contains exactly one edge incident with \(u\), so this recurrence is exhaustive and its branches are disjoint. Implementing the recurrence at \(U=V\) yields exactly 2016 rook and 1920 Shrikhande perfect matchings. These counts can be independently reproduced by the partner-array recursion in `independent.cpp`.

**Exhaustive switch criterion.** Any permitted transition changes exactly two matching edges. Their four endpoints have exactly the two other pairings displayed above. Checking both pairings for each unordered pair of matching edges is therefore necessary and sufficient to construct *every* allowed switch, without assuming that a four-cycle is induced or facial. The complete adjacency lists in `certificate.json` are generated by this criterion, with every edge present in both directions. The enumeration yields 6048 undirected rook transitions and 5280 Shrikhande transitions.

**Complete distance computation.** For every switch-space vertex not previously assigned a component, breadth-first search produces its entire connected component. Breadth-first search from *each* vertex of each nontrivial component yields every eccentricity, hence each radius and diameter. The resulting size and distance data are:

| Graph | Component size | Multiplicity | Radius | Diameter |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Rook | 1368 | 1 | 7 | 8 |
| Rook | 288 | 2 | 14 | 14 |
| Rook | 1 | 72 | 0 | 0 |
| Shrikhande | 1908 | 1 | 10 | 13 |
| Shrikhande | 1 | 12 | 0 | 0 |

For the largest rook component, the eccentricity counts are 78 at 7 and 1290 at 8. Both 288-vertex components are three-regular and all their vertices have eccentricity 14. The Shrikhande nontrivial component has eccentricity counts 264 at 10, 1068 at 11, 288 at 12 and 288 at 13. The independent C++ implementation constructs partner arrays rather than edge-index tuples and repeats all all-source breadth-first searches, confirming these exact numbers.

**Direct count of the 72 frozen rook configurations.** Call a rook edge horizontal if it joins cells of one row and vertical if it joins cells of one column. Two horizontal matching edges in one row admit a four-cycle switch inside the row's \(K_4\); similarly for two vertical edges in one column. Consequently a frozen matching contains at most one horizontal edge per row and one vertical edge per column. Since eight edges cover 16 cells, it contains precisely one horizontal edge in each row and one vertical edge in each column. The four selected horizontal column pairs are pairwise distinct: a repeated pair in two different rows would admit a rectangle switch. They form a simple two-regular graph with four edges on four column labels, hence a four-cycle. There are exactly three such four-cycles and \(4!\) ways to assign their four edges to the rows. The four vertical edges are then uniquely forced by the uncovered cells, and their row pairs are distinct; mixed horizontal/vertical edge pairs admit no alternate complete pairing. Thus every one of these \(3\cdot4!=72\) configurations is frozen and every frozen configuration has this form. This independently checks the rook isolated-state count without relying on shortest-path computation.

## Verification

All finite claims are reproduced by `enumerate.py`, `independent.cpp`, and `verify_certificate.py`. In particular, the certificate encodes the *complete* 48-edge graphs, all 3936 perfect matchings, all switch adjacencies, and component summaries. `verify_certificate.py` checks all listed matchings, reconstructs the complete set of matchings, recomputes every possible switch, checks symmetry, and redoes breadth-first searches from every configuration. `independent.cpp` independently constructs partner-array configurations and switch transitions and returns the same state, switch-edge, component-size and eccentricity counts. The recorded run outputs are supplied alongside the sources.

To replay, use standard Python 3 and a C++17 compiler:

```
python3 enumerate.py certificate.json
python3 verify_certificate.py certificate.json
g++ -O2 -std=c++17 independent.cpp -o independent
./independent
```

The computation is finite and exhaustive under the two explicit 16-vertex graph definitions. No random sampling or timeout argument enters the proof.

## Relationship to prior work

Dyer, Jerrum and Müller studied four-cycle switches for bipartite perfect matchings and Markov-chain ergodicity (arXiv:1501.07725, originally available 2015-01-30). Dyer and Müller extended the switch-chain approach to nonbipartite graph classes (arXiv:1705.05790). Bonamy et al. formulated the general perfect-matching four-cycle reconfiguration problem and obtained complexity and tractable-class results (arXiv:1904.06184). The present result computes the entire transition-state component profiles for two specific nonbipartite strongly regular graphs; it does not follow from the cited hereditary-class statements, which do not give these graph-specific cardinalities, component sizes or distances. The graphs' common strongly regular parameters alone are insufficient to infer this reconfiguration behavior.

## Limitations

The classification is limited to these two explicitly defined graphs and switches on alternating four-cycles. It does not assert any result for arbitrary strongly regular graphs with other parameters, longer-cycle flips, Markov-chain mixing times, or distances between different connected components. The comparison with previous literature is based on inspected relevant sections, available full-text searches and mathematical-record searches; an equivalent classification in unindexed literature remains possible. The computations and supporting proof have not received an independent external mathematical audit.
