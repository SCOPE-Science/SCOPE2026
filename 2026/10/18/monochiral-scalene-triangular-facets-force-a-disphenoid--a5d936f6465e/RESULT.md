# Monochiral scalene triangular facets force a disphenoid

## Finding
Let \(T\) be a nondegenerate scalene Euclidean triangle. Among full-dimensional convex polytopes \(P\subset\mathbb R^3\) whose actual facets are congruent copies of \(T\), require that their side-length triples, read in the boundary direction induced by the outward normal, have the **same cyclic order**. Then \(P\) is necessarily a disphenoid tetrahedron. Conversely such a polytope exists precisely when \(T\) is acute. Thus the right and obtuse scalene cases of the outward-orientation-preserving version of Problem B22 have no realization.

The structural step is a vertex-link law: on any oriented polyhedral surface assembled from direct copies of one scalene triangle, an interior vertex of valence \(q\) must satisfy \(3\mid q\), and its total incident face angle is \(q\pi/3\). For convex three-polytopes this forces \(q=3\).

## Assumptions and scope
A polytope is a compact, full-dimensional convex three-polytope. A facet is a maximal two-dimensional face, not an artificial triangulation of a larger planar facet. Its consistently oriented boundary is taken counterclockwise as viewed from outside. Fix the three pairwise distinct side lengths \(a,b,c\) of \(T\), without imposing an ordering by size. The direct-only assumption means that reading side lengths cyclically along every outward-oriented triangular boundary gives a cyclic rotation of \((a,b,c)\), never a cyclic rotation of \((a,c,b)\). This precisely distinguishes direct and reflected *outward-oriented* faces for a scalene triangle. It is not the weaker statement that an ambient three-dimensional proper isometry can carry the unoriented point set of any one triangle to that of another, which would forget the chosen normal.

## Proof
**Vertex-link law.** Take an arbitrary interior vertex \(v\) of the oriented triangulated surface, and list its incident edges \(e_0,\ldots,e_{q-1}\) cyclically in the direction compatible with its oriented face sectors; indices are modulo \(q\). Along the boundary of each triangular sector at \(v\), one of these two consecutive radial edges is traversed toward \(v\) and the next away from \(v\). The same choice of which is incoming holds in all sectors: adjacent oriented triangles traverse their common edge in opposite directions.

Write \(c_i\in\{a,b,c\}\) for the length/color of \(e_i\). The direct-only condition implies that the cyclicly consecutive length pair \((c_i,c_{i+1})\) is one of \((a,b),(b,c),(c,a)\), with the order perhaps reversed **uniformly** by the initial convention for traversing the vertex link. Thus progressing by one incident triangle applies the same three-cycle to the edge colors. Starting at \(e_0\), the colors repeat every three steps and the closure \(c_q=c_0\) forces \(q=3k\) for a positive integer \(k\).

Let \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) be the angles opposite side lengths \(a,b,c\), respectively. Three consecutive sectors in the vertex link use precisely the three unordered pairs of edge lengths, and therefore precisely the three angle types \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\). This pattern repeats \(k\) times. Since \(\alpha+\beta+\gamma=\pi\), the angular sum at \(v\) is \(k\pi=q\pi/3\).

**Convexity and Euler's formula.** Every vertex of a full-dimensional convex three-polytope has a strictly positive angular defect, so its incident facet angles sum to strictly less than \(2\pi\). Accordingly \(k\pi<2\pi\) and hence \(k=1\). Every vertex therefore has exactly three incident edges. Let \(V,E,F\) count vertices, edges and triangular facets. The triangular-facet incidence equation \(3F=2E\), the three-regularity identity \(3V=2E\), and Euler's formula \(V-E+F=2\) give \(V=F=4\) and \(E=6\). Thus \(P\) is a tetrahedron.

**Metric classification and converse.** Because each scalene side length occurs once on each of the four tetrahedral faces and two facets meet along every edge, each of the three edge lengths occurs on exactly two edges. Equal-length edges cannot share a vertex (their common face would then contain that length twice), so each same-length pair is an opposite-edge pair. The tetrahedron is therefore a disphenoid. With vertices \((x,y,z),(x,-y,-z),(-x,y,-z),(-x,-y,z)\), the three opposite-pair squared lengths are \(4(y^2+z^2),4(x^2+z^2),4(x^2+y^2)\). Thus, after matching lengths, its squared coordinates are
\[
x^2=\frac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{8},\qquad y^2=\frac{c^2+a^2-b^2}{8},\qquad z^2=\frac{a^2+b^2-c^2}{8}.
\]
Nondegeneracy of this tetrahedron is equivalent to the strict positivity of all three displayed numerators, which is exactly acuteness of \(T\). Conversely, for every acute scalene \(T\) all three are positive, so the displayed four vertices construct a nondegenerate disphenoid whose facets have side lengths \(a,b,c\). Its three half-turns around the coordinate axes, together with the identity, are outward-normal-preserving proper rotations acting transitively on its four facets. Hence these facets have identical outward cyclic side-length order. This establishes both directions.

## Verification
The proof uses an exact cyclic three-color transition on vertex links, strict positive angular defect at extreme convex vertices, elementary incidence identities, and the explicit disphenoid coordinate realization; no approximation, numerical optimization, finite census or unproved auxiliary classification enters. As a separate consistency check, an oriented tetrahedron with opposite edge lengths labeled \(a,b,c\) gives outward cyclic face words \((c,a,b),(c,a,b),(a,b,c),(b,c,a)\), all direct. Substituting test side lengths \((4,5,6)\) yields positive squared box coordinates \((45/8,27/8,5/8)\), whereas the right-triangle example \((3,4,5)\) yields a zero coordinate, in agreement with the sharp boundary. The analytic proof, not these tests, establishes the universal statement.

## Relationship to prior work
Georgiou's 2026 construction proves that every nondegenerate triangle occurs as the face of a convex polyhedron when reflected copies are allowed; its Remark 3 exhibits the two outward-handedness classes among the eight faces of its construction, while its concluding remarks explicitly leave the direct-only right/obtuse scalene question open. That theorem does not imply the direct-only obstruction here. Miller (2000) studies scalene icosahedra allowing both orientations and shows that the local tricoloring at a five-valent vertex necessarily contains an oppositely oriented minority triangle; this is a narrower combinatorial analogue, not a proof for arbitrary convex triangulated polyhedra. The classical disphenoid characterization, also proved in Georgiou's Lemma 5, supplies only the tetrahedron endpoint. The new vertex-link/curvature argument rules out **every** higher-facet convex candidate, independently of its graph.

## Limitations
The assertion requires three distinct side lengths and a single **outward** cyclic orientation. It does not restrict mixtures of direct and reflected faces, does not contradict scalene convex icosamonohedra, and does not classify arrangements with both chiralities or nonconvex polyhedral surfaces. Without convexity the local three-color law remains true, but vertices of valence six (zero angular defect) or greater are not excluded. The mathematical proof has not received an independent audit.
