# Odd-base long repdigit near-perfect numbers with two prime factors

## Finding
Let \(g\ge3\) be any odd integer base, let \(t\ge3\), and let \(1\le d<g\) be a digit. Put
\[
U_t(g)=1+g+\cdots+g^{t-1},\qquad N=dU_t(g).
\]
A positive integer \(N\) is *near-perfect* if \(\sigma(N)=2N+\delta\) for some proper positive divisor \(\delta\mid N\), where \(\sigma\) is the sum of all positive divisors. Let \(\omega(N)\) count distinct prime factors.

**Theorem.** For the parameters above,
\[
N\text{ is near-perfect and }\omega(N)=2
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
(g,t,d)=(3,4,1),
\]
and the unique value is \(N=40=(1111)_3\).

## Assumptions and scope
All bases, lengths, digits, divisors and prime factors are integers. The theorem is unconditional; the base is odd and the digit nonzero, and the assertion concerns exactly two distinct prime factors, not arbitrary near-perfect integers or one- and two-digit repdigits. The proof uses the published complete classification of two-prime-factor near-perfect numbers of Ren and Chen.

## Proof
**Classification lemma (published).** Ren and Chen prove that every near-perfect integer with exactly two distinct prime factors is one of the following, or is \(40\):
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm A &:2^{p-1}(2^p-1)^2,\\
\mathrm B &:2^{2p-1}(2^p-1),\\
\mathrm C &:2^{h-1}(2^h-2^k-1),
\end{aligned}
\]
where the displayed odd cofactor is prime, \(p\) is prime in A and B, and \(1\le k<h\) in C. (The case \(k=0\) has even cofactor and does not give an odd prime.) Accordingly every candidate is even, with exactly one odd prime factor \(q\) of exponent at most two. If \(a=\nu_2(N)\), inspection of the three forms and \(40\) gives the uniform strict bound
\[
N<2^{3a+2}.
\tag{1}
\]

**Odd lengths.** If \(t\) is odd, \(U_t(g)\) is odd, so \(a=\nu_2(d)\) and \(2^a\le d<g\). For \(t\ge5\), (1) gives
\[
U_t(g)=N/d<2^{3a+2}/d\le4d^2\le4(g-1)^2<g^4<U_t(g),
\]
a contradiction. Here \(g^4>4(g-1)^2\) for odd \(g\ge3\).

For \(t=3\), \(U_3(g)=g^2+g+1>g^2\) is odd. Since \(d<g\), the unique odd prime factor \(q\) of \(N\) cannot be confined to \(d\); in fact \(U_3(g)=q^\beta\), \(\beta\in\{1,2\}\). For \(\beta=2\),
\[
(2q)^2-(2g+1)^2=3,
\]
which is impossible for \(g\ge3\) because the two positive integer factors of 3 must be 1 and 3. Thus \(U_3(g)=q>g\), and \(d=2^a\). Type B has \(q=2^p-1<2^{2p-1}=d\), while Type C has \(q=2^{a+1}-2^k-1<2d<2g<U_3(g)\). Type A requires \(\beta=2\); and \(40\) has odd part 5, smaller than \(U_3(g)\ge13\). Thus length three is impossible.

**Even lengths at least six.** Write \(t=2m\), \(m\ge3\). Then
\[
U_{2m}(g)=U_m(g)(g^m+1),\qquad \gcd(U_m(g),g^m+1)\mid2.
\]
Each factor has an odd prime divisor. If \(m\) is odd, \(U_m(g)>1\) is odd and the odd integer \((g^m+1)/(g+1)>1\) divides the second factor. If \(m\) is even, \(g^m+1\equiv2\pmod8\) and exceeds 2, while \(g^{m/2}+1\) has an odd prime divisor: for even \(m/2\) its odd quotient by 2 exceeds 1; for odd \(m/2\ge3\) its alternating quotient by \(g+1\) is odd and exceeds 1. Such an odd prime also divides \(U_m(g)\), since it divides \(g^m-1\) and is coprime to \(g-1\). The two factors therefore have *different* odd prime divisors. Along with 2 dividing \(N\), this yields at least three distinct prime factors, contrary to the hypothesis.

**Length four.** Here
\[
U_4(g)=(g+1)(g^2+1).
\]
Since \(g^2+1\equiv2\pmod8\) and exceeds 2, it contains an odd prime divisor. As \(\gcd(g+1,g^2+1)\mid2\), the factor \(g+1\) must be a power of two. Write \(g=2^s-1\), \(s\ge2\), and
\[
Q=(g^2+1)/2=2^{2s-1}-2^s+1.
\]
Then \(N=2^{s+1}dQ\), \(Q>g>d\), and \(Q=q^\beta\) with \(\beta\in\{1,2\}\). No odd prime can divide \(d\), because the only odd prime \(q\) already occurs in \(Q\) and the classification bounds its exponent by two (in the \(\beta=1\) case, \(Q=q>g>d\)). Hence \(d=2^j\), \(a=\nu_2(N)=s+1+j\ge3\).

If \(\beta=2\), we would have Type A with \(q=2^{a+1}-1>g\), contradicting \(q=\sqrt Q<g\). If \(\beta=1\), then \(q=Q\equiv1\pmod4\), so Type B is impossible because its Mersenne prime is \(3\pmod4\). For Type C, \(q=2^{a+1}-2^k-1\equiv1\pmod4\) forces \(k=1\). Thus \(Q+3=2^{a+1}\). For \(s\ge3\), however,
\[
Q+3=4(2^{2s-3}-2^{s-2}+1),
\]
with odd parenthetical factor greater than 1, so it is not a power of two. For \(s=2\), \(Q+3=8<2^{a+1}\). Consequently none of types A, B, C occurs.

The isolated classified number \(40\) has \(40=(1111)_3\), and \(\sigma(40)=90=80+10\), with \(10\mid40\) proper and \(\omega(40)=2\). For every odd \(g\ge5\), \(U_4(g)>40\); hence \((g,t,d)=(3,4,1)\) is the unique remaining triple. This proves the theorem.

## Verification
Run `python3 verify.py` using Python's standard library. It prints `VERIFY_OK` after comparing all 53 distinct classified examples generated by the parameter ranges in the script against every odd base 3 through 151 (3,975 base tests), and after independently enumerating proper-divisor sums and prime supports for all integers through 10,000. These bounded computations confirm examples only; the infinite assertion follows from the proof and the published classification theorem.

## Relationship to prior work
Ren and Chen (2013), Theorem 1.2, supply the complete near-perfect two-prime-factor classification, including the isolated number \(40\). Hasanalizade (initial public preprint 2022; journal publication 2023), Theorem 1.3, discusses repdigits for bases at most ten and asserts that all such near-perfect repdigits have fewer than three digits. The displayed exception \(40=(1111)_3\) demonstrates a missed boundary case. The present theorem supplies the exact correction in *all odd bases*, not merely a single counterexample. A separately indexed result concerns even-base repdigits, so its base hypothesis does not cover this theorem.

## Limitations
The result assumes exactly two distinct prime factors and odd bases. It does not assert a classification for other prime support sizes, other divisibility notions, or shorter repdigits. The proof depends on the established complete classification of Ren and Chen. Possible independently discovered or unindexed equivalent statements remain a literature risk; this is not an independent audit.
