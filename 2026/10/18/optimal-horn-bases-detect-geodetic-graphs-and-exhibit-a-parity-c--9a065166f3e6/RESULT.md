# Exact literal-cost Horn bases on geodetic graphs and cycles

## Finding

Let \(G=(V,E)\) be a nonempty finite connected simple graph. A set is geodesically convex if it contains every vertex on every shortest path between any two of its vertices. A basis is a set of implications \(A\to B\), \(A,B\subseteq V\), \(B\ne\varnothing\), whose models are precisely the geodesically convex sets. Its weight is \(\sum_{A\to B}(|A|+|B|)\). Let \(M(G)\) be the number of unordered nonadjacent pairs and \(\operatorname{OPT}(G)\) the minimum basis weight.

1. **Lower bound and geodetic case.** \(\operatorname{OPT}(G)\ge 3M(G)\). If \(G\) is geodetic (all shortest paths between a given pair are unique), all optimum bases are obtained by giving each nonedge \(\{u,v\}\) one singleton consequence, independently chosen from the interior of its unique geodesic. The optimum is \(3M(G)\), and there are \(\prod_{\{u,v\}\notin E}(d(u,v)-1)\) optimum bases, with the product over unordered distinct pairs.
2. **Cycle census.** On \(C_{2r+1}\) (\(r\ge1\)), the optimum weight is \(3(2r+1)(r-1)\) and the number of optimum bases is \(((r-1)!)^{2r+1}\). On \(C_{2r}\) (\(r\ge2\)), the corresponding numbers are \(2r(3r-4)\) and \(((r-1)!)^{2r}\). In the even case each nonantipodal nonedge has one singleton consequence on its shorter arc. Each antipodal pair has one rule with two consequences, one in each open semicircle. These choices are arbitrary and independent.

Geodeticity is only sufficient for equality in the universal bound. The crown example below shows why the converse fails.

## Assumptions and scope

Rules with arbitrary premise and consequent sizes are allowed. A conclusion must belong to the **iterated geodesic convex hull** of its premise, not necessarily to the union of the geodesic intervals of pairs in the premise. This difference matters even for two-element premises. Counts concern labelled vertices and set-valued rules; the two consequences of a rule are not ordered. No anti-exchange assumption is made.

## Proof

**Mandatory rules.** Empty sets, singletons and edge endpoint sets are convex, while a nonedge pair \(P\) is not. Thus some rule violated by \(P\) must have premise exactly \(P\) and at least one new consequent. These rules cost at least three per nonedge. Equality forces precisely one rule of weight three per nonedge and no other rule.

**Unique shortest paths.** When all geodesics are unique, the vertex set of any single geodesic is convex: the path between any two of its vertices is its unique subpath. The true hull of a nonedge pair is therefore precisely its geodesic. At equality its rule must choose one strict interior vertex. Conversely, given any choices, in a rule-closed set containing \(u,v\) the rule adds the chosen interior vertex \(w\). Induction on distance forces the two shorter geodesics \(u\)–\(w\) and \(w\)–\(v\), hence the entire \(u\)–\(v\) geodesic. This proves sufficiency and the product count. In an odd cycle there are \(2r+1\) pairs at each distance \(d=2,\ldots,r\), giving both formulas, including the empty products for the triangle.

**Even cycles: lower bound for arbitrary rules.** Fix antipodes \(P=\{u,v\}\) and group the rules whose premises contain \(P\) and are contained in one of its two closed semicircles. The group contains a mandatory rule for \(P\). For each of the two closed semicircles \(D\), there must be a rule in this group whose premise is contained in \(D\) and whose consequent is not contained in \(D\): otherwise \(D\) would be a model. In fact any rule with premise in \(D\) *not* containing both endpoints is valid only if its consequent stays in \(D\), since that premise is contained in a proper subarc of length less than \(r\), which is convex. A closed semicircle contains only its own antipodal pair. The groups for different antipodes are therefore disjoint, and no nonantipodal mandatory pair rule belongs to any group. Each group costs at least four: a sole weight-three rule on \(P\) cannot have a head outside both semicircles. Equality for the group forces a sole rule with premise \(P\) and one head strictly on each side. This argument includes rules with larger premises that might help an antipodal pair cross to the other side; it does not assume away such rules.

**Even cycles: attainability and count.** For each nonantipodal nonedge a shorter arc is convex, so a unit rule with a head interior to that arc is valid; induction on distance shows these rules fill all shorter arcs. Each antipodal pair rule supplies a vertex on each open semicircle. Applying the shorter-arc conclusion to the pairs from these vertices to the endpoints fills both closed semicircles. All rules are valid for geodesic convexity, and their models are convex. There are \(r\) antipodal pairs and \(M(C_{2r})=r(2r-3)\). The resulting cost is \(3M(C_{2r})+r=2r(3r-4)\), and equality in the lower bounds forces exactly the stated rule forms. There are \(2r\) pairs at each distance \(d=2,\ldots,r-1\), with \(d-1\) choices each, and \(r\) pairs at distance \(r\), with \((r-1)^2\) choices each. Their product is \(((r-1)!)^{2r}\).

**Counterexample: crowns.** Let \(H_s=K_{s,s}-\{a_i b_i: i\in\mathbb Z/s\mathbb Z\}\), the crown on two shores \(\{a_i\},\{b_i\}\), \(s\ge3\). Every matched nonedge \(\{a_i,b_i\}\) has distance three and its interval contains all vertices: for \(j\ne i\), a shortest path \(a_i,b_k,a_j,b_i\) exists with \(k\notin\{i,j\}\), and switching shores gives the corresponding paths through \(b_j\). For \(s\ge5\) two same-shore vertices have at least three common neighbors in the other shore. Every vertex of their own shore is adjacent to at least two of these common neighbors, so the hull includes the entire first shore. Every other-shore vertex then has at least two neighbors in that shore and is forced in as well. Thus the hull of every nonedge pair is the whole crown for \(s\ge5\).

Use the rules \(\{a_i,b_i\}\to\{a_{i+1}\}\) and \(\{a_i,a_{i+1}\}\to\{b_{i+1}\}\), with indices cyclic mod \(s\). For every other same-shore pair, let its single head be the missing mate of either member. (This includes every pair of \(b\)-vertices.) Every rule is valid since all nonedge hulls are full. Starting with a matched pair, the two displayed rules generate the next matched pair and iterate around the crown. Every same-shore pair first yields a matched pair. Hence a rule-closed set containing any nonedge must be full, which establishes the claimed basis at cost \(3M\). Here \(M=2\binom{s}{2}+s=s^2\).

For \(s=4\), the pair \(\{a_0,a_1\}\) has hull \(\{a_0,a_1,b_2,b_3\}\): the square is convex because the two diagonals' common-neighbor sets consist exactly of the two other vertices. At weight \(3M\) the pair could add only one of \(b_2,b_3\). The resulting triple has no other nonedge, so forward chaining stops, contradicting the full square hull. Finally \(H_3\) is \(C_6\), and the even-cycle bound is strict. Each crown is nongeodetic already on a deleted-matching pair; the case \(s=5\) alone suffices to disprove the converse.

## Relationship to prior work and limitations

Bichoupan studies literal-weight optimum bases in convex geometries; Chepoi uses graph-geodesic implicational representations for semispace questions. Convex-geometry optimum-basis results do not apply directly to general graph convexity: in \(C_4\) adding either of the other two vertices to a closed edge forces the remaining one, violating anti-exchange. The hydra framework of Sloan, Stasi and Turán covers systems sending each designated pair to the whole ground set. In fact the crown tightness for \(s\ge5\) also follows from the convex-set result known as *g-minimality* and their Proposition 3.3: the complement of a crown is Hamiltonian (traverse each shore in sequence and cross via two matching edges) and every nonedge hull of the crown is full. Crown tightness is included as an illustration, not as a distinct originality claim. The full-hull premise fails for nonantipodal nonedges of cycles of length at least five, so the hydra theorem does not give the cycle census. The result does not determine optimum weights for all nongeodetic graphs, count all optimum crown bases, or classify optima with other rule-cost conventions. No categorical priority or formal-verification claim is made.
