# Token-jump connectivity of minimum paired dominating sets in Kneser graphs of pairs

## Finding

For every integer \(n\ge 6\), the minimum paired dominating sets of \(\mathrm{KG}(n,2)\) form a connected graph under one-for-one exchanges. In particular, any two optima can be joined without ever increasing the number of selected vertices or relinquishing domination or the existence of a perfect matching in the selected induced subgraph.

## Assumptions and scope

The vertices of \(\mathrm{KG}(n,2)\) are the two-element subsets of a fixed labeled \([n]\), viewed as ground edges of \(K_n\). Adjacency means disjointness. A dominating set covers all vertices by closed neighborhoods; it is paired dominating if its induced graph admits a perfect matching. An exchange replaces one selected vertex by any unselected vertex, independently of whether the two vertices are adjacent in \(\mathrm{KG}(n,2)\). No claim is made about the slide rule or about \(n<6\).

## Proof

**The four-edge characterization.** The minimum paired dominating sets are the four-edge families containing three disjoint edges. This characterization and its static classification are shared with the result *Minimum paired domination in two-subset Kneser graphs*; a short justification is included here. A paired dominating set is total dominating, since the selected vertices also have a selected neighbor from the induced perfect matching. Thus no two-element subset of \([n]\) can cover all its ground edges. If a four-edge ground graph contains a triangle and its remaining edge meets the triangle, two ground vertices cover the graph. If the remaining edge is disjoint, the graph on the four selected vertices in \(\mathrm{KG}(n,2)\) is a claw and has no perfect matching. A feasible four-edge ground graph is therefore triangle-free, hence bipartite (there are too few edges for a longer odd cycle). Its vertex-cover number is at least three, so by König's theorem it contains a three-edge matching. Conversely, any three disjoint ground edges dominate because a pair of ground vertices can meet at most two of them. The fourth distinct edge is disjoint from at least one of the three, so pair it with that one and pair the other two with each other. Four-edge examples exist for \(n\ge6\); a two-edge candidate would consist of disjoint edges and would fail to dominate a ground edge joining one endpoint of each. Nonempty feasible sets have even size, giving the claimed minimum.

**Cliques over matching triples.** For any three-edge matching \(M\) in \(K_n\), let \(C_M\) be the set of all four-edge states containing \(M\). It is a clique because changing the additional edge uses only one exchange. The cliques cover the feasible states by the four-edge characterization. Two moves on matching triples link these cliques:

1. If \(M'\) differs from \(M\) by one edge, the four-edge state \(M\cup M'\) belongs to both cliques.
2. If \(M=\{ab,cd,ef\}\) and \(M'=\{ac,bd,ef\}\) on six distinct labels, then \(M\cup\{ac\}\) and \(M'\cup\{ab\}\) are adjacent four-edge states: the common edges are \(ab,ac,ef\), and each state contains its designated matching. Renaming labels handles either way of flipping the two edges on four labels.

**Reaching the target.** Given an initial and a target matching triple, first equalize their six-label supports. For a label \(y\) in the current support but not the target, choose a label \(x\) in the target but not the current support and replace the current edge \(yz\) by \(xz\). This is a one-edge change that preserves a matching and decreases the support difference. On the common support, to create a missing target edge \(uv\), replace the current edges \(ua,vb\) by \(uv,ab\). These four endpoints are distinct and neither replaced edge could have been a correct target edge. Repeating such flips constructs the target matching. When \(n=6\), the support is already the entire ground set, so flips alone suffice. Start with any matching triple in the initial four-edge state and end with any matching triple in the target state. The clique links above allow each triple change to be made using exchanges among feasible four-edge states, with motion within a clique to reach its required link. This joins the two states as claimed.

## Verification and relationship to related results

The proof is uniform in \(n\). The supplementary finite checks enumerate four-edge optima at \(6\le n\le10\) and run connectivity searches at \(6\le n\le9\); they do not replace the uniform construction. The shared classification and labeled census are covered by [*Minimum paired domination in two-subset Kneser graphs*](https://github.com/Resultary/2026/blob/main/2026/10/26/SCOPE-minimum-paired-domination-in-two-subset-kneser-graphs--785c37304582/RESULT.md); neither the scalar optimum nor the census is asserted as a contribution here. The assertion here is about exchanges **of the four-edge optimal states**, not exchanges of the perfect matching in the induced Kneser graph. Connectivity of matching triples under flips does not by itself say that their four-edge extensions can be joined by a single replacement; the displayed links supply this step. Related work on [total domination in Kneser graphs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15464) and [optimal paired-domination slides on triangular graphs](https://github.com/Resultary/2026/blob/main/2026/10/25/SCOPE-paired-domination-slides-of-triangular-graphs-as-square-bouquet--371270f6d358/RESULT.md) uses different feasibility or adjacency rules. No assertion of literature priority is made.
