The cyclic-rotation fixed-point criterion is proved using invariant barycentric supports and explicit directed matching orbits: an element \(r^a\) has a geometric fixed point exactly when \(\gcd(n,a)\ge2\). Both reflection types have an explicit invariant two-arrow matching. The known connectivity theorem makes \(|X_n|\) simply connected for \(n\ge6\), permitting Armstrong’s orbit-space theorem. The normal subgroups are computed for every subgroup of \(D_n\), including the prime-power boundary. For prime \(p\ge7\), freeness and the cited connectivity bound justify the Cartan–Leray computation of low integral groups. The provided verifier exhaustively checks every dihedral subgroup and fixed-orbit criterion for \(6\le n\le60\), and corroborates quotient homology for \(n=7\). Finite checks do not prove the all-parameter theorem.

## Originality — PASS, residual risk acknowledged

The closest publicly indexed 2026 note *Dihedral rational homology of bidirectional-polygon multipath complexes* already identifies reflected and rotated arrow-orbit configurations in its proof and determines rational homology characters. Those ingredients are recognized as prior; rational characters do not imply finite integral torsion. Armstrong’s 1965 general theorem supplies the quotient-fundamental-group mechanism but not the object-specific complete subgroup classification; Cartan–Leray is a standard tool, not claimed original. The new exact combination of all-subgroup normal generation, prime-power arithmetic, and unbounded low-degree integral torsion was not found in targeted checks. A short synthesis of familiar theorems may have been discovered elsewhere; inaccessible or unindexed equivalent coverage remains a serious residual risk.

Its infinite family of prime quotients has new order-\(p\) torsion in arbitrarily many positive odd degrees even though the original spaces are highly connected and have torsion-free integral homology. The result addresses structural orbit topology rather than a recomputation of rational characters or a small numerical case.
