# Scientific review

## Correctness — PASS

The one-plant and two-plant secondary-extinction events are precisely the events that a uniform \(m\)-subset of removed pollinators contains a degree-\(d\) neighborhood or the union of two such neighborhoods. Their probabilities are the displayed hypergeometric expressions. The variance formula follows from indicator covariance, and the common degree constraints give the exact intersection sum \(\binom v2\). Pascal's identity proves discrete convexity for every deletion count. The strict second difference at intersection one for \(2d-2\le m\le v-2\) proves the complete equality classification, and a finite-field projective plane attains it. The sharp double-extinction threshold follows directly from neighborhood-union sizes. Exact computational checks replay the finite examples but are not used to establish the infinite-family theorem.

## Originality — PASS, subject to bibliographic risk

Memmott, Waser, and Price (2004) formulate the random-removal coextinction process and simulate empirical ecological webs. Jones, Zurell, and Wiesner (2024) prove a **mean** robustness optimum based on plant degrees and explicitly identify secondary-extinction standard deviation as a further problem. Chabert-Liddell, Barbillon, and Donnet (2021 preprint; 2022 journal) derive variance in stochastic-block-model ecological networks, not a simultaneous exact variance optimum across fixed biregular incidence networks. The present claim controls a different statistic under fixed degrees, and a degree-preserving Fano-plane edge switch has identical expected secondary losses but a strictly larger variance. The projective-plane existence and incidence axioms themselves are established prior mathematics. An equivalent optimization in a different reliability or design-theory terminology remains a residual risk; full text of the stochastic-block-model comparison was not available.

## Mathematical value — PASS

This result solves a natural variability question not determined by an average extinction curve. It simultaneously classifies every regular-web variance minimizer across all random deletion stages, establishes a sharp first-multiple-loss protection threshold, and gives explicit strictly positive variance gaps. It therefore distinguishes ecological networks with identical mean robustness but different risks of clustered secondary extinctions. Its regularity and binary interaction assumptions materially limit biological interpretation.

## Limitations and closest sources

The rule is strict all-partners-lost extinction without rewiring or feedback, and the probability space is uniform fixed-count deletion rather than an ecological time model. The closest mathematical-ecology sources are Memmott et al. (2004), Jones et al. (2024), and Chabert-Liddell et al. (2021/2022). See RESULT.md for complete scope, comparisons, and references.
