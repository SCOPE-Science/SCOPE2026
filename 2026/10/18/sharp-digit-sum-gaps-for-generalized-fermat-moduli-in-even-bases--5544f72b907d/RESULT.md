# Sharp digit-sum gaps for generalized Fermat moduli in even bases

## Finding

For an **even** integer base \(b\ge2\) and an integer exponent \(r\ge2\), let \(m=b^r+1\). For every positive integer \(N\), write \(s_b(N)\) for the sum of its ordinary base-\(b\) digits. Then the full spectrum is
\[
\boxed{\{s_b(km): k\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\}=\mathbb Z_{>0}\setminus\{1,3,5,\ldots,b-1\}.}
\]
Consequently its Frobenius number (largest omitted positive digit sum) is exactly \(b-1\), the total number of omitted positive digit sums is \(b/2\), and its natural density is \(1\). Neither the exceptional set nor the Frobenius number depends on \(r\).

For instance, the attainable decimal digit sums of positive multiples of \(101\), \(1001\), and \(10001\) are **each** exactly \(2,4,6,8,10,11,12,13,\ldots\): the positive exceptions are \(1,3,5,7,9\). These moduli exemplify different parities of \(r\), not a claim that their sets of multiples coincide.

## Assumptions and scope

All bases in this theorem are even integers \(b\ge2\); all exponents satisfy \(r\ge2\). The input multiples \(k\) are positive integers. The digit sum is the usual nonnegative digit sum in positional base \(b\), with no leading zeros required. The formula is an equality of sets of **positive digit-sum values**; it makes no assertion about the number or size of multiples having a given digit sum.

The exponent-one case is deliberately excluded from the new theorem: it belongs to the previously established spectrum of multiples of \(b+1\). The odd-base case is also excluded rather than claimed as a new contribution.

## Proof

**Signed short-relation lemma.** Fix \(b\ge2\), \(r\ge1\), and \(m=b^r+1\). Any positive multiple \(N\) of \(m\) with \(s_b(N)\le b\) must have **even** digit sum.

Indeed, write \(N=\sum_{j\ge0}d_jb^j\), where \(0\le d_j<b\) and only finitely many digits are nonzero, and put \(s=\sum_jd_j\). For every \(i\in\{0,\ldots,r-1\}\), define
\[
 u_i=\sum_{j\equiv i\pmod{2r}}d_j,\qquad
 v_i=\sum_{j\equiv i+r\pmod{2r}}d_j,\qquad c_i=u_i-v_i.
\]
Because \(b^r\equiv-1\pmod m\), divisibility of \(N\) implies
\[
 \sum_{i=0}^{r-1}c_i b^i\equiv0\pmod m,
 \qquad \sum_{i=0}^{r-1}|c_i|\le s\le b.
\]
If any \(|c_i|=b\), then all other coefficients vanish, and the integer on the left has absolute value \(b^{i+1}\le b^r<m\), contradicting divisibility by \(m\). Otherwise all \(|c_i|\le b-1\), whence
\[
 \left|\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}c_ib^i\right|
 \le(b-1)\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}b^i=b^r-1<m.
\]
The congruence therefore gives the integer equality \(\sum_i c_i b^i=0\). Reduction modulo \(b\) shows \(c_0=0\), since \(|c_0|<b\); division by \(b\) and induction yield \(c_i=0\) for every \(i\). Finally,
\[
 s=\sum_i(u_i+v_i)\equiv\sum_i(u_i-v_i)
 =\sum_i c_i=0\pmod2.
\]
This proves the lemma. In particular, when \(b\) is even no odd digit sum less than \(b+1\) can occur.

**Existence of the two generators.** Set \(m=1+b^r\); its ordinary base-\(b\) expansion has exactly two digits equal to one, so \(s_b(m)=2\). Define
\[
 W_{b,r}=1+\sum_{j=0}^{b-1}b^{r-1+2rj}.
\]
Because \(r\ge2\), all listed exponents are distinct positive integers, and the separate initial exponent \(0\) occurs only once. Thus \(W_{b,r}\) has \(b+1\) digits equal to one and \(s_b(W_{b,r})=b+1\). Moreover, \(b^{2r}\equiv1\pmod m\), so
\[
 W_{b,r}\equiv1+b\,b^{r-1}=1+b^r=0\pmod m.
\]
Both \(2\) and \(b+1\) are therefore attainable digit sums of positive multiples of \(m\).

**Closure and exhaustion.** If \(m\mid X\) and \(m\mid Y\) for positive integers \(X,Y\), placing their base-\(b\) numerals in two disjoint digit blocks gives a positive integer \(Z=Xb^t+Y\), where \(t\) is larger than the number of digits of \(Y\), such that \(m\mid Z\) and \(s_b(Z)=s_b(X)+s_b(Y)\). Every positive linear combination \(2u+(b+1)v\) with \(u,v\ge0\), \(u+v>0\), is consequently attained. Since \(b\) is even, \(b+1\) is odd. The positive semigroup generated by \(2\) and \(b+1\) consists precisely of all even positive integers and all odd integers at least \(b+1\). The short-relation lemma excludes the remaining odd integers \(1,3,\ldots,b-1\), completing the proof. \(\square\)

## Verification

The standalone Python 3 program `verify.py` performs two logically independent finite checks. First, it computes *complete* finite-state reachability for positive base-\(b\) numerals with every digit-sum weight up to \(2b+7\) in each tested base/exponent pair; arbitrary numeral length is handled by a finite graph, not by imposing a length cutoff. Second, it independently constructs the displayed witnesses, checks exact divisibility and digit sums, and tests no-carry concatenation.

Executed command: `python3 verify.py`. Recorded output: `VERIFY_OK: exact finite automata for 35 (base, exponent) settings; constructive witnesses for 325 settings/sums, all checked exactly`. Finite checks test examples and cannot replace the unbounded symbolic proof above.

## Relationship to prior work

Phunphayap, Noppakaew, and Pongsriiam [1, Theorems 1–3 and Questions 2 and 4] establish general restrictions and cofiniteness for attainable digit sums. Their Question 4 asks for Frobenius numbers of cofinite digit-sum output sets; their separate Question 2 concerns the distinct non-coprime regime. Neither gives the present uniform formula for \(b^r+1\). Here \(\gcd(b-1,b^r+1)=1\) because \(b\) is even, so their cofiniteness result applies; the exact missing values and conductor do not follow from it.

Clokie, Lidbetter, Molina Lovett, Shallit, and Witzman [2, Theorems 3–4] show how digit-sum feasibility reduces to sums of distinct powers and how an attainable digit sum leads to shifts by \(b-1\). Those results explain the available digit-sum generators, but do not rule out the entire short odd interval for this infinite family. The signed short-relation lemma gives the sharp exclusion.

The case \(r=1\) belongs to an existing explicit classification for multiples of \(b+1\), and is **not claimed here**. The even-exponent subfamily \(r=2,4,6,\ldots\) cannot be obtained from that case by divisor inclusion, because \(\gcd(b+1,b^r+1)=1\) for even \(b\) and even \(r\). No stronger equivalent published formula was identified in the inspected material; that is evidence-limited, not an absolute priority assertion.

## Limitations

This result concerns the set of possible digit sums, not the distribution of multiples, shortest witness length, or extension to arbitrary divisors or arbitrary moduli. It is valid for all even \(b\ge2\) and all \(r\ge2\), without assuming that \(b^r+1\) is prime. Overlapping unindexed treatments of digit sums of generalized Fermat numbers remain possible. Independent mathematical validation is not asserted.
