# Scientific review

**Correctness — PASS.** The state variable \((r,c)\) is invariant under the remaining bipartite labels, and the three transition multiplicities sum to \(r^2-c\). A separate moment calculation shows that the potential \(2c/[r(r+1)]\) differs from a martingale by an explicitly negative drift. The factorial-second-moment contraction bounds the sum of these losses by \(O(\log n/n^2)\) for every \(n\ge2\). Two independent exact combinatorial enumerators agree on small orders; finite checks supplement rather than replace the proof.

**Originality — PASS with residual access risk.** Specific searches for edge-random greedy matchings of crown graphs, random sequential dimer adsorption on a complete bipartite graph with a missing diagonal, and an asymptotic unmatched-pair probability revealed no matching exact theorem. The closest general studies by Dyer–Frieze and by Krivelevich et al. investigate performance of random greedy matchings/independent sets without giving this dense-family rare-defect distribution. McGregor's 2026 method addresses a different algorithm. Complete full-text coverage of the 1991 and 2026 articles was unavailable and remains a risk; the accessible abstracts do not assert the present exact claim. Other indexed findings involving crown graphs, chain graphs, and random greedy matching concern different output statistics or graph families.

**Value — PASS.** The crown graph is the one-factor-deleted dense bipartite graph, where a maximal matching can miss optimality by only one edge. The probability of that event is a natural performance invariant of greedy matching. The exact finite recurrence and sharp leading constant \(2\) quantitatively resolve a rare-event question for an infinite graph family, beyond worst-case approximation and matching-density bounds.

**Scope and limitations.** Only uniform available-edge greedy matching, not vertex-first or edge-addition processes, is covered. An unindexed equivalent result or a covering result in the inaccessible full texts cannot be ruled out. The bound on the second-order error is not claimed sharp.
