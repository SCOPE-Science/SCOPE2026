# Scientific review

**Correctness — PASS.** The model, comparators and strict sign-switch convention are explicit. The hypergeometric expectation follows by absence probabilities and linearity of expectation. Positive binomial denominators allow wholly exact integer comparison. The complete partition recursion covers every labeled-species abundance type up to relabeling; all unordered pairs for every \(N\le16\) were exhausted, and the independently generated histogram partition sets agree. Exactly two boundary pairs occur at \(N=16\). A separate explicit enumeration of all \(65{,}536\) labeled subsets for each boundary profile checks their richness profiles without the hypergeometric formula. Risk: the theorem is a finite discrete-sample classification only; interpolation and other sampling models are outside its quantifiers.

**Originality — PASS, with residual bibliographic risk.** Gotelli and Colwell (2001) already state that individual-based rarefaction curves can cross twice; that phenomenon is not claimed as new. Lande, DeVries and Walla (2000) analyze crossing of expected species-accumulation curves and an endpoint Simpson criterion, largely using a with-replacement formula. The new finite statement is the first possible equal census size of \(16\) for two strict reversals at integer sample sizes **without** replacement, together with exactly two unordered boundary partition pairs. Those sharp conclusions do not follow from the cited crossing existence or formula. The relevant full text of Gotelli and Colwell and accessible author-uploaded text of Lande et al. were compared. No matching exact-size classification was identified in targeted literature searches, indexed-result searches, or accessible local claim records; unsuccessful searches cannot prove uniqueness. The Heck (1975) and Chao et al. (2014) full texts were not completely inspected, leaving a residual bibliographic risk.

**Value — PASS.** Rank instability of rarefaction curves directly complicates ecological diversity comparisons at a fixed effort. This exact minimum gives a natural finite complexity cutoff for the already-recognized *double* crossing phenomenon, and the complete two-pair boundary classification explains the unusual abundance patterns needed to realize it: a six-species profile with four singletons versus a five-species profile with four doubletons. The result is intrinsically about a natural exact minimal invariant, not a randomly chosen partial census. It does not claim clinical, conservation-policy, or inferential significance for sample sizes of \(16\) in field studies.
