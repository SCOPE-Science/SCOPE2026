# Spinor coefficient zero sets and projective rigidity across finite binary polyhedral groups

## Finding
Let \(G\) be any finite nonabelian subgroup of \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\), with its defining irreducible complex representation \(\rho\). For arbitrary nonzero vectors \(u,v\in\mathbb C^2\), put \(f_{u,v}(g)=\langle u,\rho(g)v\rangle\). Its rank-weighted nonabelian Fourier support is exactly \(\sigma(f_{u,v})=2\).

The exact zero set \(Z(f)=\{g\in G:f(g)=0\}\) is precisely one of: the empty set; one central antipodal pair \(\{g,-g\}\); or a left coset \(gC\) of a maximal cyclic subgroup \(C\le G\) with \(|C|>2\). Conversely, **every** such subset is attained. For each prescribed coset \(gC\) with \(|C|>2\), precisely two projective rank-one matrix-coefficient functions have that zero set, modulo multiplication by \(\mathbb C^\times\). By contrast, each antipodal-pair zero set is attained by infinitely many projective coefficient functions, and an open dense family has no zeros on \(G\).

The exact zero-set incidence census is:

| Group | Group order | Sizes and numbers of nonempty zero sets | Number of distinct zero sets | Number of projective coefficients whose zero set has size at least four |
|---|---:|---|---:|---:|
| \(\mathrm{Dic}_n\), \(n\ge2\) | \(4n\) | size \(2\): \(2n\); size \(4\): \(n^2\); size \(2n\): \(2\) | \(n^2+2n+3\) | \(2(n^2+2)\) |
| \(2T\) | 24 | size 2: 12; size 4: 18; size 6: 16 | 47 | 68 |
| \(2O\) | 48 | size 2: 24; size 4: 72; size 6: 32; size 8: 18 | 147 | 244 |
| \(2I\) | 120 | size 2: 60; size 4: 450; size 6: 200; size 10: 72 | 783 | 1444 |

An empty zero set contributes one additional class to every row's count. For \(\mathrm{Dic}_2\), the two displayed size-four categories coincide in cardinality and their counts **add**: six sets of size four. More generally, when a displayed size repeats, combine its counts. The attainable spatial-support sizes at Fourier rank-support two are exactly the complements of the displayed zero sizes and the empty size: \(\{4n,4n-2,4n-4,2n\}\) for \(\mathrm{Dic}_n\), \(\{24,22,20,18\}\) for \(2T\), \(\{48,46,44,42,40\}\) for \(2O\), and \(\{120,118,116,114,110\}\) for \(2I\).

## Assumptions and scope
The group \(G\) is a nonabelian finite subgroup of \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\), so by the classical finite-subgroup classification it is a dicyclic group \(\mathrm{Dic}_n\), \(n\ge2\), or one of \(2T,2O,2I\). Its center contains \(-I\), so all zeros come in antipodal pairs. The coefficient \(f_{u,v}\) is complex-valued; no reality, positivity or character-centrality restriction is assumed. The Fourier transform uses \(\widehat f(\pi)=|G|^{-1}\sum_{g\in G}f(g)\pi(g)^*\) and \(\sigma(f)=\sum_{\pi\in\widehat G}(\dim\pi)\operatorname{rank}\widehat f(\pi)\). Only the defining two-dimensional irreducible representation and its rank-one coefficients are classified, not all Fourier-rank-two functions from other blocks.

## Proof
**1. Rank-weighted Fourier support.** A nonabelian finite subgroup of \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) acts irreducibly on \(\mathbb C^2\); an invariant complex line would give a simultaneous unitary diagonalization and force commutativity. Matrix-coefficient Schur orthogonality makes every Fourier block of \(f_{u,v}\) vanish except the defining block, which is a nonzero rank-one operator. Consequently \(\sigma(f_{u,v})=2\).

**2. The zero-plane lemma.** Identify \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) with unit quaternions \(S^3\subset\mathbb H\), using right multiplication by a fixed quaternionic unit \(i\) as the complex structure on \(\mathbb H\cong\mathbb C^2\). For nonzero \(v\), quaternionic right multiplication \(q\mapsto qv\) is an invertible real-linear map. The complex orthogonal complement of the line of \(u\) is a quaternionic real two-plane \(w\mathbb C_i\). Therefore the complex coefficient equation \(\langle u,qv\rangle=0\) cuts out a real two-plane \(P=w\mathbb C_i v^{-1}=a\mathbb C_J\), where \(J=v i v^{-1}\) is a unit imaginary quaternion. Conversely any real two-plane through zero is of the form \(a\mathbb C_J\) for suitable unit \(a\) and imaginary \(J\), hence arises from some nonzero rank-one coefficient. Intersecting the plane with \(S^3\) gives a great circle.

**3. Circle intersections with finite subgroups.** If \(P\cap G\) is nonempty, select \(g\in P\cap G\). Then \(P\cap S^3=g U_J\), where \(U_J=\{\cos\theta+J\sin\theta:\theta\in\mathbb R\}\) is a circle subgroup of unit quaternions. Thus \(P\cap G=g(G\cap U_J)\). Every finite subgroup of a circle is cyclic and contains \(\{\pm1\}\). If its order is two, this is exactly one antipodal pair. If its order exceeds two, its noncentral generator fixes the axis \(\mathbb R J\) uniquely, so \(G\cap U_J\) is a maximal cyclic subgroup of \(G\). Conversely any maximal cyclic subgroup of order greater than two is the entire intersection of \(G\) with its containing circle; its left cosets occur as intersections with left-translated real two-planes. To obtain precisely a central antipodal pair \(\{\pm g\}\), choose a plane through \(g\) and \(-g\) avoiding the finitely many other elements; to obtain the empty set, choose a generic plane avoiding all elements. This proves exact realizability, not just upper bounds.

**4. Projective two-ray rigidity.** Identify a coefficient with its nonzero rank-one Fourier matrix in \(M_2(\mathbb C)\), modulo nonzero scalar. For the zero set \(gC\), first translate \(g\) to identity, then unitary-conjugate an element \(h\in C\setminus\{\pm I\}\) to \(\mathrm{diag}(\lambda,\lambda^{-1})\) with \(\lambda\ne\pm1\). Writing a general coefficient as \(F_A(x)=\operatorname{tr}(A\rho(x))\), the two equations \(F_A(I)=F_A(h)=0\) force both diagonal entries of \(A\) to be zero. Thus \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&b\\c&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), and the rank-one condition is \(bc=0\). Its projectivization consists of exactly two points, represented by the two off-diagonal matrix units. Both vanish on exactly \(gC\). For a specified antipodal pair, the single complex linear constraint on rank-one \(A\) defines an irreducible one-complex-dimensional curve; only finitely many of its points acquire further zeros, so infinitely many have exactly the prescribed pair. Avoiding the finite union of nontrivial coefficient hyperplane sections gives an open dense family with empty zero set.

**5. Explicit finite-subgroup census.** Each noncentral rotation axis corresponds to one maximal cyclic subgroup. For \(\mathrm{Dic}_n\) there is one order-\(2n\) cyclic axis and \(n\) order-four axes. For \(2T\) the axis census is four of order six and three of order four. For \(2O\) it is three of order eight, four of order six, and six of order four. For \(2I\) it is six of order ten, ten of order six, and fifteen of order four. These counts follow from the well-known rotation-axis orbits of the rotational dihedral, tetrahedral, octahedral and icosahedral groups (respectively, the lifts of twofold, threefold, fourfold and fivefold axes). Every cyclic subgroup of order \(m>2\) contributes exactly \(|G|/m\) distinct left cosets; cosets from distinct maximal cyclic subgroups cannot coincide. There are exactly \(|G|/2\) distinct antipodal pairs. Counting them with the single empty set produces the table. The two coefficient rays associated with every coset produce its last column. Every spatial-support size is \(|G|-|Z(f)|\), as asserted.

## Verification
The standalone standard-library script `verify_spinor_coefficients.py` performs exact arithmetic in \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)\) or \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)\) for explicit unit-quaternion lists of all \(24,48,120\) exceptional group elements. It verifies norms, uniqueness, antipodal-axis incidence and all exceptional axis counts. It independently enumerates cyclic subgroups and their cosets of \(\mathrm{Dic}_n\) for each \(2\le n\le12\). Successful output contains `PASS exact finite axis and coset certificates`. These finite computations check the group-count input; the infinite dicyclic and all-coefficient assertions rest on the algebraic proof above, not on experiments.

## Relationship to prior work
Alagic and Russell (2006) establish the rank-weighted nonabelian Fourier uncertainty inequality, giving only \(|\operatorname{supp}f|\,\sigma(f)\ge|G|\), not the complete rank-one coefficient support or projective-fibre diagram. Standard binary-polyhedral classification gives the relevant axis orders but does not itself formulate coefficient zero-set rigidity. Importantly, Bao and collaborators (2010) already tabulate the \(450,200,72\) rank-two flats of the \(H_4\) root-system matroid: **the three numerical \(2I\) circle-section multiplicities are covered by that earlier geometric result and are not presented as independent new enumerative data**. The contribution under examination is the uniform coefficient-zero characterization for every binary polyhedral family, its exact support interpretation and projective two-point coefficient fibres. The connection to earlier matroid flats is explicit, not suppressed.

## Limitations
This is a rank-one theorem inside the defining spin block, not a full uncertainty profile for arbitrary functions on any of the groups. The spectrum excludes coefficients from other irreducible blocks. No assertion of independent verification or independent originality certification is made. A pre-existing abstract theorem connecting spinor coefficient hyperplanes to real root-matroid flats could subsume the geometric aspect; locating every possible equivalent formulation in older spinor or root-system literature remains a residual originality risk.
