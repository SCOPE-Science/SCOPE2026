# Ten-face curvature bottleneck for congruent isosceles triangles

## Finding

**Theorem.** Let \(P\subset\mathbb R^3\) be a full-dimensional convex polyhedron with exactly ten facets, each congruent to a fixed non-equilateral isosceles triangle \(T\). Write \(\theta\in(0,\pi)\) for the angle between the two equal sides of \(T\). Every vertex \(v\) of \(P\) has angular defect \(\kappa(v)=2\pi-\sum_{F\ni v}\angle_F(v)>0\). Then \(\theta<\pi/2\), and at least one vertex obeys

\[
0<\kappa(v)\le 2\pi-4\theta.
\]

In particular, no such ten-faced convex polyhedron exists when \(T\) is right or obtuse isosceles. For acute \(T\), the inequality is a necessary curvature bottleneck, not a realizability assertion.

## Assumptions and scope

The body is three-dimensional, convex, bounded, and has precisely ten genuine, maximal two-dimensional facets; each facet is a nondegenerate triangle congruent to \(T\) under any rigid motion, reflections permitted. The apex angle \(\theta\) is opposite the unique unequal-length base edge. The equilateral case is excluded because no base-edge class is distinguished. The vertex curvature uses the *actual* facet angles (no triangulation by auxiliary diagonals). Neither nonconvex immersed triangulations nor noncongruent isosceles facets are covered.

## Proof

Let \(F=10\) be the number of facets. Every facet has three edges, each edge belongs to precisely two facets, and Euler's formula gives
\[
E=\frac{3F}{2}=15,\qquad V=E-F+2=7.
\]
Each triangular facet has exactly one edge of the unequal length \(b\) and two edges of the common length \(a\). As edges belong to exactly two facets, there are precisely five \(b\)-edges and ten \(a\)-edges.

Delete the five \(b\)-edges from the embedded edge graph on the spherical boundary of \(P\). Each deleted edge merges its two adjacent triangles into one topological quadrilateral. Each triangle contains exactly one deleted edge, so the five merges are disjoint at the level of face incidence; each resulting face has four distinct boundary vertices. The remaining connected embedded graph \(Q\) therefore has seven vertices, ten edges, and five quadrilateral faces. Connectedness follows because either endpoint of each deleted edge remains connected by the other two edges of either original triangle.

Every facial walk of \(Q\) has even length. Because the graph is embedded cellularly on the sphere, its cycle space over \(\mathbb F_2\) is generated by facial boundaries. Hence every cycle has even length and \(Q\) is bipartite. Let \(X\sqcup Y\) be its bipartition, with \(|X|\le |Y|\). As \(V=7\), we have \(|X|\le3\). Each of the ten edges of \(Q\) has one endpoint in \(X\), so
\[
\sum_{x\in X}\deg_Q(x)=10.
\]
Consequently there is a vertex \(v\in X\) incident to at least four \(a\)-edges. Write \(r=\deg_Q(v)\ge4\) and let \(k\) be its number of incident deleted \(b\)-edges. Each \(b\)-edge is a diagonal of a quadrilateral of \(Q\), so its endpoints are in the same bipartition class of \(Q\). Since \(P\)'s edge graph is simple and \(|X|\le3\), one has \(0\le k\le2\).

Put \(\alpha=(\pi-\theta)/2\), the common base angle of \(T\). Each of the \(k\) incident \(b\)-edges is shared by two facets, contributing two base angles \(\alpha\) at \(v\). There are \(r+k\) incident facets in total (one for each edge in the cyclic vertex link), so the remaining \(r-k\) facet angles at \(v\) equal \(\theta\). Thus the exact angle sum at \(v\) is
\[
S(v)=2k\alpha+(r-k)\theta=k\pi+(r-2k)\theta.
\]
We know \(r\ge4\), \(k\le2\), hence \(r-2k\ge0\).

If \(\theta\ge\pi/2\), then
\[
S(v)\ge k\pi+(r-2k)\frac\pi2=\frac{r\pi}{2}\ge2\pi,
\]
contradicting the strict convex-vertex inequality \(S(v)<2\pi\).

If instead \(0<\theta<\pi/2\), then
\[
S(v)\ge k\pi+(4-2k)\theta
=4\theta+2k\left(\frac\pi2-\theta\right)\ge4\theta.
\]
It follows that \(\kappa(v)=2\pi-S(v)\le2\pi-4\theta\), completing the proof. \(\square\)

## Verification

All edge and face counts follow from \(3F=2E\) and Euler's formula; none depend on computer enumeration of triangulations. The graph \(Q\) is a five-face quadrangulation, whose bipartition forces a vertex with at least four common-length edges. The deleted unequal-edge diagonals join vertices of the same bipartition class, giving \(k\le2\). Substitution of \(\alpha=(\pi-\theta)/2\) in the angle sum verifies the threshold and curvature estimate. These calculations constitute a finite symbolic verification of the key counting identities. No probabilistic experiment, solver timeout, or finite sample is used as a substitute for the universal proof.

## Relationship to prior work

Georgiou, *Congruent Triangular Faces, Reflections Allowed* (arXiv:2608.01109, first public 2026-08-02), establishes that any triangle is realizable as eight congruent facets and determines the minimum face count; Section 4 explicitly leaves the attainable nonminimal counts unclassified. In particular, its six-facet argument uses the uniquely occurring base-length edges in a triangular bipyramid, whereas the theorem here rules out the ten-facet case through a bipartite quadrangulation and a seven-vertex degree obstruction.

Eppstein, *On Polyhedral Realization with Isosceles Triangles* (Graphs and Combinatorics 37 (2021), DOI:10.1007/s00373-021-02314-9), studies convex congruent-isosceles facet realizability, including pairing of base angles, local vertex restrictions, and bounds for large numbers of faces. Those results provide important background but no ten-facet nonexistence statement or this curvature estimate was identified in its accessible full article. No general originality certificate is asserted.

## Limitations

The theorem is only a necessary constraint for ten genuine facets. It does not classify which acute isosceles triangles admit ten-facet realizations, nor whether any scalene right/obtuse triangle does. The quantitative curvature bound is not claimed to be sharp or attained. Historical-priority uncertainty remains, including older unindexed treatments of monohedral polyhedra; absence of a matching search result is not proof of novelty. Independent audit has not been performed.
