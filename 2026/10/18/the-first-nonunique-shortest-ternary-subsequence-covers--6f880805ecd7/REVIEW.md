# Scientific review: first nonunique shortest ternary subsequence covers

**Correctness — PASS.** The full theorem concerns finite words over three specified symbols, including words with smaller support. The equivalence between subsequence coverage and left/right greedy embedding capacities is proved with an explicit condition for every text position. Closure of s-covers under composition restricts shortest candidates to s-primitive words; adjacent-repeat and factor-extension arguments allow exhaustive enumeration of all 147 primitive candidates, with none at length nine. Direct exhaustion of every ternary text of length at most eleven gives counts zero through ten and six at eleven. The script independently compares the greedy predicate against direct embedding enumeration in 127530 cases and explicitly rules out shorter covers for all six extremizers. The finite loops, not an experimental extrapolation, establish the quantified cutoff.

**Originality — PASS within examined evidence.** The 2025 Crochemore–Lecroq–Rytter problem collection describes subsequence covers and says nonunique shortest covers exist without specifying a minimal ternary example. The expanded *Subsequence Covers of Words* by Charalampopoulos et al., Sections 3 and 7, provides the known ternary s-primitive length bound eight and a four-symbol length-fifteen nonuniqueness example, but not the present ternary length-eleven minimum or six-word classification. Its SPIRE 2022 antecedent is recognized; the field's definitions, algorithms, and primitive bound are not newly claimed. Searches for equal-length ternary cover ambiguity and its exact witnesses yielded no equivalent statement. Risks: an unindexed classification or uninspected conference appendix may contain an equivalent result, and negative database searches cannot certify global priority.

**Value — PASS.** The original work treats multiplicity of shortest subsequence covers as a notable structural difference from ordinary covers. The classification settles the first possible ambiguity over the smallest alphabet able to exhibit it (the binary case is unique), reduces the known four-symbol witness to a three-symbol one, determines the exact shortest length eleven, and classifies every labeled extremizer. This is a natural sharp finite boundary with a complete reproducible certificate, not an arbitrary isolated computation.

**Limits.** The theorem is limited to the least ambiguity length and its six extremizers. It says nothing about the count or growth of ambiguous words at larger lengths. The review is not independent of the proof authoring process, and no external validation has been conducted.
