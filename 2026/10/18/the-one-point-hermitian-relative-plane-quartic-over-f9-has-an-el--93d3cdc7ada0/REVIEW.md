# Scientific review: the one-point Hermitian-relative quartic

## Correctness
PASS. The curve is smooth by its three partial derivatives, hence of genus three. For each of the first three extension degrees, the reproducible exact finite-field enumeration counts all affine points and all projective points on the line at infinity, with an independent enumeration agreeing: \(1,109,892\). Newton identities uniquely determine the first three Weil coefficients, and the genus-three Weil functional equation determines the other three. Exact multiplication verifies \((1-3T+9T^2)^3\). Counting the seven \(\mathbb F_3\)-points of the displayed elliptic curve proves its trace is \(-3\), and the base-changed trace is \(3\); Tate's theorem yields the isogeny. The finite check is not being generalized directly to arbitrary extensions.

## Originality
PASS, subject to literature-indexing risk. Homma–Kim (2026), Theorem 1.1, proves the one-point classification, not its complete Weil numerator. Meagher–Top (2010) develops a general method for genus-three twists; the concrete Dyck–Fermat tables in Proposition 18 explicitly assume characteristic at least five and do not yield the stated characteristic-three coefficients without additional calculation. Top (2003) treats maximal genus-three rational-point counts rather than the zeta function of this minimal-point model. Exact-equation, polynomial, elliptic-cube, and analogous twist searches disclosed no prior exact computation. These are noncoverage comparisons, not a proof that no unindexed result exists.

## Mathematical value
PASS. This quartic is the explicit extremal one-point member singled out by the published Hermitian-relative classification, not an arbitrary sample. Its entire extension-field point-count sequence and finite-field isogeny class are natural arithmetic invariants absent from the source's rational-point classification. The perfect-cube Frobenius polynomial identifies a concrete elliptic power decomposition, valuable for distinguishing arithmetic structures that the single rational-point count cannot determine.
