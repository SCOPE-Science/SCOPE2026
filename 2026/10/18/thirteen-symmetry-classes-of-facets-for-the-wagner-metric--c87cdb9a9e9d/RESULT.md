# Thirteen symmetry classes of facets for the Wagner metric

## Finding
Let \(W\) be the Wagner graph, with vertices \(\mathbb Z/8\mathbb Z\) and edges joining \(i\) to \(i+1\) and to \(i+4\), indices taken modulo eight. Give its vertices the unit-edge shortest-path metric. The seven-dimensional real Lipschitz-free unit ball of this metric has **exactly 222 facets**. Under the 16 dihedral graph symmetries, together with the antipodal action on supporting functionals, these facets form **exactly thirteen orbits**.

Choose an integer representative of each supporting functional with minimum value zero and write its values around the eight-cycle as an eight-digit word. Here is the complete list of orbit representatives. The last column counts edges where the potential difference has absolute value one.

## Assumptions and scope
All scalars are real. Vertices are numbered \(0,\ldots,7\). The free-space unit ball is identified with the symmetric edge polytope \(P=\operatorname{conv}\{\pm(e_u-e_v):uv\in E(W)\}\) in the seven-dimensional zero-sum subspace. Its polar, after quotienting potentials by constants, consists of functions \(f\) satisfying \(|f(u)-f(v)|\le1\) for every edge. The unit shortest-path metric gives the same ball: a nonedge molecule divided by its distance is a convex combination of directed edge molecules along a shortest path.

We classify facets of \(P\), equivalently extreme points of this polar Lipschitz ball. The claimed symmetry group is the explicit action generated by \(i\mapsto i+1\), \(i\mapsto-i\), and \(f\mapsto-f\) modulo additive constants. It has 32 transformations on potentials. No assertion about further non-obvious polytope automorphisms is needed.

## Proof
**Facet criterion.** Fix \(f(0)=0\). The polar is the intersection of the 24 inequalities \(-1\le f(u)-f(v)\le1\), one pair per graph edge. At any feasible point, the rows of its active constraints are the signed incidence vectors of the tight edges \(T_f=\{uv:|f(u)-f(v)|=1\}\). They have rank seven precisely when the tight-edge subgraph is connected and spanning. Thus \(f\) is a vertex precisely when \(T_f\) is connected and spanning. In particular, choose a spanning tree of tight edges: its edge differences are \(\pm1\), so every vertex potential has integral differences and is integral after fixing one vertex to zero.

**Range reduction.** Every pair of vertices of \(W\) has graph distance at most two: differences one, two, three, and four along the outer eight-cycle can be bridged in respectively one, two, two, and one edges, using the opposite chords. Hence every feasible integral potential, after subtracting its minimum, takes values in \(\{0,1,2\}\). There are only \(3^8\) words to inspect. A normalized word gives a distinct facet if and only if it satisfies all twelve edge inequalities and its tight graph connects all eight vertices. This is a complete finite criterion; no heuristic search or vertex-number cutoff is involved.

**Orbit enumeration.** For each retained word, apply the 32 transformations \(i\mapsto si+t\), where \(s\in\{-1,1\}\), \(0\le t<8\), followed optionally by negation of every potential value; subtract the resulting minimum. These transformations preserve the graph edges, feasibility, tight connectivity, and facet status. The standalone exact checker enumerates all \(3^8\) words; tests the edge inequalities and tight-edge connectivity using integer union-find operations; constructs the orbit of each of the thirteen displayed representatives; and verifies that their disjoint orbits cover the retained set. Its orbit sizes sum to 222. The same checker independently enumerates all integer potentials with \(f(0)=0\) and entries in \([-2,2]\), checks the criterion, and obtains exactly the same normalized facet set. All enumerations are complete over the proved finite domain. This establishes the orbit list and all stated distributions.

## Verification
Run `python3 checker.py` in a directory containing the accompanying standalone program. It uses only the Python standard library and terminates with `PASS`. The checks include all 6,561 normalized ternary candidates, the independently normalized potential search, the thirteen pairwise disjoint orbits, their cardinalities, and both displayed spectra. The bound from graph diameter proves the finite search exhaustive for this mathematical problem; the code is part of the finite certificate and not evidence for a claim about an unbounded graph family.

## Relationship to prior work
D’Alì, Delucchi, and Michałek connect symmetric edge polytopes to Lipschitz-free and Kantorovich–Rubinstein geometry, and describe facet labels by edge differences and connected tight graphs. Braun and Bruegge investigate facet counts of sparse graphs, and tabulate *extremal* numbers of facets over graph classes specified by vertex and edge counts; their family formulas concern graphs with fewer independent cycles or different constructions. Neither inspected work gives a thirteen-orbit classification for the Wagner graph. The characterization of tight potentials is therefore prior machinery; the finite Wagner-specific classification and its exact orbit and tight-edge distributions are the claim here. Public aggregate tabulations of eight-vertex graph facets may already imply the scalar count 222 for a graph if the underlying data are available, but do not themselves classify these thirteen symmetry types.

## Limitations
This is a single natural finite metric, not a formula for all Möbius ladders. It does not establish the complete face vector, affine symmetry group of the polytope, or independent external validation. The general tight-edge criterion is established prior work; the newly supplied content is the exact graph-specific symmetry census. Exhaustive arithmetic verification of the finite list does not establish novelty in every possible unpublished or unindexed dataset.
