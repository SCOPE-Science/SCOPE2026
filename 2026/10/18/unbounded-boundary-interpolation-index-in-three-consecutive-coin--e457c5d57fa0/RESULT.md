# Unbounded boundary-interpolation index in three consecutive coin equations

## Finding
For every pair of integers \(k\ge2\) and \(1\le r\le k-1\), set
\[
a=2k+1,\qquad b=2k+2,\qquad c=2k+3,\qquad N=a(k+3+r)+1.
\]
Then the full set of nonnegative integer solutions of \(ax+by+cz=N\) is
\[
\{C\}\cup\{D_j:0\le j\le r+1\},\quad
C=(k+2+r,1,0),\quad D_j=(j,2(r+1-j),k-r+j).
\]
In particular, there are exactly \(r+3\) solutions, including exactly three coordinate-boundary solutions \(C,D_0,D_{r+1}\), one in the relative interior of each coordinate edge of the positive-octant triangle. These three boundary solutions are affinely independent; the sublattice generated by their differences has index exactly \(r+1\) in the full integer homogeneous-solution lattice. For \(1\le j\le r\), the minimal positive denominator of an affine expression
\[
D_j=\frac{u C+vD_0+wD_{r+1}}{m},\qquad
u,v,w\in\mathbb Z,\quad m=u+v+w\in\mathbb Z_{>0},
\]
is \((r+1)/\gcd(j,r+1)\). The point \(D_1\) requires denominator \(r+1\). Thus these denominators are unbounded over the family despite there being exactly one integer solution on each coordinate edge.

## Assumptions and scope
All parameters and variables are integers, with \(k\ge2\), \(1\le r\le k-1\), and \(x,y,z\ge0\). The three denominations are consecutive, hence have greatest common divisor one. A boundary solution means an integer solution having at least one zero coordinate; an interior solution has all three coordinates positive. Normalization denominators are positive integers; coefficients in the numerator may be negative. The index is the finite subgroup index of two rank-two integer lattices in the same real plane.

## Proof
Write \(T=x+y+z\) and \(s=k+3+r-T\). Because \(b=a+1\) and \(c=a+2\), the equation becomes
\[
y+2z=as+1.
\]
Nonnegativity implies \(0\le y+2z\le2T=2(k+3+r-s)\). Since \(a\ge5\), the first inequality forces \(s\ge0\). The second implies
\[
(a+2)s\le2(k+3+r)-1\le4k+3<2(a+2).
\]
Therefore \(s=0\) or \(s=1\), with no other possibilities.

If \(s=0\), the relation \(y+2z=1\) gives \(y=1,z=0\), and \(T=k+3+r\) then gives \(x=k+2+r\), which is \(C\). If \(s=1\), then \(T=k+2+r\) and \(y+2z=2k+2\). Writing \(j=x\) yields \(z=k-r+j\) and \(y=2(r+1-j)\). Both are nonnegative precisely when \(0\le j\le r+1\), because \(k-r\ge1\). This gives every \(D_j\), proves completeness and shows all the displayed points are distinct.

The only zero coordinate among \(D_j\) occurs at \(x=0\) for \(j=0\), or at \(y=0\) for \(j=r+1\); every \(D_j\) has \(z\ge1\). The point \(C\) has \(z=0\), while its other coordinates are positive. For \(1\le j\le r\), all coordinates of \(D_j\) are positive. Thus exactly three boundary solutions occur, one on each edge.

Let \(L=\{(u,v,w)\in\mathbb Z^3:au+bv+cw=0\}\). Its projection onto the first two coordinates consists of \((u,v)\in\mathbb Z^2\) satisfying \(au+bv\equiv0\pmod c\). Because \(\gcd(a,b,c)=1\), the corresponding homomorphism \(\mathbb Z^2\to\mathbb Z/c\mathbb Z\) is surjective; hence the projected lattice has index \(c\) in \(\mathbb Z^2\). The projected difference vectors of the three boundary solutions, choosing \(D_0\) as origin, are
\[
(r+1,-2(r+1))\quad\text{and}\quad(k+2+r,-2r-1).
\]
Their determinant is \((r+1)(2k+3)=(r+1)c\ne0\), so the three points are affinely independent and their difference lattice has index exactly \(r+1\) in \(L\).

Finally, \(D_j=(1-j/(r+1))D_0+(j/(r+1))D_{r+1}\). Since \(C,D_0,D_{r+1}\) are affinely independent, these rational affine coefficients are unique. An expression with integer numerators and positive total denominator \(m\) is possible precisely when \(m j/(r+1)\) is an integer, so its smallest denominator is \((r+1)/\gcd(j,r+1)\). At \(j=1\) this equals \(r+1\), which becomes arbitrarily large as \(r\) increases (take \(k=r+1\)). This completes the proof.

## Verification
Run `python verify.py` using only Python's standard library. An independent double loop exhaustively computes all nonnegative solutions for 741 admissible parameter pairs \(2\le k\le39\), including 9,880 positive interior solutions. It compares sets with the theorem, checks exact boundary sets and projected determinants, and checks each predicted rational combination and denominator. The accompanying `verification_output.txt` contains its actual output. Finite computation is only corroborative; the two-case argument and lattice-index proof establish all parameter values.

For example, at \(k=3,r=2\), the denominations are \(7,8,9\) and the target is \(57\); the boundary solutions are \((7,1,0)\), \((0,6,1)\), and \((3,0,4)\), while the interior solutions are \((1,4,2)\) and \((2,2,3)\). The second requires an affine-normalization denominator divisible by three.

## Relationship to prior work
He, Shiue, and Venkat [1] study three-variable Frobenius equations using coordinate-boundary solutions. Their §3 considers both integer affine combinations (coefficient sum one) and more general normalized combinations with unrestricted nonzero coefficient sum. The present family is compatible with the latter approach: every interior solution is a rational combination of two boundary solutions. The new sharp result is that the necessary denominator of this reconstruction is unbounded even with one boundary solution on each edge. It does *not* refute their unrestricted-denominator formulation.

Binner [2] already disproved a boundary-reconstruction conjecture/consequence using a different, cardinality-based example. This theorem is **not** presented as a first counterexample to that conjecture; it instead supplies exact saturation indices and a uniform family of particularly sparse, nondegenerate boundary configurations. Binner's general restricted-partition formula [3] counts all solutions for given coefficients; its counting statement does not contain the present uniform sharp denominator law.

## Limitations
This is a special infinite family of consecutive denominations and specially positioned target values; no universal classification of three-variable Frobenius fibers is claimed. The theorem does not prove or disprove the unrestricted rational-interpolation conjecture. A published but unlocated equivalent saturation-index calculation is a possible bibliographic risk. Same-author or independent validation has not been performed.
