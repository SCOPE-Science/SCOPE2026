# Walsh rigidity of optimal strong-majority edge colourings of hypercubes

## Finding

For every integer \(d\ge2\), let \(Q_d\) be the Boolean \(d\)-cube, with vertex set \(\{0,1\}^{d}\) and edges joining vertices differing in one coordinate. In a **strong-majority edge colouring**, each edge has at most half of its adjacent edges in each individual colour; adjacency means sharing a vertex with that edge. Colours are not required to be proper. Write \(\operatorname{Maj}'(G)\) for the minimum number of colours.

**Theorem.** The strong-majority edge index is \(\operatorname{Maj}'(Q_d)=2\). Moreover, for a fixed pair of named colours, identified with \(\{+1,-1\}\), there are **exactly \(2^d\)** strong-majority two-colourings. All are given uniquely by \(d\) independent signs \(\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_d\in\{+1,-1\}\): the edge in direction \(i\), with either endpoint \(x=(x_1,\ldots,x_d)\), has sign
\[
 c(\{x,x\oplus e_i\})=\varepsilon_i(-1)^{\sum_{j\ne i}x_j}.
\]
The expression is independent of the chosen endpoint. Up to graph automorphisms **preserving the two named colours**, these colourings form one orbit when \(d\) is even, and exactly two orbits when \(d\) is odd, distinguished by \(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\varepsilon_i\in\{+1,-1\}\). If global exchange of the two colours is also allowed, there is one orbit in every dimension.

## Assumptions and scope

The graphs are finite, simple, and undirected. The dimension satisfies \(d\ge2\). Every edge of \(Q_d\) has \(2(d-1)\) adjacent edges. The count \(2^d\) uses a fixed coordinate labeling and two **named** colours (a colouring and its global colour swap are distinct). The orbit statements use the full automorphism group of the uncoloured cube. The isolated edge \(Q_1\) has minimum colour count one and is excluded from the main theorem.

For every \(S\subseteq[d]\), write \(\chi_S(x)=(-1)^{\sum_{j\in S}x_j}\). Set \(\widehat i=[d]\setminus\{i\}\). We use the standard Walsh basis of real-valued functions on the Boolean cube.

## Proof

**Step 1: turn local balancing into an eigenvalue equation.** Encode the two colours as signs \(\pm1\). Since each edge has exactly \(2(d-1)\) adjacent edges, the strong-majority condition with two colours is equivalent to having exactly \(d-1\) adjacent edges of each sign, or equivalently a zero signed sum over the edge neighbourhood.

For a direction \(i\), write \(s_i(x)\) for the sign of \(\{x,x\oplus e_i\}\); this satisfies \(s_i(x)=s_i(x\oplus e_i)\). Define the sum of signs incident with a vertex by \(T(x)=\sum_{i=1}^d s_i(x)\). The signed sum over edges adjacent to the edge in direction \(i\) is exactly
\[
 T(x)+T(x\oplus e_i)-2s_i(x).
\]
Therefore local balance is equivalent to
\[
 T(x)+T(x\oplus e_i)=2s_i(x)\quad\text{for every }x,i. \tag{1}
\]
Summing (1) over \(i\) and using \(\sum_i s_i(x)=T(x)\) gives
\[
 \sum_{i=1}^{d}T(x\oplus e_i)=(2-d)T(x). \tag{2}
\]

**Step 2: exploit Walsh diagonalisation.** For the adjacency operator \(A\) of \(Q_d\), each Walsh character is an eigenfunction:
\[
 A\chi_S=(d-2|S|)\chi_S.
\]
The \(2^d\) characters form a basis, so (2) forces \(T\) to be supported entirely on the degree-\(d-1\) characters. Hence there are unique real coefficients \(a_1,\ldots,a_d\) such that
\[
 T(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_j\chi_{\widehat j}(x). \tag{3}
\]
For a fixed \(i\), the character \(\chi_{\widehat j}\) is unchanged under flipping coordinate \(i\) precisely when \(j=i\); it changes sign otherwise. Substituting (3) into (1) yields
\[
 s_i(x)=\tfrac12\bigl(T(x)+T(x\oplus e_i)\bigr)=a_i\chi_{\widehat i}(x). \tag{4}
\]
Since all actual edge signs belong to \(\{+1,-1\}\) and the characters are also signs, (4) implies \(a_i\in\{+1,-1\}\) for each \(i\). This proves that **every** valid two-colouring belongs to the stated family, with \(\varepsilon_i=a_i\). Conversely, for any selection of these \(d\) signs, define \(s_i\) by (4). Its vertex sum is exactly the right side of (3), and substituting back shows (1), hence all the required local balance equalities. Different coefficient tuples produce different edge colourings. There are therefore exactly \(2^d\) of them.

Each edge has a nonempty edge neighbourhood for \(d\ge2\), so a one-colour assignment cannot satisfy the strong-majority condition. The constructed two-colour assignments exist, proving \(\operatorname{Maj}'(Q_d)=2\).

**Step 3: classify automorphism orbits.** The automorphism group of \(Q_d\) is generated by coordinate permutations and translations \(x\mapsto x\oplus a\) with \(a\in\{0,1\}^d\). This follows, for example, by fixing the image of \(0\), then permuting its \(d\) neighbours; all other images are determined inductively by Hamming distance and common-neighbour incidence.

A coordinate permutation permutes the parameters \(\varepsilon_i\). A translation by \(a\) sends the parameter in direction \(i\) to
\[
 \varepsilon'_i=\varepsilon_i(-1)^{\sum_{j\ne i}a_j}. \tag{5}
\]
Writing signs as binary exponents, this is the linear map on \(\mathbb F_2^d\) with matrix \(J+I\), where \(J\) is all ones. A vector in its kernel has every coordinate equal to its coordinate sum. If \(d\) is even, this forces the zero vector; thus translations act transitively on all \(2^d\) parameter tuples. If \(d\) is odd, the kernel consists of the zero vector and the all-ones vector, and the image is exactly the even-parity subspace. Equivalently, translations can make precisely those sign changes that preserve \(\prod_i\varepsilon_i\). Coordinate permutations also preserve the product, yielding exactly two orbits in odd dimension. Global exchange of colours sends all \(\varepsilon_i\) to \(-\varepsilon_i\); in odd dimension this interchanges the two orbits, while in even dimension there was already just one. This completes the classification.

## Verification

The accompanying standalone program `verify_hypercube.py` generates cube edges and their adjacent edge lists directly from the bit-string definition. It exhausts **every** two-colour edge assignment on \(Q_2\) and \(Q_3\), finding respectively \(4\) and \(8\) feasible colourings and checking exact equality with the predicted signed families. It also checks every displayed pattern and the translation-orbit counts for \(2\le d\le8\). The finite tests are independently useful consistency checks; the unrestricted theorem and orbit statement are established by the preceding symbolic argument rather than by finite enumeration.

To reproduce with Python 3, run `python verify_hypercube.py` from the directory containing the program. There are no third-party dependencies.

## Relationship to prior work

Kalinowski, Kamyczura, Pilśniak and Woźniak introduced strong-majority edge colouring and established general bounds and results for selected graph classes. Antoniuk, Prorok and Salia improved the general colour bounds, and Pękała and Przybyło showed that three colours suffice for all graphs of minimum degree at least five. These results provide context, but a three-colour upper bound does not determine an exact two-colour classification or the number of such colourings. The earlier two-colour examples for cycles cover only the special case \(Q_2\), not higher-dimensional cubes. The present argument identifies **all** optimally balanced sign assignments and their orbit types, not merely an existence construction.

The theorem may also be formulated as a classification of \(\{\pm1\}\)-valued nullvectors of the adjacency matrix of the line graph \(L(Q_d)\). The ordinary zero-eigenspace is \(d\)-dimensional, but its intersection with the discrete sign cube is exactly the explicit set of \(2^d\) vectors. This full sign-vector classification is not implied by the eigenvalue multiplicity alone.

## Limitations

The result applies to two named colours and the ordinary binary hypercube only. It does not classify strong-majority colourings that use three or more colours, general Hamming graphs, or arbitrary Cartesian products. The literature comparison covered accessible primary papers and focused index searches; an unindexed equivalent description of line-graph sign eigenvectors cannot be excluded. Independent expert assessment has not been performed.
