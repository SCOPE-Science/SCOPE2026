# Seven-frequency positive Fourier designs on prime cycles: the three eleven-point optimizer orbits

## Finding
Let \(p\ge7\) be prime, and sample the four consecutive sites \(A=\{0,1,2,3\}\subset\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z\). A positive Fourier design consists of distinct frequencies \(\Lambda\subset\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z\) and strictly positive real weights \(w_\lambda\), such that the weighted restricted characters form a Parseval frame on \(\mathbb C^A\) (counting inner product). Then the **minimum possible number of frequencies is exactly seven for every prime \(p\ge7\)**.

More particularly, for \(p=11\), exactly **33 of the \(\binom{11}{7}=330\) seven-frequency supports** admit such weights. They split into exactly **three translation/reflection orbits**, each of size eleven, with representatives
\[
\{0,1,2,3,5,7,9\},\qquad
\{0,1,2,4,6,7,9\},\qquad
\{0,1,3,4,6,7,9\}.
\]
On each feasible support, the normalized strictly positive weights are unique. The same statement applies to any translate of the four consecutive spatial sites or any nonzero common step modulo \(p\).

## Assumptions and scope
Put \(\zeta_p=e^{2\pi i/p}\) and take the vector \(v_\lambda=(\zeta_p^{a\lambda})_{a=0}^{3}\). Parseval tightness means
\[
\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}w_\lambda v_\lambda v_\lambda^*=I_4.
\]
The diagonal normalization is \(\sum_{\lambda}w_\lambda=1\); the distinct off-diagonal conditions are
\[
\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}w_\lambda\zeta_p^{r\lambda}=0,
\qquad r=1,2,3.
\tag{1}
\]
The corresponding negative-frequency moment conditions follow from real weights. Supports are *unordered* seven-element subsets. Frequency translation \(\lambda\mapsto\lambda+a\) and reflection \(\lambda\mapsto-\lambda+a\) preserve feasibility, with weights transported accordingly. No equal-weight restriction is imposed.

## Proof
**Step 1: universal lower bound on prime cycles.** Equation (1) says that the (complex) discrete Fourier transform of the nonzero weight vector has at least six zeros, at \(\pm1,\pm2,\pm3\), which are distinct modulo \(p\ge7\). If the weight support had \(m\le6\) points, select any \(m\) of these six zero-frequency rows. Chebotarev's nonvanishing-minors theorem for the prime-order Fourier matrix makes that \(m\times m\) submatrix invertible, contradicting the nonzero weights. Hence \(|\Lambda|\ge7\). This uses the established Chebotarev theorem, not an unproved numerical assertion.

**Step 2: existence for every prime.** The \(p\) vectors
\[
\Phi(\lambda)=\big(\cos(2\pi\lambda/p),\sin(2\pi\lambda/p),
\cos(4\pi\lambda/p),\sin(4\pi\lambda/p),
\cos(6\pi\lambda/p),\sin(6\pi\lambda/p)\big)\in\mathbb R^6
\]
have uniform average zero. The origin therefore belongs to their convex hull. Carathéodory's theorem supplies a convex representation on at most seven vectors. Discard zero weights; Step 1 forces exactly seven remaining vectors, all with positive weights. This proves the sharp seven-frequency law.

**Step 3: unique weights on any specified seven frequencies.** Write their distinct points on the circle as \(z_1,\ldots,z_7\) and define Lagrange interpolation polynomials
\[
L_j(X)=\prod_{k\ne j}\frac{X-z_k}{z_j-z_k}.
\]
For every polynomial \(Q\) of degree at most six, interpolation gives \([X^3]Q=\sum_j Q(z_j)[X^3]L_j\). In particular, the unique solution of all seven real/complex moment equations with exponents \(-3,\ldots,3\) is
\[
\boxed{w_j=z_j^3[X^3]L_j(X)
=-\frac{z_j^3\,e_3(z_1,\ldots,\widehat z_j,\ldots,z_7)}
{\prod_{k\ne j}(z_j-z_k)}}.
\tag{2}
\]
Here \(e_3\) is the third elementary symmetric polynomial in the six indicated points, and the hat means omission. The equations \(\sum_jw_jz_j^r=\mathbf1_{\{r=0\}}\) hold for every \(-3\le r\le3\) by the coefficient identity. Because this system is invariant under conjugation of all equations and its Vandermonde matrix is invertible, every \(w_j\) is real. Thus a support works exactly when the seven explicit numbers (2) are positive, and its weights are necessarily unique.

**Step 4: the eleven-point orbit census.** At \(p=11\) the dihedral action generated by frequency translations and reflection partitions the 330 seven-element supports into exactly twenty orbits. Burnside's formula verifies this independently: the identity fixes 330 supports, ten nontrivial rotations fix none, and each of eleven reflections fixes \(\binom53=10\) supports (one fixed point plus five transposed pairs). Thus the orbit count is \((330+11\cdot10)/22=20\).

## Relationship to prior work
Bownik and Yu (2026) study near-tight discretization of continuous frames and exponential systems; they do not count exact positive Fourier designs on four consecutive points of a finite prime cycle. The published *Sharp positive Fourier sampling on three consecutive cyclic sites* establishes the corresponding sharp size law for \(\{0,1,2\}\), and explicitly leaves longer progressions outside its scope. This is the closest directly comparable exact design statement; its three-site theorem does not give the eleven-point optimizer census here.

The frameworks of Dutkay–Ranasinghe and Frederick–Mayeli provide weighted or finite Fourier frame context without this sharp four-site orbit count. Chebotarev's Fourier-minor theorem and Carathéodory's convex-hull theorem already imply the prime-order seven-node **cardinality alone when combined**, so no standalone priority is asserted for that elementary structural deduction. The additional exact three-orbit, 33-support classification and the strict sign certificates are the substantive new part. We found no checked source proving or dominating that finite optimizer census.

## Limitations
This theorem classifies all *minimum-size* positive rules for the four consecutive spatial sites only at prime eleven, not for arbitrary spatial patterns or composite cycle orders. For general primes \(p\ge7\), it identifies the exact minimum size and supplies formula (2) as a support test, not a closed combinatorial enumeration of every optimizer. The exhaustive eleven-point classification relies on the included, self-contained rational-interval checker; its proof has been reproduced here but not independently audited. Historical priority against unindexed quadrature tables remains a residual uncertainty.
