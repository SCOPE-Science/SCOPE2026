# A parity phase transition in the state complexity of commutative closures of dihedral identity languages

## Finding
For every integer \(n\ge2\), let \(D_n\) denote the dihedral group of order \(2n\), generated by rotation \(a\) and reflection \(b\), with relations \(a^n=b^2=1\) and \(bab=a^{-1}\). Let \(W_n\subseteq\{a,b\}^*\) comprise the positive words evaluating to the group identity, and write \(K_n=\operatorname{perm}(W_n)\) for its commutative closure (all permutations of the letters of words of \(W_n\)). Then the number of states of its minimal complete DFA is
\[
\operatorname{sc}(K_n)=
\begin{cases}
4,&n=2,\\
n+4,&n\ge4\text{ is even},\\
4n-1,&n\ge3\text{ is odd}.
\end{cases}
\]
The original identity language has exactly \(2n\) states. In particular, commutative closure increases this complexity for odd \(n\ge3\), decreases it for even \(n\ge6\), and leaves it unchanged for \(n\in\{2,4\}\).

## Assumptions and scope
A **complete** DFA has a defined transition on every state and letter. The state complexity is the number of states in the minimal such deterministic automaton, including its initial state. Both generators are interpreted as positive letters; no inverse letters are included. The group \(D_n\) contains pairs \((k,e)\) with \(k\in\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z\) and \(e\in\{0,1\}\), representing \(a^kb^e\), and product \((k,e)(\ell,f)=(k+(-1)^e\ell,e+f\bmod 2)\). For \(n=2\) this is the Klein four-group with the stated presentation.

Write \(x=|w|_a\) and \(y=|w|_b\), with \(x,y\) arbitrary nonnegative integers. Membership in \(K_n\) depends only on \((x,y)\). No claim is made for different generating sets or languages accepting nonidentity group elements.

## Proof
**1. Exact Parikh criterion.** Every word with an odd number \(y\) of reflections has odd reflection parity and cannot be the identity. If \(y=0\), its value is \(a^x\), hence identity exactly when \(n\mid x\). Suppose that \(y\ge2\) is even. Moving rotation letters among the \(y+1\) gaps around reflections yields a rotation exponent congruent to \(x-2j\pmod n\), with \(j\in\{0,1,\ldots,x\}\): each \(j\) is obtained by placing \(j\) copies of \(a\) between the first two copies of \(b\), the other \(x-j\) copies before them, and the remaining reflections in adjacent pairs. Conversely, every arrangement has \(j\) rotations after an odd number of reflections, so is covered by this formula. The group identity is attained iff \(2j\equiv x\pmod n\) for some \(0\le j\le x\). For even \(x\), choose \(j=x/2\). For odd \(x\) and even \(n\), parity makes this impossible. For odd \(x\) and odd \(n\), the smallest nonnegative solution of \(2j\equiv x\pmod n\) is \(j=(x+n)/2\) when \(x<n\), which exceeds \(x\); if \(x\ge n\), that same candidate lies between zero and \(x\). Thus, for all \(x,y\ge0\),
\[
(x,y)\in\Psi(K_n)\iff
\begin{cases}
n\mid x,& y=0,\\
\text{false},& y\text{ odd},\\
x\text{ even},& y>0\text{ even and }n\text{ even},\\
x\text{ even or }x\ge n,& y>0\text{ even and }n\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
\tag{1}
\]
Here \(\Psi\) denotes the letter-count map.

**2. Right quotients are precisely shifted Parikh predicates.** A prefix containing \(x\) copies of \(a\) and \(y\) copies of \(b\) has right quotient determined by the boolean function on suffix counts \((u,v)\mapsto\mathbf1_{K_n}(x+u,y+v)\). This follows directly from commutativity of \(K_n\) and is an exact characterization, not a bound. Every nonnegative count pair occurs as a prefix, so all such quotients are reachable. By the Myhill–Nerode theorem, counting different shifted predicates gives the minimal complete-DFA state number.

**3. Even dihedral parameter, \(n\ge4\).** If \(y=0\), equation (1) implies that the quotient depends only on \(x\bmod n\), giving at most \(n\) types. If \(y>0\), it depends only on \((y\bmod2,x\bmod2)\), giving at most four additional types. All \(n+4\) types are distinct. Among the \(y=0\) types, two different residues are separated by a suffix made of copies of \(a\): choose its count so exactly one accumulated count is divisible by \(n\). For fixed positive \(y\)-parity, opposite \(x\)-parities are separated by a suffix with the necessary \(b\)-parity and a suitable \(a\)-count; opposite \(y\)-parities are separated by taking the suffix \(b\)-count so one yields positive even parity and the other odd parity, and then choosing an \(a\)-count of accepting parity. Every \(y=0\) state differs from every positive-odd-\(y\) state by a suffix \(a^u\) making \(x+u\) divisible by \(n\): the former accepts, the latter never does. Every \(y=0\) state differs from every positive-even-\(y\) state as follows. If the prefix rotation counts have the same parity, choose a suffix \(a^u\) making the no-reflection count congruent to \(2\pmod n\); it is rejected at the no-reflection state but accepted at the positive-even state. If they have opposite parities, choose a suffix \(a^u\) making the no-reflection count divisible by \(n\); it is accepted at the no-reflection state but rejected at the positive-even state. These arguments use \(n\ge4\) and even. Hence \(n+4\) is exact.

**4. Exceptional parameter \(n=2\).** Now (1) reduces to simultaneous evenness of \(x\) and \(y\), independent of whether \(y=0\). There are precisely four distinct shifted parity predicates, giving \(\operatorname{sc}(K_2)=4\).

**5. Odd dihedral parameter, \(n\ge3\).** For a prefix with \(y>0\), equation (1) says the quotient depends only on \(y\bmod2\) and \(t=\min(x,n-1)\). Indeed, when \(y+v\) is positive even, its acceptance is \(x+u\) even or \(x+u\ge n\), equivalently to \(x+u\) even or \(x+u\ge n-1\), because \(n-1\) is even. Therefore precisely \(2n\) candidates arise, one for each choice of \(y\)-parity and \(t\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\). They are mutually distinct: at fixed parity, for \(0\le t<s\le n-1\), take an \(a\)-only suffix of length \(n-2-t\) if the resulting positive \(y\)-count is even, or append one \(b\) first if it is odd. The smaller accumulated rotation count becomes \(n-2\), an odd rejected count, while the larger is at least \(n-1\), always accepted. Opposite \(y\)-parities are separated by suffixes of suitable \(a\)-count and zero-or-one \(b\)-count, making one resulting reflection count even with accepting rotation count and the other odd.

For \(y=0\), the shifted predicate is indexed by \(x\) itself if \(x\le n-2\), and by \(x\bmod n\) if \(x\ge n-1\). Thus there are at most \((n-1)+n=2n-1\) types. Distinct residues are separated by a pure-\(a\) suffix ensuring exactly one total is divisible by \(n\). A low-count type \(x\le n-2\) is not equivalent to a high-count type with the same residue: add \(a^u b^2\), choosing \(u=0\) if \(x\) is odd and \(u=1\) otherwise. The low accumulated count is odd and at most \(n-2\), hence rejected; the high accumulated count is at least \(n\), hence accepted. In particular all \(2n-1\) candidate types differ.

A \(y=0\) state differs from any positive-odd-\(y\) state by an \(a\)-only suffix making its rotation total divisible by \(n\), while positive-odd states always reject such suffixes. A \(y=0\) state differs from any positive-even-\(y\) state by a sufficiently long \(a\)-only suffix with rotation total not divisible by \(n\): the \(y=0\) state rejects, whereas the positive-even state accepts because its rotation count then exceeds \(n\). Summing the disjoint reachable families gives \((2n-1)+2n=4n-1\) states.

**6. Comparison with the noncommutative language.** The usual Cayley DFA for \(W_n\) has all \(2n\) group elements as reachable states. If two group elements differ, multiply by a positive word representing the inverse of the first element: finite generator orders guarantee a positive representative. Exactly one of the two resulting products is the identity, so the states have different right languages. Thus \(\operatorname{sc}(W_n)=2n\) and all the asserted parity comparisons follow.

## Verification
Run `python verify.py` using only the Python standard library. The program computes the group product sets over all words with fixed counts directly from the multiplication law and compares them to (1) in \(4{,}445\) test cases for \(2\le n\le30\). Independently it generates the quotient automaton, marks distinguishable pairs by a reverse product-graph search, and counts minimization classes using union-find. It checks the asserted exact state counts for every \(n\) from \(2\) to \(30\), and tests the DFA language on \(11{,}747\) explicit words. Successful execution prints `VERIFY_OK`. These finite checks are corroboration only: the all-\(n\) proof is in sections 1–6, not inferred from the experiments.

## Relationship to prior work
Hoffmann (2020a) establishes general state-complexity upper bounds for commutative closures of group languages, including a product bound in terms of original states and generator orders; the checked general theorem and examples do not give these sharp dihedral-family counts. Hoffmann (2020b) establishes regularity of closures built from shuffle expressions over group languages; it likewise does not imply the present piecewise formula. Cano Gómez, Guaiana, and Pin (2013) study partial commutations and include dihedral groups as algebraic examples; their group-language calculations concern other acceptance predicates and do not determine these identity-language quotients. The theorem here fixes the dihedral rotation/reflection generating set and exposes the sharp dependence on the parity of \(n\), including the direction of the state-complexity change.

## Limitations
The result is restricted to the positive generator alphabet \(\{a,b\}\), identity acceptance, commutative closure, and complete deterministic automata. It does not cover alternative generating sets, arbitrary group languages, nondeterministic state complexity, or arbitrary finite groups. The cited literature comparison cannot exclude equivalent results in unindexed or otherwise inaccessible sources. Computation checks a finite range only; its purpose is to detect errors rather than certify the universal statement.
