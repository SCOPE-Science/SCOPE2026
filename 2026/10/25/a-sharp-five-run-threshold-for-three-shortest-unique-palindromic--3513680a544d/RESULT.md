# A sharp five-run threshold for three shortest unique palindromic substrings

## Finding

For a finite nonempty binary word \(w\), write \(r(w)\) for its number of maximal constant-symbol runs. A palindrome occurrence \(w[i..j]\) is *unique* if its factor occurs at no other starting position. For a nonempty query interval \(I=[s,t]\), a shortest unique palindromic substring (SUPS) is a unique palindromic occurrence containing \(I\) whose length is minimal among all unique palindromic occurrences containing \(I\). Denote the number of its distinct positional answers by \(\alpha_w(I)\), with zero answers when there is no eligible occurrence.

**Theorem.** For every finite binary word and every nonempty query interval,
\[
 r(w)\le4\ \Longrightarrow\ \alpha_w(I)\le2,
 \qquad r(w)\le5\ \Longrightarrow\ \alpha_w(I)\le3.
\]
The first occurrence of three answers among words with at most five runs is at length fourteen. Up to reversal and exchange of the letters, the unique minimum-length example is
\[
 w_*=0^7 1 0^3 1 0^2=\texttt{00000001000100}.
\]
For its point query \(I=[7,7]\), the three answers are the length-seven occurrences at positions \([1,7]\), \([5,11]\), and \([7,13]\): respectively \(0^7\), \(0^3 1 0^3\), and \(0 1 0^3 1 0\). All four words generated by reversal and binary complementation, and no others of length fourteen with at most five runs, admit a three-answer interval.

Moreover an unbounded family of five-run witnesses is
\[
 W_{b,e}=0^{b+2e+2}1^b0^{2e-b}1^b0^e
 \quad(b\ge1,\ e\ge b+1).
\]
For \(a=b+2e+2\), the point query \([a,a]\) has exactly three SUPSs, all of length \(a\), with factor strings
\[
 0^a,\qquad 0^{e+1}1^b0^{e+1},\qquad
 0 1^b0^{2e-b}1^b0.
\]
Thus five is the least run count for three SUPS answers, and fourteen is the least witness length within the at-most-five-run class. In particular a binary word admitting four answers, if any, must have at least six runs.

## Assumptions and scope

Intervals and factor positions are inclusive and one-based. A factor is counted by its *occurrence*, not merely its character string; uniqueness refers to occurrence multiplicity in the entire word. Both letters may be interchanged, and words or intervals may be reflected, without changing the SUPS count. No condition is imposed on the lengths of the runs, except positivity. The upper bounds apply to **all** query intervals, including intervals with several positions, not only point queries. The example classification at length fourteen is restricted to words with at most five runs. This theorem does not settle the maximum number of SUPSs for arbitrary binary words.

## Proof

**1. Centers of unique palindromes.** Number the runs of \(w\) consecutively. Every palindromic occurrence is centered within a run. Indeed, an even palindrome has two equal central letters, and an odd palindrome has one central letter; neither can have its center at a transition between unequal symbols. A monochromatic factor of length \(h\) occurring inside a run of length \(m\) is unique only when \(h=m\), because \(h<m\) gives at least two distinct occurrences within that run. A mixed-symbol palindrome centered in a run must contain its entire central run and equal-length neighboring portions, so its center is precisely the midpoint of that run. Consequently, for any prescribed length, there is at most one unique palindromic occurrence centered in each run. In particular, all SUPSs for one query interval have distinct run centers.

For at most two runs this directly bounds the number of SUPSs by two. At three runs, the two outer runs have the same letter. A unique palindrome centered in an outer run is monochromatic, and both such centers cannot yield a unique palindrome of the same length: the shorter common monochromatic factor would recur, or, if the lengths differ, only the longer run can provide a unique occurrence. Hence there is at most one outer candidate and one interior candidate, giving the bound two.

For four runs, candidates centered at run 1 and run 3 cannot cover a common query interval: the former is confined to run 1, whereas even a mixed palindrome centered at run 3 is confined to runs 2--4. Analogously candidates centered at runs 2 and 4 are disjoint; candidates at runs 1 and 4 are also disjoint. Every three-element subset of the four possible centers contains at least one of these three disjoint center-pairs. Therefore no interval is contained in three same-length unique palindromes, proving \(\alpha_w(I)\le2\).

For five runs, the outer runs have the same symbol; at most one of them can center a unique monochromatic palindrome at a fixed length. If neither does, there are only three interior centers. If run 1 does, any palindrome centered at run 4 is contained in runs 3--5 and disjoint from run 1. If run 5 does, any palindrome centered at run 2 is contained in runs 1--3 and disjoint from run 5. In each case at most three centers can jointly cover an interval. This proves \(\alpha_w(I)\le3\).

**2. The two separated interior centers cannot both supply SUPSs.** Consider a five-run word
\[
 w=0^a1^b0^c1^d0^e,\qquad a,b,c,d,e\ge1.
\]
Suppose unique palindromes centered in the second and fourth runs both contain \(I\) and have the same least feasible length \(\ell\). They cannot be monochromatic because the supports of the second and fourth runs are disjoint from one another and from the opposite mixed center. Thus they are mixed palindromes
\[
 0^u1^b0^u,\qquad 0^v1^d0^v,
 \quad u,v\ge1.
\]
Their intersection is in the middle zero run, and they can both cover \(I\) only if \(u+v\ge c+1\). Equal lengths give \(b+2u=d+2v=\ell\). If \(b=d\), then \(u=v\) and the two occurrences have the same string, contradicting uniqueness. Otherwise \(b,d\) are distinct positive integers of the same parity; hence \(b+d\ge4\) and
\[
 \ell=\frac{b+d}{2}+u+v\ge c+3.
\]
But \(1 0^c1\) is a palindrome occurring exactly once, around the entire central zero run; it contains every interval \(I\) within that run and has length \(c+2<\ell\). This contradicts the SUPS definition. Thus centers 2 and 4 cannot both contribute answers for one query interval. A three-answer configuration in a five-run word must therefore use an outer center; up to reversal, its three centers are necessarily 1, 2, and 3.

**3. Minimum-length necessity.** By reversing and complementing if necessary, write a three-answer word as above, with the outer candidate centered at the first zero run. The unique first-run palindrome must be \(0^a\), so all three SUPSs have length \(a\). The second-run answer, since it overlaps the first run, must be
\[
 0^v1^b0^v,\quad 1\le v\le\min(a,c),
 \qquad a=b+2v.
\]
The central-run answer must also meet the first run; it therefore crosses all five runs and is of the form
\[
 0^u1^b0^c1^b0^u,
 \quad 1\le u\le\min(a,e),
 \qquad b=d,\quad a=c+2b+2u.
\]
Since \(b=d\), if \(v\le e\) the second-run palindrome occurs also around the fourth run (its left zero arm fits inside the central run because \(v\le c\)), contradicting uniqueness. Hence \(v\ge e+1\). If \(v>e+1\), the smaller palindrome \(0^{e+1}1^b0^{e+1}\) occurs uniquely around the second run: the fourth run has only \(e\) trailing zeros, and there are no other one-runs. The query \(I\) lies within the intersection of the first-run and central-run palindromes, hence in the final \(u\le e\) positions of run 1; this smaller unique palindrome covers that whole intersection. It contradicts minimality of length \(a\). Therefore
\[
 v=e+1,\quad c\ge v,\quad a=b+2v=c+2b+2u.
\]
Using \(u\ge1\) gives \(2v\ge c+b+2\). Together with \(c\ge v\), this yields \(v\ge b+2\). The word length is
\[
 n=a+2b+c+e=3b+3v+c-1
 \ge3b+4v-1\ge7b+7\ge14.
\]
Equality \(n=14\) forces \(b=1\), \(v=3\), \(c=v=3\), \(e=v-1=2\), \(a=b+2v=7\), and \(d=b=1\). Thus the only minimum word up to reversal and complement is \(w_*\). This is a complete argument for the lower bound, not an extrapolation from finite enumeration.

**4. Sufficiency and the infinite family.** Choose arbitrary integers \(b\ge1\), \(e\ge b+1\), and put \(a=b+2e+2\), \(c=2e-b\). Then \(c\ge e+1\), \(a>b,c,e\), and all five runs are nonempty. At the position \(a\), the full first run \(0^a\) is unique; \(0^{e+1}1^b0^{e+1}\) is unique because it cannot be centered at the fourth run, whose trailing zero run has length only \(e\); and \(0 1^b0^c1^b0\) is unique because only the middle zero run is bounded by one-runs on both sides. The three occurrences have length \(a\) and occupy, respectively,
\[
 [1,a],\quad[a-e,\ 2a-e-1],\quad[a,\ 2a-1].
\]
All three contain \([a,a]\). To rule out a shorter unique palindrome covering position \(a\), examine its possible run center. A first-run palindrome of length less than \(a\) is repeated within that run. A second-run mixed palindrome covering \(a\) has shorter length only if each zero arm has length at most \(e\), and then its identical fourth-run occurrence makes it nonunique; a second-run monochromatic palindrome does not cover \(a\). A middle-run palindrome covering \(a\) must cross both one-runs and therefore has at least \(c+2b+2=a\) symbols. Centers in the fourth or fifth runs cannot cover \(a\). Thus the three specified occurrences are exactly the SUPSs, by the three-answer upper bound. Taking \(b=1,e=2\) gives \(n=14\) and \(w_*\). Reversal and complementation give four distinct minimum-length examples, and the uniqueness argument in Step 3 shows that there are no others among words with at most five runs.

## Verification

The supplied standalone `verify_sups.py` constructs all palindromic factors by direct substring comparison, counts occurrences with a complete substring-frequency multiset, and filters eligible unique occurrences independently for every interval. It exhaustively checks all \(6944\) binary words with one to five runs at lengths \(1\) through \(14\), testing all query intervals and both sharp upper bounds. It finds exactly four length-fourteen three-answer instances (one interval per word). It checks all three predicted positions in \(48\) distinct members of the infinite family and additionally tests \(2400\) randomly generated interval instances for larger low-run words. Run `python3 verify_sups.py`; the expected terminal line starts `VERIFY_OK`. These finite checks provide reproducibility and cross-check the sharp witnesses but do not constitute the proof of the infinite bounds.

## Relationship to prior work

The run-length encoded SUPS data structures of Watanabe, Nakashima, Inenaga, Bannai, and Takeda (arXiv:1903.06290, first public 2019-03-14) establish general algorithms and run-centered structure, not the sharp maximum answer count at five runs or its shortest binary witness. Mieno and Funakoshi (arXiv:2204.07327, first public 2022-04-15) show that any string over any alphabet has at most four SUPSs for an interval and exhibit an unrestricted tight example. Their general bound is compatible with, but does not imply, the stronger two- and three-answer bounds under run restrictions. Nakashima and collaborators (arXiv:1608.05550, 2016-08-19) give optimal general query algorithms and relevant definitions, but not this restricted sharp threshold. Searches for run-count, center-pattern, binary-alphabet and length-threshold equivalences found no statement subsuming the present claim in the materials inspected; this search is not a proof of historical uniqueness.

## Limitations

The theorem only bounds and classifies the first occurrence of *three* answers in words with at most five runs; it neither decides whether four SUPSs can arise in an unrestricted binary word nor characterizes all longer five-run words with three answers. The cited general SUPS theory is not being claimed as new. The literature comparison relies on inspected accessible texts and indexed records, so an unindexed or inaccessible equivalent specialization remains possible. No independent expert audit or machine-checked formal proof is claimed.
