# Scientific review

## Correctness — PASS
The count is an exact two-family incidence argument based on the published star/top covering theorem. Distinct Ferrers-diagram intersections and unions give uniqueness of a star or top description; a clique of at least three vertices cannot have both because the union would have too many cells. The shift in each partition size is explicit, the two corner binomial sums are enumerated by a formal product, and the staircase threshold follows from its unique lowest exponent. Edges are counted only via lower stars, avoiding a factor-of-two mistake. Independent graph enumeration and corner enumeration agree with the formula. The proof does not infer an infinite theorem from numerical testing.

## Originality — PASS
The motivating March 24, 2026 paper presents the triangular first-occurrence formula and staircase one-cell-extension family as unproved Conjectures 5.1 and 5.2. The March 27, 2026 numerical paper explicitly disclaims a closed formula for the full family of clique counts. The March 15 foundational paper does establish the global star/top characterization and the two-sphere wedge type, but does not present the all-dimensional face-generating identity in the inspected complete text. Accessible earlier research separately supplies a closed generating function for the **two-sphere multiplicities**, which is not claimed again here. That Euler characteristic specialization does not determine the individual higher-dimensional face counts, nor the unique simplex geometry at each first-occurrence threshold. No equivalent or stronger published statement emerged from searches, subject to bibliographic uncertainty.

## Mathematical value — PASS
This single exact theorem resolves two explicitly posed first-occurrence conjectures throughout their infinite range, gives uniqueness and actual supporting partitions at each threshold, and replaces many finite face tables by one closed product family for every size and face dimension. The information is strictly finer than the previously established Euler characteristics or nonequivariant homotopy types. Its significance lies in the complete simplex distribution and combinatorial thresholds, not in a purported new homotopy classification.
