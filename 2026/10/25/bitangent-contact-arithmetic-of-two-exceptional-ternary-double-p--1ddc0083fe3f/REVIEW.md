# Scientific assessment

## Correctness
PASS. Both branch quartics have no projective singular points by their displayed partial derivatives. Restricting to every line in the full chart yields monic quartics whose square conditions reduce exactly to the stated two additive polynomial systems. The complementary chart has only the hyperflex \(y=0\). Explicit factorization of the additive \(s\)-polynomials, trace-zero criteria for \(t^3-t=c\), and discriminants of the contact quadratics give all lines, all contacts, and all Frobenius cycles. The standalone exact field enumerator separately checks the finite counts; no extrapolation from an incomplete experiment is needed.

## Originality
PASS, with bounded-literature risk. Salgado–Testa–Várilly-Alvarado already provide the exceptional surfaces and certain abstract Galois-action information; Festi–van Luijk already give their equations and unirationality. These existing results can cover the ancillary Frobenius permutation on exceptional curves and some bitangent-line orbit information. The new claim is the fully explicit *contact-point* arithmetic, including the one-versus-28 hyperflex dichotomy and the 55-point versus 28-point support with individual Frobenius cycles and complete parameter equations. The closest primary texts inspected do not state that contact-divisor classification. The second quartic's Hermitian hyperflex property is a known comparison, not freestanding novelty. This is a best-knowledge rather than a uniqueness certificate.

## Mathematical value
PASS. The two rare exceptional degree-two del Pezzo surfaces are distinguished by their unusually small rational-point sets; bitangents correspond naturally to anticanonical splitting, and their contact points are the ramification-divisor geometry not determined by the surface's abstract Galois cohomology. A complete exact contact classification identifies different arithmetic splitting thresholds and ordinary/hyperflex boundaries for these named surfaces; it is a natural invariant relevant to rational tangent geometry, not an arbitrary finite-field slice.
