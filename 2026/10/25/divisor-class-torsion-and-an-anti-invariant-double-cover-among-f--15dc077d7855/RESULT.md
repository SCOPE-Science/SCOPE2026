# Divisor-class torsion and an anti-invariant double cover among four-dimensional complete-intersection quotient counterexamples

## Finding

Let \(G_i\subset\operatorname{SL}_4(\mathbb Z)\), for \(i\in\{45,105,65,66\}\), be the four groups specified by the integral generators in Appendix A of Burek–Cynk [1]. Put \(X_i=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb C[z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4]^{G_i}\). Their affine Weil divisor class groups are
\[
\operatorname{Cl}(X_{45})\cong (\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^2,\qquad
\operatorname{Cl}(X_{105})\cong (\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^2,
\]
\[
\operatorname{Cl}(X_{65})\cong (\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z)^2,\qquad
\operatorname{Cl}(X_{66})\cong\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z.
\]
In particular, all four affine singularities are \(\mathbb Q\)-factorial but nonfactorial. The exponent of each class group gives the least common positive multiple annihilating all Weil divisor classes: respectively \(2,2,3,2\).

Moreover \(G_{65}\triangleleft G_{66}\) has index two. The induced finite map \(\pi:X_{65}\to X_{66}\) is a degree-two Galois quasi-étale cover. Its pullback \(\pi^*:\operatorname{Cl}(X_{66})\to\operatorname{Cl}(X_{65})\) is zero, while its nontrivial deck transformation acts as multiplication by \(-1\) on the entire group \(\operatorname{Cl}(X_{65})\cong(\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z)^2\).

## Assumptions and scope

All varieties and class groups are over \(\mathbb C\). The groups are *exactly* the four integral representations numbered 45, 105, 65 and 66 in [1], not arbitrary abstract groups of the same orders. The invariant rings carry their normal affine quotient structures. A quasi-étale map means a finite dominant morphism étale away from a closed subset of codimension at least two. The theorem addresses the affine quotient class groups, not the class groups of terminalizations or projective crepant resolutions. The nonexistence of projective crepant resolutions and the complete-intersection properties are inputs established in [1], not new assertions.

## Proof

**1. From group characters to divisors.** For finite \(G\subset \operatorname{SL}_4(\mathbb C)\), a nonidentity complex pseudo-reflection would have three eigenvalues equal to \(1\); its determinant-one condition would force the fourth eigenvalue to be \(1\), making it the identity because finite-order complex matrices are semisimple. Thus \(G\) contains no pseudo-reflections. The Benson–Nakajima quotient class-group theorem, stated in [2, Section 2.1], gives
\[
\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb A^4/G)\cong
\operatorname{Hom}(G,\mathbb C^\times)
\cong \operatorname{Hom}(G/[G,G],\mathbb C^\times).
\]
These isomorphisms are natural under restriction of characters for inclusions of such groups.

**2. Exact finite group calculation.** The accompanying `verification_quotients.py` contains the integer matrices transcribed from [1, Appendix A]. It generates each group by exhaustive finite breadth-first multiplication. It computes \([G,G]\) as the subgroup generated by the normal closure of the pairwise commutators of the displayed generators. This equals the full derived subgroup: the derived subgroup is, by definition, the smallest normal subgroup with abelian quotient, and normalizing those generating commutators forces every generator to commute in the quotient. Exhaustive coset enumeration and multiplication determine the number of elements of each possible order in \(G/[G,G]\). The results are

| Case | \(|G|\) | \(|[G,G]|\) | Element orders in \(G/[G,G]\) | Abelianization |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- | --- |
| 45 | 36 | 9 | one identity; three involutions | \((\mathbb Z/2)^2\) |
| 105 | 24 | 6 | one identity; three involutions | \((\mathbb Z/2)^2\) |
| 65 | 288 | 32 | one identity; eight elements of order three | \((\mathbb Z/3)^2\) |
| 66 | 576 | 288 | one identity; one involution | \(\mathbb Z/2\) |

These finite enumerations are **exhaustive**, not extrapolations from a bounded family. The algorithm uses arbitrary-precision integer matrix multiplication and exact rational matrix inversion; the only termination bounds exceed the independently displayed group orders. The two invariant-ring class groups follow immediately by character duality. Finite class groups make all Weil classes torsion, hence \(\mathbb Q\)-Cartier (a principal multiple is Cartier); nontrivial class groups prove nonfactoriality. The claimed exponents follow from the group structures.

**3. The index-two cover and its action.** In Appendix A, the seven matrices generating \(G_{65}\) occur also among the eight generators of \(G_{66}\). Write \(F\) for the additional matrix, whose rows are \((1,0,0,-1)\), \((0,1,0,2)\), \((0,0,1,-1)\), \((0,-1,0,-1)\). Exact multiplication verifies \(F\notin G_{65}\), \(F^2\in G_{65}\), and, for every listed generator \(g\) of \(G_{65}\),
\[
FgF^{-1}g\in [G_{65},G_{65}].
\]
Therefore \(G_{65}\) is normal of index two in \(G_{66}\), and \(F\) induces inversion on \(G_{65}/[G_{65},G_{65}]\). Dualizing, the deck involution multiplies each character by \(-1\), hence acts by \(-1\) on \(\operatorname{Cl}(X_{65})\).

The invariant-ring inclusion produces a finite Galois map of degree two. Neither group contains pseudo-reflections. Outside the union of nonidentity fixed-point sets in \(\mathbb A^4\), each quotient map is étale; all those fixed-point sets have codimension at least two. Thus the intermediate quotient is étale in codimension one. Finally, restriction of characters sends the order-two class group of \(G_{66}\) into a group of exponent three, so that \(\pi^*=0\). More explicitly, its sole nontrivial character is the character of the index-two quotient and restricts trivially to \(G_{65}\).

## Verification

Run `python3 verification_quotients.py` using Python 3.9 or later; no third-party modules are required. It reproduces the exact group and commutator-subgroup cardinalities, all abelian-quotient element-order histograms, subgroup containment and index, square of the extra generator, and the conjugation identities for the seven generators. The expected final line is `VERIFY_OK four_group_abelianizations_and_cover_involution`. Each finite enumeration closes the actual matrix groups and checks its outputs by assertions. The geometry uses Benson's theorem, finite-quotient invariant theory, and the no-pseudo-reflection argument rather than empirical extrapolation. The test program does **not** independently prove Benson's theorem.

## Relationship to prior work

Burek–Cynk [1] introduced these four distinguished quotient representations as counterexamples to a crepant-resolution conjecture, computed their invariant presentations, and supplied the integral generating matrices and order data. Their Cox-ring sieve concerns the class group of a terminalization, not the four affine Weil divisor class groups determined here. Their broader computational methods use group abelianizations; this result does not claim the underlying character-group method is new. Benson's character description of the class group is classical, as explained by Schmitt [2]. The additional specific calculation is the full four-case affine divisor-torsion profile together with the geometric quasi-étale cover between cases 65 and 66, its vanishing class pullback, and its nontrivial action on three-torsion. The index-two inclusion is also directly visible in the published matrices, but its class-group consequences require computation and interpretation.

## Limitations

The theorem is confined to these four representations; it does not classify the other 102 catalogue cases. Their published lack of projective crepant resolutions is cited, not reproved. Class groups of partial resolutions or of quotient stacks are not calculated. Although the exact group computations and identified source statements are reproducible, an equivalent earlier calculation could be implicit in external group catalogues or unpublished symbolic computations. No independent mathematical audit has been performed.
