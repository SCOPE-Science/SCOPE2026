# Scientific review: first Euclidean pair-distance transition of simplices

## Correctness (C): PASS
The claim holds for every full-dimensional Euclidean simplex in dimension at least three, without a genericity assumption. The overlap of a simplex with its translate is exactly a homothetic simplex, with linear shrinkage given by the sum of positive barycentric-gradient projections. This produces the exact covariogram and hence the random two-point-distance integral. Distinct subset-sum gradients supply the finite polyhedral support function, so the smallest Euclidean ball encountering the positive-part clipping is precisely the reciprocal of the largest subset-sum norm. Before any shorter dual normal becomes active or any two maximal caps overlap, the correction is exactly a sum of congruent spherical-cap integrals. Their longitudinal integration and beta-function asymptotic prove the coefficient, parity and sharp exponent. This argument explicitly handles simultaneous maximal normals. In dimension three the cap integral is algebraically evaluated and the seven vertex/edge-pair distances account for all subset sums.

A numerical checker rebuilt the overlap polytope independently from its eight facet inequalities for tetrahedra (and ten for a four-simplex), verified their intersection volumes, and directly tested the first-cap correction using separate spherical quadrature. The checker includes a planar triangle only as a control. Numerical sampling supports the result but is not used to justify the universal theorem.

## Originality (O): PASS, subject to historical-priority uncertainty
The precise object is the **Euclidean** two-uniform-point distance CDF for *all* simplices of dimension at least three. Prior covariogram and triangle laws imply the starting integral and, in two dimensions, some explicit formulas; they do not by themselves give the sharp all-dimensional Euclidean first threshold, the first disjoint-cap interval, the exact correction, and the asymptotic coefficient. The known beta law for a *difference-body gauge* is not equivalent to Euclidean radial-ball clipping. A previously reported parallelotope clipping result uses a product covariogram and does not cover simplices. Sinan Wang's 2026 work treats general concavity and variational behavior of random-pair distributions, without a matching theorem in the primary material inspected. Searches of the published research records and accessible recent geometry claims found no implication covering the proposed statement. These searches do not constitute a formal proof of global novelty; archival stereology or unindexed simplex-distance calculations remain possible.

Beck’s *Mean distance in polyhedra* (arXiv:2309.13177v1, 2023) was additionally inspected at the main theorem and tetrahedron reduction: it obtains elementary expressions for each natural moment, not the proposed piecewise Euclidean CDF or its singular coefficient. Moment determinacy on compact support is acknowledged, but the ability to reconstruct a distribution from an infinite sequence of moments does not itself imply the claimed exact local formula or cap-active geometry. The 2013 arbitrary-triangle paper and 2016 arbitrary-polygon paper were inspected in full-text form at their theorem/formula sections; their hypotheses are restricted to dimension two. The 2022 covariogram paper was read in the introductory/general relation and worked planar cases. The 1999 homothetic simplex paper confirms that the simplex intersection input is older and must not be claimed as new. The 2026 motivating paper was checked at its main results and searchable text for relevant simplex/polyhedral explicit laws. No independent systematic bibliographic priority audit has been carried out.

## Mathematical value (V): PASS
The question is natural in geometric probability: where does the elementary small-distance distribution for a simplex first cease to be polynomial, and with what singularity? The theorem answers that question for every higher-dimensional simplex, gives an actual next branch rather than only an asymptotic, identifies the geometry controlling it through a finite spectrum of barycentric normals, handles multiplicity/ties, and gives a characteristic quintic onset for all tetrahedra. The difference between a regular tetrahedron and an orthogonal one illustrates a meaningful shape-dependent multiplicity, while the universal exponent \((3d+1)/2\) exposes dimension-dependent regularity. These facts are neither an arbitrary numerical slice nor a direct restatement of a known one-dimensional distance calculation.

The covariogram-distance identity and homothetic simplex overlap are established inputs, not original parts.
