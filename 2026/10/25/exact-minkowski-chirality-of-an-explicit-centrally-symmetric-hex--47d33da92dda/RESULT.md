# Exact Minkowski Chirality of an Explicit Centrally Symmetric Hexagon

## Finding

Let
\[
K=[-e_1,e_1]+[-2e_2,2e_2]+[-(e_1+e_2),e_1+e_2]\subset\mathbb R^2,
\]
where the plus signs denote Minkowski sums and \(e_1=(1,0)\), \(e_2=(0,1)\). If \(\alpha_1(K)\) is the first Minkowski chirality, defined as the infimum of the dilation needed to contain \(K\) in a translate of its reflection across a line, then
\[
\boxed{\alpha_1(K)=\frac{9}{\sqrt{61}}.}
\]
The minimizing reflection lines have angle
\[
\theta=\frac12\left(\frac\pi2+\arctan\frac56\right)\pmod{\frac\pi2}.
\]

## Assumptions and scope

All distances, angles, support functions, and containments are Euclidean. The body \(K\) is the Minkowski sum of the three centered segments generated by \(e_1\), \(2e_2\), and \(e_1+e_2\). Their directions are pairwise nonparallel, so \(K\) is a full-dimensional centrally symmetric hexagon. For a line through the origin with angle \(\theta\), reflection sends a direction angle \(\varphi\) to \(2\theta-\varphi\). Since both bodies are centrally symmetric, translations do not improve the containment dilation.

## Proof

The support function on unit directions is
\[
h(\varphi)=|\cos\varphi|+2|\sin\varphi|+|\cos\varphi+\sin\varphi|.
\]
For a reflection line of angle \(\theta\), the least dilation is
\[
\lambda(\theta)=\max_{\varphi\in[0,\pi)}\frac{h(\varphi)}{h(2\theta-\varphi)}.
\]
Put \(x=2\theta\pmod\pi\). The breakpoints of \(h\) on \([0,\pi)\) are \(0,\pi/2,3\pi/4\), with values \(2,3,3\sqrt2/2\). On every interval where the signs inside both support functions are fixed, the ratio is a quotient of two linear combinations of sine and cosine; its derivative has constant sign or vanishes identically. Thus its maximum occurs at a breakpoint of the numerator or denominator. A denominator breakpoint gives the reciprocal of the ratio at one of the three numerator breakpoints. Consequently,
\[
\lambda(x)=\max_{\varphi_i\in\{0,\pi/2,3\pi/4\}}\max\left\{\frac{h(\varphi_i)}{h(x-\varphi_i)},\frac{h(x-\varphi_i)}{h(\varphi_i)}\right\}.
\]
In particular, \(\lambda(x)\) is bounded below by
\[
F(x)=\max\left\{\frac{h(x)}2,\frac{h(x-\pi/2)}3,\frac{h(x-3\pi/4)}{3\sqrt2/2}\right\}.
\]
We prove \(F(x)\ge9/\sqrt{61}\) by four intervals.

1. If \(0\le x\le\pi/4\), then
\[
\frac{h(x-3\pi/4)}{3\sqrt2/2}=\frac{5\cos x+\sin x}{3}\ge\sqrt2>\frac9{\sqrt{61}}.
\]
The inequality follows because the only interior critical point of \(5\cos x+\sin x\) is a maximum, so its minimum on this interval is at an endpoint.

2. If \(\pi/4\le x\le\pi/2\), then \(h(x)/2=\cos x+\tfrac32\sin x\). Its only interior critical point is a maximum, so it is at least \(3/2>9/\sqrt{61}\).

3. If \(\pi/2\le x\le3\pi/4\), write \(\beta=x-\pi/2\in[0,\pi/4]\). Then
\[
F(x)\ge\max\left\{\frac32\cos\beta,\frac{2\cos\beta+3\sin\beta}{3}\right\}.
\]
The first term decreases and the second increases on this interval. They are equal exactly when \(\tan\beta=5/6\); their common value is \(9/\sqrt{61}\). Hence their maximum is never smaller.

4. If \(3\pi/4\le x\le\pi\), put \(y=x-3\pi/4\in[0,\pi/4]\). Two terms of \(F\) are
\[
B(y)=\frac{5\cos y+\sin y}{3\sqrt2},\qquad C(y)=\frac{4\cos y+6\sin y}{3\sqrt2}.
\]
The function \(B\) increases until \(\tan y=1/5\), then decreases; \(C\) increases throughout the interval. They cross at \(\tan y=1/5\), where \(B\) has its maximum. Before that crossing, \(B(y)\ge B(0)=5/(3\sqrt2)\); after it, \(C(y)\ge\sqrt{13}/3\). Both constants exceed \(9/\sqrt{61}\). Thus \(F(x)>9/\sqrt{61}\) on this interval.

It remains to attain the lower bound. Choose \(x=\pi/2+\beta\), where \(\tan\beta=5/6\). Then \(\cos\beta=6/\sqrt{61}\), \(\sin\beta=5/\sqrt{61}\), and
\[
h(x)=\frac{18}{\sqrt{61}},\qquad h(x-\pi/2)=\frac{27}{\sqrt{61}}.
\]
Also \(\tan(\beta-\pi/4)=-1/11\), giving
\[
h(x-3\pi/4)=\frac{23}{\sqrt{122}}.
\]
The three breakpoint ratios and their reciprocals are therefore
\[
\frac{h(0)}{h(x)}=\frac{h(\pi/2)}{h(x-\pi/2)}=\frac{\sqrt{61}}9,
\qquad
\frac{h(3\pi/4)}{h(x-3\pi/4)}=\frac{3\sqrt{61}}{23}<\frac9{\sqrt{61}}.
\]
The reciprocal of the last ratio is also less than \(9/\sqrt{61}\). The finite-breakpoint formula gives \(\lambda(x)=9/\sqrt{61}\). Combined with the global lower bound, this proves the claim. The intersection in interval 3 is unique, and the other intervals have strict larger lower bounds, so the minimizing value of \(x\) is unique modulo \(\pi\), equivalently the reflection line is unique modulo \(\pi/2\).

## Verification

The analytic proof reduces the optimization over all directions to three support breakpoints and four explicit angular intervals. The included symbolic checker verifies the exact trigonometric identities at the candidate angle and the strict comparisons used to separate the other intervals. It is a reproducibility aid; the interval argument above, rather than numerical sampling, establishes the global lower bound.

## Relationship to prior work

Caragea, von Dichter, Gottwald, Grundbacher, Jahn, and Runge define Minkowski chirality by optimal containment in a reflected copy of a convex body. Their 2025 preprint proves general bounds, treats triangles and parallelograms in detail, and identifies exact optimal containment for those classes. It also notes that optimal-containment descriptions for general bodies can be involved. The present body is a three-generator zonotope with six sides, and the calculation gives its exact value and minimizing line; it is not a restatement of the triangle or parallelogram theorems. Searches also surfaced results on the Minkowski asymmetry of parallel-opposite hexagons and the minimum enclosing-parallelogram ratio for centrally symmetric hexagons. Those are different invariants and do not imply this value.

## Limitations

This is an exact evaluation for one explicitly specified hexagon, not a formula for all centrally symmetric hexagons or all three-generator zonotopes. The literature comparison is bounded by the cited preprint and the recorded database queries; an exhaustive novelty claim is not made. Independent audit has not been performed.
