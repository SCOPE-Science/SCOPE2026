# Scientific review: exact myopic cumulative-envy minimization

**Correctness — PASS.** The statement specifies two agents with one identical, additive, strictly positive valuation, fixed arrival order, and cumulative maximum envy. The suffix dynamic-programming recurrence is exact. Backward induction establishes three interlocking invariants: two-step monotonicity above imbalance \(1\), a two-unit bound between starting imbalances \(0\) and \(2\), and dominance of imbalance \(0\) over \(4\). These certify every greedy Bellman choice reachable from the empty allocation. The counterexample \((1,1,k,1)\) has analytically optimal cost \(2k-2\), against greedy cost \(2k\) for every integer \(k\ge3\). A monotonicity argument establishes the minimum four-item length. The standalone exact checker passes on \(8191\) binary words and the further tests specified in RESULT.md. Finite tests are corroboration, not proof of unbounded quantifiers.

**Originality — PASS, with priority risk.** The closest paper is Goldberg, Robinson, and Teh, *Minimizing Cumulative Envy in Allocating a Sequence of Items* (arXiv:2610.09843, Section 4 and Appendix 0.B.1). Its myopic optimum is restricted to identical \(\{0,1\}\) valuations and follows from a per-stage lower bound. For two positive values \(\{1,2\}\), the same per-stage argument fails on the explicit instance \((1,1,2)\). The general dynamic program of that paper computes an optimum but does not imply exact myopic optimality. Relevant two-value-online and temporal-EF1 works optimize different properties. Targeted mathematical-result and literature queries produced no exact equivalent. A result under the alias signed cumulative prefix discrepancy or two-machine imbalance scheduling could be unindexed, and some related full texts were not inspected; publication priority therefore remains uncertain.

**Value — PASS.** The dynamic objective captures both magnitude and persistence of inequality. It matters operationally whether a memoryless irrevocable rule attains the optimum of a fully informed allocation; doing so eliminates both prediction and dynamic programming for a natural smallest unequal positive-value alphabet. The proof is not a routine per-prefix balance argument, because a locally worse split can equal the optimal total even with \(\{1,2\}\), and becomes strictly superior with \(\{1,3\}\). A four-item, size-minimal witness precisely locates where the integer-valued guarantee breaks. The finding neither asserts a general ratio threshold for real values nor applies to heterogeneous preferences.
