# Finite-level ball-illumination of planar disks: an exact profile and a saturation cusp

## Finding

Fix radii \(0<r<R\), and put \(K=rB_2\subset\mathbb R^2\). The \(R\)-ball convex hull of \(K\) and a point \(z\) is the intersection of all closed Euclidean disks of radius \(R\) containing \(K\cup\{z\}\), with the usual empty-intersection convention \(\mathbb R^2\). Define the \(R\)-ball illumination body by
\[
 I_R^\delta(K)=\{z\in\mathbb R^2:\operatorname{area}([K,z]_R\setminus K)\le\delta\},\qquad \delta\ge0.
\]

The complete family \(I_R^\delta(K)\), at **every** finite level, is given by an explicit one-variable circular-arc formula. Put
\[
 k=R-r,\quad T=2R-r,\quad \delta_*=\pi(R^2-r^2).
\]
For \(r\le t\le T\), set
\[
 a(t)=\frac{t^2+k^2-R^2}{2t},\qquad h(t)=\sqrt{k^2-a(t)^2},
\]
\[
 \phi(t)=\operatorname{atan2}(h(t),-a(t))\in[0,\pi],\qquad
 \beta(t)=\operatorname{atan2}(h(t),t-a(t)),
\]
where \(\operatorname{atan2}(0,-k)=\pi\). Define
\[
 F(t)=rt\sin\phi(t)-r^2\phi(t)
       +R^2\bigl(\phi(t)-\beta(t)-\sin(\phi(t)-\beta(t))\bigr).
\]
Then \(F\) is continuous and strictly increasing from \(F(r)=0\) to \(F(T)=\delta_*\), and
\[
 \boxed{I_R^\delta(K)=\rho(\delta)B_2,\qquad
 \rho(\delta)=\begin{cases}
  F^{-1}(\delta),&0\le\delta<\delta_*,\\
  T,&\delta\ge\delta_*.
 \end{cases}}
\]
In particular, **finite-level illumination saturates**: the same disk of radius \(2R-r\) persists at all levels \(\delta\ge\delta_*\). Its two sharp endpoint laws are
\[
 \rho(\delta)-r\sim
 \left(\frac{3}{2\sqrt2}\sqrt{\frac{R-r}{rR}}\,\delta\right)^{2/3}
 \quad(\delta\downarrow0),
\]
\[
 T-\rho(\delta)\sim \frac{T}{32R^3(R-r)}(\delta_*-\delta)^2
 \quad(\delta\uparrow\delta_*).
\]
Thus the small-level exponent \(2/3\) coexists with a **quadratic approach to a finite saturation level**. The first coefficient agrees with the known relative affine-surface-area asymptotic; the exact finite-level profile and upper-end law are not consequences of that asymptotic alone.

## Assumptions and scope

All disks are closed; area is planar Lebesgue measure. The strict inequalities \(0<r<R\) are essential to the stated nondegenerate endpoint coefficients. The area of \(\mathbb R^2\setminus K\) is infinite. The inverse \(F^{-1}\) is the ordinary inverse of a strictly increasing continuous function on \([r,T]\). The result is planar and radial, not a formula for general nonsymmetric ball-convex sets or arbitrary dimension.

## Proof

**1. Possible enclosing centers.** By the triangle inequality, a radius-\(R\) disk with center \(c\) contains \(rB_2\) if and only if \(\|c\|\le k=R-r\). For \(z=(t,0)\), the centers of disks also containing \(z\) form
\[
 C_t=B_2(0,k)\cap B_2((t,0),R).
\]
For \(t\le r\), the point already lies in \(K\), so the hull equals \(K\). For \(r<t<T\), the two boundary-circle intersections of \(C_t\) are
\[
 c_\pm=(a(t),\pm h(t)).
\]
They exist precisely for \(r\le t\le T\). For \(t>T\), no enclosing disk exists, so the empty-intersection convention gives \([K,z]_R=\mathbb R^2\), with infinite excess area.

**2. Boundary of the hull for \(r<t<T\).** Let
\[
 p_\pm=(r\cos\phi,\pm r\sin\phi).
\]
The identities \(p_+=-rc_-/k\) and \(p_-=-rc_+/k\) show that \(B_2(c_-,R)\) touches \(rB_2\) internally at \(p_+\), and \(B_2(c_+,R)\) touches it at \(p_-\). Both radius-\(R\) disks contain \(z\). The proposed hull boundary consists, counterclockwise, of the radius-\(r\) circle from \(p_+\) around the left to \(p_-\), the radius-\(R\) arc centered at \(c_+\) from \(p_-\) to \(z\), and the radius-\(R\) arc centered at \(c_-\) from \(z\) to \(p_+\). These arcs fit tangentially at \(p_\pm\); their outward-normal angles rotate monotonically, with a convex corner at \(z\). Thus they bound a convex set \(H_t\).

To verify equality with the **intersection over every feasible center**, first note that each outer arc is part of the radius-\(R\) spindle between \(z\) and the corresponding \(p_\pm\). Hence every disk containing \(K\cup\{z\}\) contains both outer arcs. It also contains \(K\). Since it is convex, it contains their convex hull \(H_t\). Therefore \(H_t\subseteq[K,z]_R\).

For the reverse inclusion, take any \(y\notin H_t\) and its metric projection \(w\) onto the convex set \(H_t\). At a smooth point \(w\) of an outer arc, the corresponding feasible disk \(B_2(c_+,R)\) or \(B_2(c_-,R)\) is tangent at \(w\) with the same outward normal; it excludes \(y\). At a point \(w=ru\) of the exposed radius-\(r\) arc, choose the disk centered at \(-ku\), which contains \(K\) and also \(z\), because
\[
 \|z+ku\|^2=t^2+k^2+2tk\cos\angle(u,e_1)\le R^2
\]
for the arc angles \(\phi\le\angle(u,e_1)\le2\pi-\phi\). This disk has the same outward normal at \(w\) and excludes \(y\). At the corner \(z\), its outward normal cone is generated by those of the two feasible disks centered at \(c_\pm\), so at least one excludes \(y\). The common tangency points \(p_\pm\) follow by continuity. Thus \(y\notin[K,z]_R\), proving \([K,z]_R=H_t\).

**3. Exact area.** Let \(\Delta=\phi-\beta\in(0,\pi)\). The right-hand circular segment of \(K\), bounded by the chord \(p_-p_+\), has area \(r^2(\phi-\sin\phi\cos\phi)\). The triangle \(zp_+p_-\) has area \(r\sin\phi(t-r\cos\phi)\). Each of the two outer radius-\(R\) circular segments contributes \(R^2(\Delta-\sin\Delta)/2\). Consequently the area of the hull minus that of \(K\) is
\[
 -r^2(\phi-\sin\phi\cos\phi)
 +r\sin\phi(t-r\cos\phi)+R^2(\Delta-\sin\Delta)=F(t).
\]
This calculation remains valid when \(\phi\) exceeds \(\pi/2\): the circular-segment term is the (potentially major) right-hand segment.

**4. Strict increase and exact saturation.** For \(r<t_1<t_2\le T\), every radius-\(R\) disk containing \(K\) and \((t_2,0)\) contains \((t_1,0)\), because the latter lies between \((r,0)\in K\) and \((t_2,0)\). Thus the hulls are nested. The point \((t_2,0)\) is outside the feasible disk centered at \(c_-(t_1)\), whose circle already passes through \((t_1,0)\) and whose rightmost intersection with the horizontal ray is that point. This shows strict inclusion and strictly increasing area. At \(t=T\), the feasible-center lens degenerates to the singleton \((k,0)\); the hull equals its radius-\(R\) disk and \(F(T)=\pi(R^2-r^2)\). Orthogonal rotational invariance now gives precisely the asserted illumination disks, including the saturation for larger levels.

**5. Endpoint laws.** Using \(\gamma=\pi-\phi\), the circle-intersection condition may be parameterized by
\[
 t=R\cos\beta-k\cos\phi,\qquad
 \sin\beta=\frac{k}{R}\sin\phi.
\]
At \(\phi\downarrow0\), Taylor expansion gives
\[
 t-r=\frac{rk}{2R}\phi^2+O(\phi^4),\qquad
 F(t)=\frac{r^2k}{3R}\phi^3+O(\phi^5).
\]
Eliminating \(\phi\) yields
\[
 F(t)=\frac{2\sqrt2}{3}\sqrt{\frac{rR}{k}}(t-r)^{3/2}
 +O((t-r)^{5/2}).
\]
At \(\gamma\downarrow0\), expansion at the opposite endpoint gives
\[
 T-t=\frac{Tk}{2R}\gamma^2+O(\gamma^4),\qquad
 \delta_*-F(t)=4Rk\gamma+O(\gamma^3).
\]
Eliminating \(\gamma\) gives the quadratic inverse law displayed above. No finite sampling is used as a proof of either asymptotic.

## Verification

Run `python3 verify_illumination.py` in Python 3 (standard library only). The script independently constructs the feasible-center lens for several \((R,r,t)\) triples, discretizes **both boundary arcs of that lens**, computes the radial function of the intersection of all associated radius-\(R\) disks, and integrates it numerically. These area estimates agree with the closed circular-segment formula to the specified sampling tolerance. It also checks strict increase on fine grids, exact endpoint areas, and both analytic asymptotic coefficients for four radius pairs.

These numerical checks are finite-resolution consistency tests; the all-parameter theorem follows from the geometric argument above. A reproducible instance is \((R,r,t)=(2,1,3/2)\), for which \(F(t)\approx0.480537418994\).

## Relationship to prior work

Oliva, Werner and Yalikun introduced \(R\)-ball illumination bodies and proved convexity and a small-level relative-affine-area limit. That theorem determines the leading \(\delta^{2/3}\) coefficient here, but does **not**, by itself, determine the full finite-level inverse profile, the finite saturation level, or the quadratic approach to saturation. The definitions and spindle geometry of Lángi, Naszódi and Talata underlie the geometry; they do not supply this illumination-area profile. Classical illumination bodies (Werner, 1994) use ordinary convex hulls, whereas this theorem uses a fixed-radius ball hull. Hyperbolic and spherical geodesic-ball illumination concerns a different hull operation.

## Limitations

Only the planar Euclidean disk with \(0<r<R\) is classified. The near-zero law is a special case of an existing general first-order theorem and is presented as a consistency check, **not** as a separate novelty claim. Exact novelty in unindexed classical circular-arc geometry cannot be absolutely excluded; no independent external review has been performed. The large-level saturation uses the explicitly stated empty-intersection convention. The numerical verifier does not certify the continuous geometry.
