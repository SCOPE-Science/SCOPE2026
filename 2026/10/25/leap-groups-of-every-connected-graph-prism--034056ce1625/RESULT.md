# Leap groups of every connected graph prism

## Finding

Let \(G\) be any finite, simple, connected graph on \(n\ge1\) vertices, and let \(P(G)=G\square K_2\) be its two-layer Cartesian prism. A **hop** on a graph \(H\) is a bijection \(h:V(H)\to V(H)\) for which \(h(x)\) is adjacent to \(x\) for every vertex \(x\). The **leap group** \(\Lambda(H)\) is the permutation subgroup generated by all hops on \(H\). Then, as permutation groups on the \(2n\) vertices of \(P(G)\),

\[
\Lambda(P(G))=\begin{cases}
\operatorname{Sym}(V(P(G))),&G\text{ is nonbipartite},\\
(\operatorname{Sym}(X)\times\operatorname{Sym}(Y))\rtimes C_2,&G\text{ is bipartite},
\end{cases}
\]

where in the second case \(X,Y\) are the two size-\(n\) bipartition classes of \(P(G)\), and the nontrivial element of \(C_2\) exchanges them. Thus the abstract groups are respectively \(S_{2n}\) and \(S_n\wr C_2\), with orders \((2n)!\) and \(2(n!)^2\).

**Explicit consequences.** For every \(r\ge3\), the cyclic prism \(C_r\square K_2\) has leap group \(S_{2r}\) when \(r\) is odd and \(S_r\wr C_2\) when \(r\) is even. For every \(d\ge1\), the binary hypercube \(Q_d=K_2^{\square d}\) has leap group \(S_{2^{d-1}}\wr C_2\). These determine both graph families proposed in Question 5.4 of Schachner--Udell (2026), and the general theorem gives a Cartesian-product instance of their Question 5.5.

## Assumptions and scope

All graphs are finite, undirected, simple and loopless. Connectedness includes the one-vertex graph \(K_1\). Vertex labels are fixed; \(\Lambda(P(G))\) is the actual subgroup of the full vertex-permutation group, not merely an abstract group of the same order. The notation \(S_n\wr C_2\) refers to the imprimitive action preserving or interchanging two blocks of size \(n\). The statement says nothing about disconnected base graphs, directed hops, or graph automorphism groups.

## Proof

**1. Two hops realize each diagonal transposition.** For \(u v\in E(G)\), write \(a=(u,0)\), \(b=(v,0)\), \(c=(v,1)\), and \(d=(u,1)\). These are the vertices of an elementary four-cycle in \(P(G)\), in the cyclic order \(a,b,c,d\). Let \(v\) denote the globally defined vertical hop \((w,0)\leftrightarrow(w,1)\) for all \(w\in V(G)\). Let \(p\) be the hop which acts as \((a\ b\ c\ d)\) on this four-cycle and as \(v\) on all other columns; let \(p'\) use the reversed four-cycle \((a\ d\ c\ b)\), again agreeing with \(v\) outside it. Every image under these permutations traverses an edge, so they genuinely are hops. Multiplication is right-to-left. Directly on these four vertices,

\[
pv=(b\ d),\qquad p'v=(a\ c),
\]

and each product fixes every other vertex. Consequently the leap group contains, for every base edge \(uv\), the individual transpositions \(((u,0)\ (v,1))\) and \(((u,1)\ (v,0))\).

**2. The generated transposition graph is the bipartite double cover.** On the vertex set \(V(G)\times\{0,1\}\), define \(D\) by joining \((u,i)\) to \((w,1-i)\) whenever \(uw\in E(G)\). Step 1 shows that the leap group contains every edge transposition of \(D\). Edge transpositions of any connected graph generate its full symmetric group: along a path, conjugating consecutive edge swaps realizes a swap between the path endpoints, and a spanning tree suffices. Therefore the leap group contains the direct product of the full symmetric groups on the connected components of \(D\).

If \(G\) is connected and nonbipartite, then \(D\) is connected. Indeed, a walk of length \(k\) in \(G\) lifts from \((u,i)\) to \((w,i+k\bmod2)\); an odd closed walk in connected \(G\) permits either parity between any two base vertices. Hence the edge swaps in Step 1 already generate \(S_{2n}\), and no larger permutation group exists.

If \(G\) is connected and bipartite, choose a proper two-coloring \(c:V(G)\to\{0,1\}\). The invariant \(c(u)+i\bmod2\) partitions \(D\) into exactly two connected components, namely \(X\) and \(Y\), each with \(n\) vertices: each component is a lifted copy of \(G\). Step 1 therefore gives \(\operatorname{Sym}(X)\times\operatorname{Sym}(Y)\le\Lambda(P(G))\). The classes \(X,Y\) are also the bipartition classes of the prism: every horizontal or vertical edge crosses between them. Thus every hop interchanges \(X\) with \(Y\), and every leap either preserves both or interchanges them; this bounds \(\Lambda(P(G))\) by \((\operatorname{Sym}(X)\times\operatorname{Sym}(Y))\rtimes C_2\). The vertical hop \(v\) exchanges the classes, meeting the upper bound. This proves equality, including \(G=K_1\).

**3. Consequences.** Cycles are bipartite precisely for even length. Hypercubes are connected bipartite graphs and may be written as \(Q_d=Q_{d-1}\square K_2\) with \(|V(Q_{d-1})|=2^{d-1}\); for \(d=1\), use \(Q_0=K_1\). Substituting these base graphs into the theorem gives the two stated formulas. \(\square\)

## Verification

The standalone file `verify_leap_prisms.py` independently constructs the prism adjacency relation, enumerates **all** admissible hop permutations and computes their generated permutation-group order using SymPy. It checks all 44 connected labeled base graphs with at most four vertices and five further named examples (five-vertex path, five-, six-, and seven-cycles, and the three-cube as an eight-vertex base graph). In total it checks 49 connected base graphs and enumerates 77,477 hops, recovering the group orders predicted by the theorem. It also independently checks both single-transposition constructions from every elementary square. The observed completion string is `VERIFY_OK connected_base_graphs=49 enumerated_hops=77477`.

These are finite consistency tests, **not** the proof for arbitrary graph order; that proof is the elementary four-cycle and double-cover argument above. Run the verifier using `python verify_leap_prisms.py` in an environment with Python 3 and SymPy installed.

## Relationship to prior work

Schachner and Udell introduced leap groups as groups generated by graph-admissible derangements. Their 2026 manuscript computes some basic examples and explicitly poses determination of the leap groups of cycle prisms and hypercubes (Question 5.4), alongside questions about graph products (Question 5.5); it does **not** give the present Cartesian-prism theorem. Their result for even cycles concerns the leap group of the base cycle itself, not the Cartesian prism of an arbitrary graph.

Clark (2013) studied existence and cycle types of graph derangements, including grid examples, without computing the subgroup generated by all such derangements for prisms. Bucić et al. (2019/2021) studied perfect-matchings and ratios of graph derangements, rather than the group generated by these permutations. Bailey et al. (2020/2021) studied the subgroup generated by derangements *inside a specified transitive permutation group*; this is a different premise from requiring each moved vertex to follow an edge of an arbitrary graph. The present proof is self-contained and needs none of those results beyond the motivating definition.

## Limitations

Connectedness is essential to the stated classification. Only ordinary finite undirected graph-admissible hops are considered, not graph automorphisms, lazy hops, directed graphs, or iterated higher-hop groups. Computations cover finite examples only. The literature comparison is a documented, bounded historical-priority search, not an independently certified originality guarantee; unpublished computations associated with the originating paper could precede this result.
