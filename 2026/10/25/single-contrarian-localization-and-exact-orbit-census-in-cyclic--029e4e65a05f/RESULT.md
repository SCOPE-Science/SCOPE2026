# Single-contrarian localization and exact orbit census in cyclic majority dynamics

## Finding
Fix an integer \(n\ge3\) and distinguish site \(0\) of an \(n\)-cycle. At ordinary sites use binary majority of the site and its two neighbours; at site \(0\), complement that same majority. Every orbit eventually has period at most two, and **only site \(0\) moves in any primitive two-cycle**.

Put \(q(z)=z^2/(1-z)\) and write \([z^m]P(z)\) for coefficient extraction. The exact number of fixed configurations and the number of distinct primitive two-cycles are, respectively,
\[
 A_n=2[z^{n-1}]\frac{q(z)}{1-q(z)^2},\qquad
 B_n=2[z^{n-1}]\frac{q(z)^2}{1-q(z)^2}.
\]
Equivalently, these are coefficients of \(2z^2(1-z)/((1-z-z^2)(1-z+z^2))\) and \(2z^4/((1-z-z^2)(1-z+z^2))\), respectively. There are \(A_n+2B_n\) periodic configurations and the proportion belonging to primitive two-cycles converges to \(2/3\) as \(n\to\infty\).

## Assumptions and scope
The vertex set is \(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z\), with states in \(\{0,1\}\). Let \(M(a,b,c)=1\) when at least two of its three arguments equal \(1\), and \(0\) otherwise. For \(i\ne0\), \((F_nx)_i=M(x_{i-1},x_i,x_{i+1})\), while \((F_nx)_0=1-M(x_{n-1},x_0,x_1)\). The update is synchronous, with all indices modulo \(n\). An orbit is eventually periodic because the state space is finite, but the period bound here is stronger than finiteness. The sole exceptional local rule is the complemented three-input majority at site \(0\).

## Proof
**Frozen pairs.** Whenever two neighbouring ordinary sites have the same bit, neither can ever change: each sees its own bit twice among its three inputs. In a finite orbit, that adjacent equality therefore persists permanently. If an ordinary site is next to a frozen ordinary site, it eventually becomes constant. Indeed, if its bit agrees with the frozen neighbour it is already in a frozen equal pair. If they disagree, then at any update at which the site changes, it necessarily changes to the frozen neighbour's bit and immediately joins a frozen pair. If it never changes, it is constant. Applying this argument successively along the path of ordinary sites \(1,\ldots,n-1\) establishes eventual constancy of all ordinary sites once one frozen pair exists.

**A frozen pair appears.** Suppose initially the ordinary path has no adjacent equal pair. Then its bits alternate. For \(n=3\), the two ordinary bits differ and both become the current hub bit after one update, creating an equal ordinary pair. For \(n\ge4\), all ordinary sites with two ordinary neighbours reverse their bits at the next update. To keep the whole ordinary path alternating, both ordinary endpoints must also reverse; an endpoint reverses only when the exceptional site currently holds the bit opposite to that endpoint. If \(n\) is odd, the endpoints differ, so both cannot reverse and an equal ordinary pair appears in one update. If \(n\) is even, the endpoints agree, say with value \(b\). If the exceptional bit equals \(b\), neither endpoint reverses and an equal ordinary pair appears immediately. Otherwise every ordinary site reverses while the exceptional bit stays \(1-b\), because its two neighbours both equal \(b\). On the next update, both ordinary endpoints agree with the exceptional bit and do not reverse, whereas their neighbouring interior ordinary sites reverse if no equal pair has yet appeared. Hence an equal ordinary pair appears within two updates. This also applies at \(n=4\), where there is one interior ordinary site.

Consequently all ordinary sites become constant after finitely many updates. Let their final ordinary-site bits be \(u_1,\ldots,u_{n-1}\). The remaining exceptional state \(h\) follows \(h\mapsto1-M(u_{n-1},h,u_1)\). If the two endpoint bits agree and equal \(b\), this map is the constant map \(h\mapsto1-b\). If they differ, it is exactly \(h\mapsto1-h\). This proves every orbit enters a fixed point or a two-cycle, and in the latter case only the exceptional site flips.

**Which ordinary strings are recurrent?** In a recurrent state the ordinary bits do not change. In every primitive two-cycle the exceptional bit alternates, so an endpoint ordinary site must agree with its ordinary neighbour in order to remain fixed under both values of the exceptional bit. In a fixed point, the exceptional site's rule forces its two ordinary neighbours to agree with each other and to be opposite to the exceptional bit; again each ordinary endpoint must agree with its next ordinary neighbour. Any other ordinary site is stable if and only if it belongs to a homogeneous run of length at least two. Thus all ordinary-site strings in recurrent states have every maximal constant run of length at least two. Conversely every such ordinary string is fixed under its ordinary-site updates for any exceptional bit. If its two endpoints agree, there is exactly one exceptional-bit choice giving a fixed point, and if they differ there are exactly two exceptional-bit choices giving the two states of a single primitive two-cycle. This establishes a bijective classification.

**Counting and asymptotics.** Each homogeneous ordinary run of length at least two has ordinary generating function \(q(z)=\sum_{j\ge2}z^j=z^2/(1-z)\). There are two choices for the first run's bit, and the endpoint bits agree exactly when the number of runs is odd. Hence the fixed strings of ordinary length \(n-1\) number \(2[z^{n-1}]\sum_{k\ge0}q^{2k+1}\), while the strings giving distinct two-cycles number \(2[z^{n-1}]\sum_{k\ge1}q^{2k}\). This proves the formulas.

The common rational denominator factors as \((1-z-z^2)(1-z+z^2)\). Its unique smallest-modulus zero is \(\rho=(\sqrt5-1)/2\), a simple positive root; the other factor's zeros lie on the unit circle, and the other zero of \(1-z-z^2\) has modulus greater than one. Both numerators are nonzero at \(\rho\), and the quotient of the two generating-function numerators at \(\rho\) is \(q(\rho)=1\). Thus \(B_n/A_n\to1\), whence \(2B_n/(A_n+2B_n)\to2/3\).

## Verification
The standalone script `verify.py` implements the two distinct local update rules, enumerates all states on rings of size \(3\) through \(16\), and independently compares their eventual cycles with the coefficient formulas. The script additionally checks the claimed localization of every primitive two-cycle to site \(0\). Run `python3 verify.py`; a successful run prints `CONTRARIAN_VERIFY_OK`. These finite checks corroborate but are not used as a substitute for the infinite-family proof above.

## Relationship to prior work
The classical period-two property for symmetric majority networks and subsequent convergence bounds concern a uniform majority rule, while the distinguished contrarian violates that symmetry. Benjamini et al., arXiv:1405.2486, analyze consensus and disagreement on ordinary undirected majority networks; Kaaser, Mallmann-Trenn and Natale, arXiv:1508.03519, study voting times under uniform majority dynamics. Nakar and Ron, arXiv:2205.08972, give detailed eventual-periodic structure for uniform one-dimensional majority cellular automata, including radius one. Those descriptions do not directly classify a rule that complements the update at precisely one site. The new point is localization of every nontrivial recurrent oscillation to that site together with the exact fixed/two-cycle census and its \(2/3\) asymptotic split.

## Limitations
This result does not provide the distribution of transient entry times or attraction-basin sizes, nor does it classify multiple contrarians, other local response rules, or nonsynchronous updates. Historical uniqueness remains uncertain despite the documented searches and source comparisons. Independent external validation has not been performed.
