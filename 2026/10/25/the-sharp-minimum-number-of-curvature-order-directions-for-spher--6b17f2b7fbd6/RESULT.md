# The sharp minimum number of curvature-order directions for spherical rigidity in every dimension

## Finding

Let \(d\ge3\), let \(K\subset\mathbb R^d\) be a convex body with closed \(C^2\) boundary \(M\), and fix \(m\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}\). Write \(\sigma_m\) for the \(m\)-th elementary symmetric function of the principal curvatures. For a unit direction \(e\), impose the chord-order condition

\[
\sigma_m(p)\le\sigma_m(q)
\]

whenever \(p,q\in M\), \(p-q=\lambda e\) for some \(\lambda\ge0\), and the open segment joining \(p\) and \(q\) lies in \(\operatorname{int}K\).

**Theorem.** Define \(e_1,\ldots,e_{d-1}\) to be the first \(d-1\) standard basis vectors of \(\mathbb R^d\), and set

\[
v=\left(\frac1{\sqrt3},\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{2}{3(d-1)}},\ldots,\sqrt{\frac{2}{3(d-1)}}}_{d-1\text{ entries}}\right).
\]

If the chord-order condition holds for each of the \(d\) directions \(e_1,\ldots,e_{d-1},v\), then \(K\) is a Euclidean ball. This count is sharp: for any list of \(k\le d-1\) directions, some nonround ellipsoid satisfies all \(k\) conditions with equality.

## Assumptions and scope

The dimension is \(d\ge3\); \(K\) is compact, convex, and has nonempty interior; \(M=\partial K\) is \(C^2\); and the same fixed index \(m\) is used in all directional tests. The curvature convention is the one in Ghomi's theorem: \(\sigma_m\) is the unnormalized elementary symmetric polynomial of the principal curvatures. The result gives one explicit successful \(d\)-tuple and proves that fewer tests can never suffice universally. It does not assert that every \(d\)-tuple works.

## Proof

**1. From curvature order to reflections.** Ghomi's Theorem 1.1 states that the chord-order condition in a single direction \(e\) forces \(M\), and hence \(K\), to be symmetric about some hyperplane orthogonal to \(e\). Apply it to all \(d\) directions. Each corresponding affine reflection preserves \(K\). The volume centroid of \(K\) is fixed by every Euclidean symmetry of \(K\), so it lies on all these hyperplanes. Translate the centroid to the origin; the hyperplanes become the linear hyperplanes \(e_i^\perp\) and \(v^\perp\).

**2. The reflections generate a dense rotation group.** Let \(S_u\) denote reflection in \(u^\perp\), and let \(G\) be generated by \(S_{e_1},\ldots,S_{e_{d-1}},S_v\). Since \(v_1^2=1/3\),

\[
\langle v,S_{e_1}v\rangle=1-2v_1^2=\frac13.
\]

Put \(\theta=\arccos(1/3)\). The product of reflections with normals \(v\) and \(S_{e_1}v\) is a rotation in \(\operatorname{span}\{v,e_1\}\) through angle \(2\theta\). The ratio \(\theta/\pi\) is irrational: otherwise \(2\cos\theta=2/3\) would be a rational algebraic integer, hence an integer, which it is not. Therefore powers of this rotation are dense in the full circle of rotations in that plane.

Let \(H=\overline G\). Its Lie algebra contains the generator \(v\wedge e_1\). Conjugating the continuous rotations by \(S_{e_j}\), for \(2\le j\le d-1\), shows that it also contains \((S_{e_j}v)\wedge e_1\). Since \(S_{e_j}v=v-2v_j e_j\) and every \(v_j\ne0\), subtraction gives \(e_j\wedge e_1\) for each \(2\le j\le d-1\). Subtracting these components from \(v\wedge e_1\), and using \(v_d\ne0\), gives \(e_d\wedge e_1\). The Lie brackets of these generators yield every \(e_i\wedge e_j\), so the Lie algebra of \(H\) is all of \(\mathfrak{so}(d)\). Consequently \(H\) contains \(SO(d)\); because it also contains a reflection, \(H=O(d)\).

The translated body \(K\) is invariant under \(G\), hence under its closure \(O(d)\). An origin-centered convex body invariant under every orthogonal transformation is a Euclidean ball. Translating back proves the first assertion.

**3. Sharpness of the count.** Given any \(k\le d-1\) tested directions \(u_1,\ldots,u_k\), choose a unit vector \(w\) orthogonal to their span. For any \(\eta>0\),

\[
E_\eta=\left\{x\in\mathbb R^d:\ |x|^2+\eta\langle x,w\rangle^2\le1\right\}
\]

is a smooth, strictly convex, nonround ellipsoid of revolution about \(w\). Each \(u_i^\perp\) contains \(w\), so reflection across \(u_i^\perp\) preserves \(E_\eta\). The endpoints of every chord parallel to \(u_i\) are interchanged by this reflection. Since reflections preserve all principal curvatures, the endpoint values of \(\sigma_m\) are equal. Thus all \(k\) conditions hold with equality, proving that fewer than \(d\) directions cannot force a sphere.

## Verification

The direction vector is unit because \(1/3+(d-1)\,2/[3(d-1)]=1\), and every component is nonzero. The irrational-angle step uses only the standard fact that \(\zeta+\zeta^{-1}\) is an algebraic integer when \(\zeta\) is a root of unity. The reflection-group argument is analytic; no finite numerical experiment is used to infer density. The ellipsoid establishes the lower bound for every list of at most \(d-1\) directions and for every fixed \(m\).

## Relationship to prior work

Ghomi's *Mean curvatures and symmetry of convex hypersurfaces* proves the one-direction implication from ordered \(\sigma_m\)-values on parallel chords to a reflection hyperplane, for every \(1\le m\le d-1\). Alfonseca-Cubero, Cordier, Morales-Amaya, and Verdusco Hernández study convex bodies with sequences of axes or hyperplanes of symmetry. The present statement combines the curvature-order hypothesis with an explicit finite generating family of reflections and proves a dimension-uniform sharp count. The literature comparison does not establish uniqueness of this result.

## Limitations

The conclusion is spherical for the displayed tuple; other \(d\)-tuples need not force a sphere. No quantitative stability estimate is proved. The novelty assessment is based on the inspected sources and targeted indexed searches; unindexed or unpublished overlapping work remains a residual priority risk.
