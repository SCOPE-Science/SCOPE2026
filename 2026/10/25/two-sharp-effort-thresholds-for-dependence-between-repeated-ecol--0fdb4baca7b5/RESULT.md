# Two sharp effort thresholds for dependence between repeated ecological detections

## Finding

**Theorem (complete local and global phase diagram).** Let \(N\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)\), with \(\lambda>0\), denote the stable abundance at a randomly selected site. In each of two visits, each of the \(N\) individuals is independently detected with probability \(q\in[0,1]\); all individual-visit trials are mutually independent conditional on \(N\). Let \(Y_j\) indicate detection of at least one individual on visit \(j\), and set \(C_\lambda(q)=\operatorname{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2)\). Then

\[
C_\lambda(q)=e^{-\lambda(2q-q^2)}-e^{-2\lambda q}.
\]

Define \(q_0\) as the unique root in \((1/2,3/4)\) of

\[
\frac{q_0}{1-q_0}+2\log(1-q_0)=0,
\qquad \lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}=\frac{-\log(1-q_0)}{q_0^2}.
\]

For every \(\lambda>\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}\), denote by \(q_-(\lambda)<q_0<q_+(\lambda)\) the two solutions in \((0,1)\) of

\[
\frac{-\log(1-q)}{q^2}=\lambda.
\]

There is a unique \(\lambda_{\mathrm{glob}}>\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}\) satisfying

\[
C_{\lambda_{\mathrm{glob}}}(q_-(\lambda_{\mathrm{glob}}))
=C_{\lambda_{\mathrm{glob}}}(1).
\]

The complete classification is as follows.

1. For \(0<\lambda\le\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}\), \(C_\lambda\) is strictly increasing on \([0,1]\), and its unique global maximizer is \(q=1\). At equality, it has a single horizontal tangent at the interior point \(q_0\).
2. For \(\lambda>\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}\), the function has exactly one strict interior local maximum, at \(q_-\), and exactly one strict interior local minimum, at \(q_+\). It increases on \((0,q_-)\), decreases on \((q_-,q_+)\), and increases on \((q_+,1)\).
3. For \(\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}<\lambda<\lambda_{\mathrm{glob}}\), the unique global maximizer remains \(q=1\). At \(\lambda=\lambda_{\mathrm{glob}}\), the two distinct points \(q_-\) and \(1\) tie for the global maximum. For \(\lambda>\lambda_{\mathrm{glob}}\), the unique global maximizer is \(q_-\).
4. As \(\lambda\to\infty\), the unique maximizing detection probability obeys

\[
q_-(\lambda)\sim\lambda^{-1},\qquad
\max_{0\le q\le1}C_\lambda(q)\sim e^{-2}\lambda^{-1},
\]

whereas perfect per-individual detection gives \(C_\lambda(1)=e^{-\lambda}(1-e^{-\lambda})\).

Numerically, \(q_0\approx0.715331862959\), \(\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}\approx2.455407482284\), \(\lambda_{\mathrm{glob}}\approx2.610227149204\), and \(q_-(\lambda_{\mathrm{glob}})\approx0.563708374010\). The exact definitions, not the decimals, specify the thresholds.

## Assumptions and scope

The population size is a closed, nonnegative Poisson count across independent sites; it is shared by the two visits to each site. Detections are binary at the *visit* level, and no false positives are allowed. The common probability \(q\) is applied independently to each individual on each visit. This differs from a model that draws a fresh Poisson population independently for each visit. Parameter \(\lambda\) is fixed while \(q\) varies. The optimization concerns **the unstandardized covariance of the two binary visit indicators**, an observable measure of abundance-induced repeat-visit dependence; it does not optimize detection probability, Fisher information, sample size, survey time or economic cost.

This statistic is ecologically motivated: positive within-site dependence between replicated detection/non-detection records is generated by heterogeneity in the number of available individuals. At high mean abundance, almost every fully detecting visit reports presence, so such binary records conceal the abundance variation. Intermediate detectability can generate stronger repeat-visit covariance than perfect detectability.

## Proof

**1. Covariance identity.** Conditional on \(N=n\), the two visit indicators are independent Bernoulli variables with common success probability \(p_n=1-(1-q)^n\). Therefore \(\operatorname{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2)=\operatorname{Var}(p_N)\). The Poisson probability-generating function is \(\mathbb E[s^N]=e^{\lambda(s-1)}\) for \(0\le s\le1\). Thus

\[
\operatorname{Var}(p_N)
=\mathbb E[(1-q)^{2N}]-\mathbb E[(1-q)^N]^2
=e^{-\lambda(2q-q^2)}-e^{-2\lambda q}.
\]

The formula is continuous at \(q=0,1\) (with \(s^0=1\) even at \(s=0\)). In particular \(C_\lambda(0)=0\) and \(C_\lambda(1)>0\).

**2. Exact derivative sign.** Differentiating yields

\[
\frac{dC_\lambda}{dq}
=2\lambda e^{-\lambda(2q-q^2)}
\left(e^{-\lambda q^2}-(1-q)\right).
\]

For \(0<q<1\), its sign is exactly the sign of

\[
R(q)-\lambda,\qquad R(q)=\frac{-\log(1-q)}{q^2}.
\]

The function \(R\) diverges to \(+\infty\) at both endpoints and

\[
R'(q)=\frac{q/(1-q)+2\log(1-q)}{q^3}.
\]

Writing \(H(q)=q/(1-q)+2\log(1-q)\), we have

\[
H'(q)=\frac{2q-1}{(1-q)^2},\qquad H(0)=0.
\]

Thus \(H\) decreases strictly up to \(1/2\), then increases strictly. Moreover, \(H(1/2)=1-2\log2<0\) and \(H(3/4)=3-2\log4>0\). It follows that \(H\) has exactly one zero \(q_0\in(1/2,3/4)\). Hence \(R\) has a unique strict minimum at \(q_0\), proving the asserted local-extrema trichotomy, including the equality case. The lower bound \(\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}>2\) follows because

\[
\frac{d}{dq}\bigl[-\log(1-q)-2q^2\bigr]
=\frac{(2q-1)^2}{1-q}\ge0,
\]

and the bracket starts at zero and is strictly positive for every \(q>0\).

**3. Exactly one global crossover.** For \(\lambda>\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}\), the only candidates for a global maximum are the interior local maximum \(q_-(\lambda)\) and the endpoint \(q=1\). Define their positive ratio

\[
G(\lambda)=\frac{C_\lambda(q_-(\lambda))}{C_\lambda(1)}.
\]

At the stationary interior point, \(e^{-\lambda q_-^2}=1-q_-\). Using the covariance formula and differentiating with respect to \(\lambda\) while holding \(q\) fixed gives

\[
\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\log C_\lambda(q)\right|_{q=q_-}
=-2q_-+\frac{q_-^2}{1-e^{-\lambda q_-^2}}=-q_-.
\]

The chain-rule term from differentiating \(q_-(\lambda)\) vanishes because \(\partial C_\lambda/\partial q=0\). Since \(C_\lambda(1)=e^{-\lambda}(1-e^{-\lambda})\),

\[
\frac{d}{d\lambda}\log G(\lambda)
=1-q_-(\lambda)-\frac{1}{e^\lambda-1}
>\frac14-\frac1{e^2-1}>0.
\]

The first strict inequality uses \(q_-<q_0<3/4\) and \(\lambda>\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}>2\). As \(\lambda\downarrow\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}\), \(q_-\to q_0\), and strict increase of \(C_{\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}}\) implies \(G(\lambda)\to C_{\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}}(q_0)/C_{\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}}(1)<1\). Meanwhile, \(q_-\to0\) as \(\lambda\to\infty\) and the stationary equation implies \(\lambda q_-\to1\). Therefore

\[
C_\lambda(q_-)
=e^{-2\lambda q_-}\left(e^{\lambda q_-^2}-1\right)
\sim\frac{e^{-2}}{\lambda},
\]

whereas \(C_\lambda(1)\sim e^{-\lambda}\), so \(G(\lambda)\to\infty\). Continuity and strict monotonicity prove existence and uniqueness of \(\lambda_{\mathrm{glob}}\) and the complete global classification. The same calculation proves the asymptotics in item 4.

**4. Exact strict witness.** At mean abundance \(\lambda=3\), choosing \(q=1/2\) gives \(C_3(1/2)=e^{-9/4}-e^{-3}\), whereas \(C_3(1)=e^{-3}-e^{-6}\). Their difference is \(e^{-3}(e^{3/4}-2+e^{-3})>0\), since \(e^{3/4}>1+3/4+(3/4)^2/2=65/32>2\). Thus imperfect detection truly can produce greater repeat-visit covariance than perfect detection without relying on decimals.

## Relationship to prior work

The Royle–Nichols abundance-induced heterogeneity model supplies the underlying visit-level detection mechanism and the dependence of site-level detectability on latent abundance; neither the Poisson prior nor that mechanism is claimed as new. The Donovan–Hines 2007 chapter gives its explicit conditional binary-visit likelihood and Poisson mixing. Moore and colleagues' 2014 work optimizes the *number and duration of surveys* under stochastic detection rates, a different objective and observation model. Wenger and Freeman's 2008 zero-inflated abundance work concerns occurrence/abundance estimation rather than this covariance maximization. The present claim is the **two-transition phase diagram for maximization of the covariance**, including a unique interior local-extremum onset, a distinct global-maximum crossover and the high-abundance scaling. Its relationship to general parameter-dependent extrema theorems is elementary calculus, but no equivalent concrete ecological-statistic classification was identified in the inspected material.

## Limitations

This is a theoretical sensitivity analysis, not a recommendation to deliberately reduce detection or a claim of optimal survey design. Covariance maximization alone does not account for cost, estimation variance, true species occupancy, false-positive records, temporal population turnover, unequal detection probabilities across visits, observer effects or field constraints. Standardized correlation behaves differently (with perfect detection giving correlation one). The original 2003 research article was accessible at the abstract and bibliographic level but its full text was not inspectable; a pre-existing derivation of these extrema in that text or wider occupancy literature remains a priority risk. Independent external mathematical review has not been performed.
