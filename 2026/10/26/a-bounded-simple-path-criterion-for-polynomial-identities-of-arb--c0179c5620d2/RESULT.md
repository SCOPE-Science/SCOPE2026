# A bounded-simple-path criterion for polynomial identities of arbitrary quiver path algebras

## Finding

Let \(F\) be any field and let \(Q\) be a quiver, possibly infinite. Its path algebra \(FQ\) is a polynomial-identity (PI) algebra if and only if both conditions hold:

1. Every vertex belongs to at most one distinct oriented simple cycle.
2. There is a uniform integer \(L\ge 0\) such that every directed path whose vertices are pairwise distinct has length at most \(L\).

This criterion gives a uniform identity bound. If the two conditions hold and \(N=(L+1)^2\), then the standard polynomial
\[
S_{2N}(x_1,\ldots,x_{2N})=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{2N}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)x_{\sigma(1)}\cdots x_{\sigma(2N)}
\]
belongs to \(\operatorname{Id}(FQ)\). The bound is sufficient and is not claimed to be minimal.

In particular, finite GK-dimension of a Leavitt path algebra is not enough to guarantee that the associated path algebra is PI. The infinite directed ray satisfies the finite-GK-dimension criterion for its Leavitt path algebra, but its path algebra is not PI because its vertex-simple paths have unbounded length.

## Assumptions and scope

A path is a finite directed path, including paths of length zero at vertices. A vertex-simple path is one whose vertices are pairwise distinct. An oriented simple cycle has no repeated vertices other than its initial/final vertex. The path algebra has the usual path basis and concatenation multiplication, with a product equal to zero when concatenation is not defined. The statement applies over every field, including positive characteristic, and to quivers with infinitely many vertices or arrows. No finiteness of the whole quiver is assumed.

## Proof

### Necessity of the cycle condition

Suppose two distinct oriented simple cycles share a vertex \(v\). Rotate both cycles to start at \(v\), and call the resulting path words \(c_1,c_2\). Neither word can be a proper prefix of the other: that would make the longer simple cycle revisit \(v\) before its end. Thus \(c_1,c_2\) form a prefix code, and distinct words in them give distinct paths. The subalgebra generated by \(c_1,c_2\) is therefore a free associative algebra on two generators. A free associative algebra on two generators has no nonzero polynomial identity, so neither does \(FQ\).

### Necessity of a uniform bound on vertex-simple paths

If vertex-simple paths have unbounded length, then for every \(n\) the quiver contains a linearly oriented path on \(n\) vertices. Its path algebra is a subalgebra isomorphic to the upper-triangular matrix algebra \(UT_n(F)\). The intersection of the polynomial identities of all \(UT_n(F)\) is zero. Indeed, for a nonzero polynomial choose a word \(w\) of maximal degree \(d\) with nonzero coefficient, and evaluate each variable \(x_i\) as the sum of matrix units \(E_{j,j+1}\) at positions where the \(j\)-th letter of \(w\) is \(x_i\), in \(UT_{d+1}(F)\). The \((1,d+1)\) entry of the evaluation is exactly the coefficient of \(w\), so the polynomial is not an identity. Hence \(FQ\) cannot be PI.

### A uniform bound on path supports

Now assume the two stated conditions. Fix \(L\) as in condition 2. Every simple cycle has at most \(L+1\) vertices. Consider the finite subquiver traced by any one path. Its strongly connected components are either single vertices or single simple cycles: a larger component or two distinct cycles in one component would force a vertex to lie on two distinct simple cycles. The component sequence along the path has no repetitions. Selecting a simple directed segment through each component produces a vertex-simple path, so there are at most \(L+1\) components. Each component has at most \(L+1\) vertices. Thus every finite path has at most \(N=(L+1)^2\) distinct vertices.

### The standard identity

It remains to prove that \(S_{2N}\) is an identity. By multilinearity, it suffices to evaluate it on basis paths \(p_1,\ldots,p_{2N}\). If two paths coincide, alternation gives zero. If every permuted product is zero, the evaluation is also zero. Otherwise reorder the paths so that \(p_1\cdots p_{2N}\ne0\).

Form the finite Swan quiver \(\Sigma\) whose arrows are the paths \(p_i\), directed from their starting to their ending vertices. It has \(2N\) arrows, and every endpoint lies in the support of the nonzero concatenated path. Hence \(\Sigma\) has at most \(N\) vertices, so \(|\Sigma_1|\ge 2|\Sigma_0|\). Swan's Eulerian-path theorem states that, for each pair of endpoints, the numbers of Eulerian paths of the two permutation signs are equal.

The following consequence of the cycle condition identifies the terms being counted: for fixed endpoints, all Eulerian paths in \(\Sigma\) represent the same concatenated path in \(Q\). Prove this by induction on the number of arrows of \(\Sigma\). If every arrow of \(\Sigma\) lies on a directed cycle, the existence of an Eulerian path and a return path for every arrow make \(\Sigma\) strongly connected. Choose a directed cycle in \(\Sigma\); replacing its arrows by their corresponding paths in \(Q\) gives a closed path, whose support must lie on one simple cycle \(C\) of \(Q\), by the no-shared-cycle hypothesis. If some arrow \(p_i\) of \(\Sigma\) were not a subpath of \(C\) (a length-zero vertex path at a vertex of \(C\) is allowed), strong connectivity supplies paths from \(C\) to the source of \(p_i\) and from its target back to \(C\). Together with \(p_i\), these would give a second simple cycle through a vertex of \(C\), a contradiction. Hence every \(p_i\) is a subpath of \(C\). All Eulerian paths use each \(p_i\) once, so their total path length is fixed; on an oriented simple cycle, a path of fixed length and fixed endpoints is unique. Otherwise choose an arrow \(a\) of \(\Sigma\) lying on no directed cycle and write an Eulerian path as \(\gamma_1 a \gamma_2\). If deleting \(a\) did not disconnect the support graph, the prefix and suffix would share a vertex and yield a return path from the target of \(a\) to its source, putting \(a\) on a cycle. Thus deletion separates the support into two pieces. Every Eulerian path factors into a trail in the first piece, then \(a\), then a trail in the second; the induction hypothesis fixes both factors, so the full concatenation is unique.

Consequently, the positive-sign and negative-sign terms in the evaluation of \(S_{2N}\) are equal in number and represent the same path, and their signed sum is zero over every field. Therefore \(S_{2N}\in\operatorname{Id}(FQ)\), proving sufficiency and the claimed identity bound.

### Leavitt-path-algebra consequence

Rangaswamy's finite-GK-dimension characterization for Leavitt path algebras requires the cycle relation to be a partial order of finite height; in particular, distinct cycles are vertex-disjoint. It does not bound the lengths of acyclic paths. The infinite directed ray has no cycles, so its Leavitt path algebra has GK-dimension zero under that characterization, while its path algebra fails condition 2 and is not PI. The extra uniform path-length condition is therefore necessary for the infinite-quiver analogue.

## Verification

The proof treats arbitrary fields and all finite-path lengths symbolically. The necessity argument uses explicit matrix-unit evaluations, not a finite enumeration. The sufficiency argument reduces every evaluation to the finite Swan quiver associated with the chosen basis paths and uses the Eulerian-path cancellation theorem. The stated identity degree is a proved upper bound only; no optimality claim is made.

## Relationship to prior work

Cerulli Irelli, De Loera Chávez, and Pascucci prove that a **finite** quiver has a PI path algebra exactly when each vertex lies in at most one oriented cycle. Their paper also proves that the infinite directed ray is not PI and explicitly asks whether infinite quivers with finite-GK-dimension Leavitt path algebras are PI. The criterion above extends the finite-quiver statement to arbitrary quivers by adding the necessary and sufficient uniform bound on vertex-simple path length, and gives an explicit uniform standard-identity bound.

Rangaswamy characterizes finite GK-dimension of Leavitt path algebras through disjoint cycles and finite height of the cycle order. That condition alone does not control arbitrarily long acyclic paths, which is exactly the missing obstruction here. Swan's graph-theoretic Eulerian-path cancellation theorem supplies the combinatorial cancellation step; the original finite-quiver proof is the closest methodological antecedent.

## Limitations

The bound \(2(L+1)^2\) is not asserted to be sharp. The theorem classifies PI-ness and gives a sufficient standard identity, but it does not compute the minimal degree of a polynomial identity for each infinite quiver. The originality assessment is relative to the sources and database searches recorded in the review; an unindexed equivalent generalization remains possible. The original Swan article was not directly accessible during this run, so its theorem was checked through the full statement reproduced as Theorem 4.4 in the inspected 2025 paper.
