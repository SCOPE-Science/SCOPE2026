# A functional Gaussian law inside the superdiffusive persistent-walk phase

## Finding
Fix \(c\in(0,1]\) and \(\alpha\in(0,1)\). Let \(V_0\in\{-1,+1\}\) have any distribution, independent of the subsequent independent reversal trials. At integer step \(k\ge1\), set \(V_k=-V_{k-1}\) with probability \(p_k=c k^{-\alpha}\), and \(V_k=V_{k-1}\) otherwise. Put \(X_0=0\) and \(X_n=\sum_{k=1}^n V_k\). Extend \(t\mapsto X_{\lfloor t\rfloor}\) by linear interpolation, and write \(\widetilde X_N(t)\) for its value at \(Nt\).

**Theorem.** As integer \(N\to\infty\), the processes
\[
N^{-(1+\alpha)/2}\widetilde X_N(t)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
B\!\left(\frac{t^{1+\alpha}}{c(1+\alpha)}\right)
\]
converge weakly in \(C([0,T])\) for every finite \(T>0\), and hence in the topology of uniform convergence on compact time intervals. Here \(B\) is standard Brownian motion. In particular, the limiting process is Gaussian with independent, generally nonstationary increments and
\[
\operatorname{Cov}(G(s),G(t))=
\frac{\min(s,t)^{1+\alpha}}{c(1+\alpha)}.
\]
The limit holds for either deterministic starting velocity and for arbitrary mixtures thereof; no initial-velocity centering is necessary.

## Assumptions and scope
The reversal trials are independent across different times and independent of \(V_0\). The parameter inequalities ensure \(0<p_k\le1\) for every \(k\), including the boundary \(c=1\). Motion takes discrete time and unit steps in one spatial dimension. The normalization depends on \(\alpha\) and the operational clock depends on \(c\). The claim is a weak functional limit for each fixed \(0<\alpha<1\), not a uniform theorem as \(\alpha\uparrow1\).

## Proof
Let \(\mathcal F_k\) be generated by the initial velocity and the first \(k\) reversal trials. The exact velocity regression is
\[
\mathbb E[V_k\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]=(1-2p_k)V_{k-1}.
\]
Consequently define the bounded martingale differences and their weighted sum by
\[
\eta_k=V_k-(1-2p_k)V_{k-1},\qquad
M_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\eta_k}{2p_k}.
\]
Conditionally on the past, \(\eta_k\) has mean zero and variance \(4p_k(1-p_k)\). Hence its predictable quadratic variation is exactly the deterministic quantity
\[
\langle M\rangle_n=
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1-p_k}{p_k}
=\frac1c\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^{\alpha}-n.
\]
The uniform Riemann-sum estimate supplies, for every \(T>0\),
\[
\sup_{0\le t\le T}\left|
N^{-(1+\alpha)}\langle M\rangle_{\lfloor Nt\rfloor}
-\frac{t^{1+\alpha}}{c(1+\alpha)}
\right|\longrightarrow0.
\]
Also \(|\eta_k/(2p_k)|\le1/p_k=k^{\alpha}/c\). For \(k\le NT\), the maximal increment divided by \(N^{(1+\alpha)/2}\) is bounded by \(T^{\alpha}N^{(\alpha-1)/2}/c\), which tends to zero. Thus the conditional Lindeberg condition holds deterministically. The martingale functional central limit theorem gives
\[
N^{-(1+\alpha)/2}M_{\lfloor Nt\rfloor}
\Longrightarrow B\!\left(\frac{t^{1+\alpha}}{c(1+\alpha)}\right)
\]
in the càdlàg path space; since all normalized jumps vanish, its linearly interpolated version has the same continuous limit.

It remains to compare this martingale to the physical displacement. The identity
\[
\frac{\eta_k}{2p_k}
=V_{k-1}+\frac{V_k-V_{k-1}}{2p_k}
\]
is exact. Let \(w_k=1/(2p_k)=k^{\alpha}/(2c)\) and \(R_j=\sum_{k=1}^j w_k(V_k-V_{k-1})\). Summation by parts gives
\[
R_j=w_jV_j-w_1V_0-
\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}(w_{k+1}-w_k)V_k.
\]
Because the positive weights \(w_k\) are increasing and \(|V_k|=1\),
\[
|R_j|\le w_j+w_1+\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}(w_{k+1}-w_k)
=2w_j=\frac{j^{\alpha}}c.
\]
Finally
\[
M_j=\sum_{k=1}^{j}V_{k-1}+R_j
=X_j-V_j+V_0+R_j,
\]
so, uniformly for \(0\le j\le NT\),
\[
|X_j-M_j|\le2+\frac{(NT)^{\alpha}}c.
\]
The right side is \(o(N^{(1+\alpha)/2})\) exactly because \(\alpha<1\). Linear interpolation preserves the same uniform bound up to vanishing endpoint terms. Slutsky's theorem in the continuous-path space completes the proof. The displayed covariance follows from the standard Brownian covariance formula.

## Verification
All three critical ingredients are explicit algebraic or deterministic bounds: the exact predictable bracket, the maximal normalized martingale increment, and the pathwise summation-by-parts remainder. In particular the proof uses neither finite enumeration nor simulations, and no numerical experiment is needed to establish the functional limit. The inequality \(\alpha<1\) is used twice, in the martingale Lindeberg estimate and in the vanishing displacement/martingale error. The result is not extrapolated to the marginal regime \(\alpha=1\).

## Relationship to prior work
Pradhan, Roy, and Ray [1] specify precisely this velocity reversal model and prove the superdiffusive mean-square displacement scaling proportional to \(n^{1+\alpha}\), including its leading constant for \(0<\alpha<1\); those statements are prior. They also analyze correlation functions, persistence and a transition to ballistic behavior at \(\alpha=1\). The present theorem identifies the complete *functional distributional* limit and asymptotically independent macroscopic increments. Second-moment asymptotics do not imply Gaussianity or functional convergence. The martingale functional central limit theorem is a classical tool and is not itself new. Established functional-limit studies of other persistent-random-walk constructions form a further possible source of implicit coverage; no blanket priority or independent audit is asserted here.

## Limitations
This result assumes independent reversal trials, a deterministic power-law decay of reversal probabilities and \(0<\alpha<1\). It makes no claim about nonindependent flips, random environments, \(\alpha\ge1\), the crossover as \(\alpha\) approaches one jointly with sample size, or a uniform large-deviation principle. The standard martingale invariance principle is used as a cited classical theorem, not reproved from first principles.
