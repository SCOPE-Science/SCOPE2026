# All-prefix optimal EFX for equal-duration jobs and its four-job perturbation barrier

## Finding
Let \(m\ge 1\) identical machines receive a finite online sequence of half-open intervals, in nondecreasing order of start time, with identical positive duration \(\ell\) and unit value to every machine. Then an irrevocable algorithm accepts, after **every prefix**, as many intervals as an unconstrained offline optimum for that prefix, while its feasible allocation is envy-free up to **any** interval (EFX) after every prefix. No revocations or machine reassignment are needed.

This zero-price guarantee is discontinuous in duration dispersion for two machines. For every real \(\varepsilon>0\) there is a four-interval, unit-value instance whose durations all lie in \([1,1+\varepsilon]\) such that the unrestricted optimum accepts four intervals but every EF1 (equivalently EFX) schedule accepts at most three. A three-interval instance cannot exhibit an efficiency loss on two machines. Thus the two-machine worst-case offline price of fairness, and the optimal achievable online fair competitive ratio, equal \(1\) at exactly common duration, but are **at least** \(4/3\) over the class with duration ratio at most \(1+\varepsilon\), for every positive \(\varepsilon\). No equality with \(4/3\) for the perturbed class is asserted.

## Assumptions and scope
An interval is \([s,s+\ell)\) with rational \(s\ge0\) and common rational \(\ell>0\), and intervals arrive in nondecreasing order of \(s\); ties may be resolved arbitrarily. The algorithm sees an interval's endpoints when it arrives, must decide immediately whether to accept, and never changes accepted assignments. Every accepted interval has common additive utility \(1\) to each machine. EF1/EFX concerns the full cumulative collection of jobs assigned to each machine, not merely jobs currently running. The optimum for any prefix is the maximum cardinality subset that can be scheduled feasibly on \(m\) machines without a fairness constraint. The perturbation example has rational endpoints and positive durations; the assertion holds for real \(\varepsilon\) by choosing a rational perturbation small enough.

## Proof
**Algorithm.** Maintain the accepted start times and a total acceptance counter \(a\). When \([s,s+\ell)\) arrives, reject it if \(m\) previously accepted intervals are active at \(s\); otherwise accept it on machine \(1+(a\bmod m)\), then increment \(a\). Already ended intervals are never revoked. By sorting start times, a queue of currently active accepted intervals implements the test in amortized constant time per arrival (apart from storage).

**Feasibility and prefixwise EFX.** Suppose the incoming interval becomes accepted as accepted interval number \(a+1\). If the preceding interval with the same cyclic machine label were still active, then this predecessor and the \(m-1\) accepted intervals between it and the new interval in acceptance order would all be active: those intervening starts are no earlier, and all durations equal \(\ell\). The acceptance test would therefore see at least \(m\) active accepted intervals, contradiction. Hence the chosen machine is free. Cyclic labels give per-machine cumulative counts differing by at most \(1\) after every prefix. Identical unit values make this condition exactly EF1 and EFX.

**Prefixwise cardinality optimality.** Fix any prefix and order its intervals by their arrival order. Among all cardinality-optimal feasible schedules, choose one agreeing with the greedy acceptance/rejection decisions for the longest initial segment. Let \(I=[s,s+\ell)\) be the first disagreement. If the greedy rule rejects \(I\), its \(m\) active earlier accepted jobs are in the chosen optimum by initial agreement; they overlap \(I\) at \(s\), so the optimum cannot accept it. Thus the greedy rule accepts \(I\) while the selected optimum rejects it. In the optimum, an assignment of the agreed earlier accepted intervals has a machine free at \(s\), because at most \(m-1\) earlier accepted intervals are active. Insert \(I\) on this machine. If it conflicts with a future assigned interval, remove the **first** such interval \(J\). Then \(s_J\ge s\), and since durations are equal, \(I\) ends no later than \(J\); every interval scheduled after \(J\) on that machine still starts after \(I\) ends. The modified assignment remains feasible, has the same cardinality, and agrees on one additional decision, a contradiction. If there is no conflicting future interval, adding \(I\) strictly improves the optimum, also impossible. Hence the greedy decisions are cardinality optimal, for every finite prefix.

**Arbitrarily small length perturbations.** Fix \(\varepsilon>0\) and choose rational \(0<\delta<\min\{1,\varepsilon/2\}\). Consider the three mutually nonoverlapping unit intervals \([0,1),[1,2),[2,3)\) and the interval \([1-\delta,2+\delta)\), of duration \(1+2\delta<1+\varepsilon\). The fourth interval overlaps all three unit intervals. Two machines can run all four only by placing the fourth alone on one machine and all three unit intervals on the other. This produces loads \((1,3)\), violating EF1. Rejecting the fourth interval and distributing the remaining three with loads \((2,1)\) is EFX, proving fair optimum \(3\) and unrestricted optimum \(4\). This instance bounds from below any online fair competitive ratio, irrespective of how its algorithm decides.

**Four jobs are minimal.** Take any instance of at most three jobs and two machines, and let \(q\le3\) be its unrestricted maximum scheduled count. If \(q\le2\), distribute the selected jobs between distinct machines. If \(q=3\), the intersection graph on these three accepted intervals has no triangle, since all three could not overlap and still fit on two machines. A triangle-free three-vertex graph contains a nonadjacent pair. Put that pair on one machine and the remaining interval on the other; the resulting \((2,1)\) assignment is feasible and EFX. Thus no instance with fewer than four jobs has a positive unweighted EF1 price.

## Verification
A standalone exact checker, `check.py`, enumerates \(19{,}305\) equal-duration time-grid instances for one through three machines and up to eight intervals, comparing every prefix-safe cyclic-greedy final acceptance count with an independently enumerated unrestricted maximum. It also enumerates all possible assignments of the perturbation witness using rational arithmetic at four distinct positive perturbation sizes, and all two-machine instances with at most three intervals on a small rational endpoint grid (\(455\) instances). Its successful output is:

```
equal_length_exhaustive_instances 19305
exact_fractional_perturbations 4 max_unrestricted 4 max_ef1 3
three_job_minimality_checks 455
```

These finite checks corroborate the analytic proof but do not replace its universal quantifiers.

## Relationship to prior work
Borst, Shahkarami and Vaish (2026) introduce the online/offline EF1 interval-scheduling framework and establish a general unweighted offline price of fairness \(3/2\) and deterministic online ratio \(2-1/m\), with constructions allowing interval-length variation. Their paper also gives lower bounds for equal-duration *weighted* instances. The present result concerns the jointly unweighted and equal-duration regime, with an irrevocable prefix-optimal allocation, and shows an arbitrarily small relaxation of the duration equality admits a strict two-machine EF1 loss. It does not claim the general guarantees or static equitable-coloring results as new.

An older equitable-coloring theorem for claw-free graphs implies that a feasible set of equal-duration intervals can be re-colored with balanced static class sizes; that does not establish online cardinality-optimal irrevocable assignments at every prefix. Weighted equal-duration online scheduling was previously studied by Fung, Poon and Yung (2012), but a full-text equivalence comparison was unavailable; its abstract describes weighted competitive bounds, not the combined prefixwise fairness and perturbation statement.

## Limitations
The perturbed-case bound is a lower bound only; the exact competitive ratio for nonidentical, nearly equal durations is not determined. The equal-duration theorem uses equal **weights** as well as lengths and requires start-time arrival order. It does not apply to arbitrary orders, unequal valuations, changing machine eligibility, or resource preemption. Independent verification and a complete historical priority assessment have not been performed.
