# Connected-backbone exchange thresholds distinguish the Shrikhande and rook graphs

## Finding
Let \(S\) be the Shrikhande graph and \(R=K_4\square K_4\) the four-by-four rook graph. These are the two nonisomorphic strongly regular graphs with parameters \((16,6,2,2)\), hence have identical adjacency spectra. For a graph \(G\), write \(\mathcal C_k(G)\) for the graph whose vertices are the connected dominating subsets of \(V(G)\) of size exactly \(k\), with two subsets adjacent precisely when their symmetric difference contains two vertices (one selected vertex is replaced).

**Theorem.** Both graphs have connected domination number four. Their minimum exchange graphs and their first two higher-cardinality layers satisfy:

| Graph | \(k\) | States | Edges | Connected components | Eccentricities when \(k=5\) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- | --- |
| Shrikhande \(S\) | 4 | 32 | 0 | \(32K_1\) | — |
| Shrikhande \(S\) | 5 | 672 | 5,136 | one component | all 672 have eccentricity 5 |
| Shrikhande \(S\) | 6 | 4,064 | 74,304 | one component | — |
| Rook \(R\) | 4 | 8 | 0 | \(8K_1\) | — |
| Rook \(R\) | 5 | 384 | 2,112 | two components of order 192 | 96 have eccentricity 6; 288 have eccentricity 7 |
| Rook \(R\) | 6 | 3,744 | 63,024 | one component | — |

In particular, the least cardinality \(k\ge\gamma_c(G)\) for which the fixed-cardinality one-vertex-replacement graph is connected is **five for the Shrikhande graph and six for the rook graph**. The diameter of \(\mathcal C_5(S)\) is five (and its radius is five); the two components of \(\mathcal C_5(R)\) are isomorphic, each with radius six and diameter seven. The degrees in the former are 11 for 192 states and 17 for 480 states; in the latter they are 9 for 288 states and 17 for 96 states.

Furthermore, the 32 minimum connected dominating sets of \(S\) are exactly its induced four-vertex claws, while the eight minimum connected dominating sets of \(R\) are its four entire rows and four entire columns. The 384 five-vertex connected dominating sets of \(R\) admit a complete two-class classification, with 192 row-covering states and 192 column-covering states; these are precisely its two reconfiguration components.

## Assumptions and scope
All graphs are finite, undirected and simple. The Shrikhande graph has vertex set \(\mathbb Z_4^2\), with vertices joined when their difference belongs to \(\{(1,0),(-1,0),(0,1),(0,-1),(1,1),(-1,-1)\}\), coordinates taken modulo four. The rook graph has vertex set \(\{0,1,2,3\}^2\), with distinct vertices adjacent when they agree in one coordinate. Thus the graphs and every vertex label are completely specified.

A set \(D\) is dominating when \(D\cup\bigcup_{v\in D}N(v)=V(G)\) and connected dominating when, additionally, \(G[D]\) is connected. The parameter \(\gamma_c(G)\) is the minimum size of such a set. The graph \(\mathcal C_k(G)\) has only **exactly-\(k\)** connected dominating sets: no intermediate change of cardinality is allowed. Its edges are unrestricted token jumps, not necessarily token slides and not token addition/removal. Eccentricity is evaluated inside the component of each vertex. The findings concern these two graphs, not an asserted classification of all strongly regular graphs.

## Proof
**1. The two finite graphs and the small-set lower bound.** The definitions above specify adjacency by simple modular arithmetic and coordinate equality. Every vertex has degree six. Every pair of distinct vertices, adjacent or not, has exactly two common neighbors, which proves their common strongly regular parameters and their common spectrum \(\{6,2^{(6)},(-2)^{(9)}\}\). The local neighborhood graph is a hexagon in \(S\) and two disjoint triangles in \(R\), so they are nonisomorphic.

For a set encoded by an integer mask \(m\in\{1,\ldots,2^{16}-1\}\), compute its domination mask as the bitwise union of \(m\) and the neighborhood masks of its set bits. Starting from the lowest set bit, repeatedly adjoining its set-bit neighbors gives the entire component of the induced graph containing that vertex. A mask is a connected dominating set if and only if the domination mask has all 16 bits and the final reachability mask equals \(m\). The attached two complete implementations enumerate **every** such mask for cardinalities one through six and give, in increasing cardinality order, the exact state-count vectors
\[
S:\quad(0,0,0,32,672,4064),\qquad
R:\quad(0,0,0,8,384,3744).
\]
This exhaustive finite classification proves the lower bound four, the size-four equality counts, and the higher-layer state counts without sampling or heuristic search. In particular, each connected three-vertex set dominates at most 13 of the 16 vertices in both graphs; the exhaustive triple counts check this bound directly.

**2. Geometry at the optimum.** The six neighbors of every Shrikhande vertex form a cycle. Choosing alternating three neighbors and their common center makes an induced claw. The two choices of alternating triples at each of the 16 centers give 32 distinct, connected dominating four-sets. Exhaustive checking of the \(\binom{16}{4}\) four-subsets establishes that no other connected dominating four-set exists. In the rook graph, a selected set dominates all cells if and only if every row or every column contains a selected cell. Four cells covering every row and inducing a connected rook graph must occupy one common column; dually the four-cell column-covering case is a whole row. There are precisely eight such sets. Both optimum exchange graphs have zero edges by exhaustive neighbor comparison (also, the described minimum structures cannot be obtained from one another by changing only one cell).

**3. The rook five-set obstruction.** Interpret a rook-graph vertex \((i,j)\) as the edge between row vertex \(i\) and column vertex \(j\) in \(K_{4,4}\). A nonempty set induces a connected rook subgraph if and only if its chosen edges form a connected edge-induced support in the bipartite graph. Domination means that all four row vertices or all four column vertices are incident to chosen edges. For five chosen edges covering all rows, if \(c\) columns occur, connectivity requires at least \(4+c-1\) edges. Therefore \(c\le2\); the simple bipartite incidence graph cannot have five distinct edges in only one column, hence \(c=2\). With two columns, its five edges form a tree, with exactly one row incident to both columns (the bridge row) and the other three rows incident to exactly one chosen column. Such a state is specified by the two columns, the bridge row, and three independent binary assignments, yielding
\[\binom42\cdot4\cdot2^3=192\]
row-covering states. Interchanging row and column yields 192 further states. No five-set can cover all eight row/column vertices and be connected, since that would require at least seven bipartite edges.

A single exchange cannot take a row-covering state to a column-covering state. Otherwise the union of both five-edge sets would be a connected spanning graph on the eight bipartite vertices, with at most six edges, contradicting the spanning-tree bound of seven. Within one orientation, leaf rows can switch their assigned column while retaining the bridge row, the bridge can be transferred to a leaf row by one exchange, and the pair of column labels can be changed through a configuration containing a whole column plus one extra cell. Therefore every state in an orientation reaches every other state in that orientation. These are exactly the two components.

**4. Exact exchange and distance enumeration.** For each \(k\in\{4,5,6\}\), enumerate all eligible masks as in step 1. From each mask, replace every one of its \(k\) set bits in turn by each of its \(16-k\) unset bits, and retain the new mask exactly when it is in the computed eligible-set dictionary. This exhausts all and only the \(k(16-k)\) candidate one-replacement moves. Symmetric adjacency checks and division of summed degrees by two give the six edge counts in the theorem. Breadth-first searches over each complete exchange graph give the stated connected-component counts. For both five-set graphs, separate breadth-first searches from **every** state give the complete eccentricity distributions, hence the asserted diameters and radii. For \(k=6\), complete-component searches establish connectivity; the argument does not extrapolate from smaller graphs.

For additional geometric insight into the rook threshold, the six selected cells \(\{(0,0),(0,1),(0,2),(1,0),(2,0),(3,0)\}\) form a row-covering connected dominating six-set. Replacing \((1,0)\) by \((0,3)\) gives a column-covering connected dominating six-set. Thus the orientation barrier from step 3 is sharp. The complete enumeration proves that all 3,744 rook six-sets can participate in one connected exchange component. In \(S\), the exhaustive five-set exchange graph is already connected and has diameter five, so the analogous threshold is one lower.

## Verification
The package includes `verify.py` and an independently implemented C++ checker `verify_alt.cpp`. The first enumerates all 65,535 nonempty subsets of each 16-vertex graph, tests exact connected domination for sizes up to six, verifies all claimed four-set structural forms, builds each exchange graph, checks state/edge/degree/component counts and conducts all-source breadth-first searches at size five. The C++ implementation separately regenerates graph adjacency from coordinates, independently enumerates the same exhaustive universe, rebuilds exchanges and independently calculates components and eccentricities. Both accept with final lines `ALL_VERIFY_OK` and `INDEPENDENT_VERIFY_OK`, respectively. These are two computational implementations and **not** an external or independent research audit.

For reproducibility, run `python3 verify.py`; compile the second implementation using `c++ -std=c++17 -O2 -Wall -Wextra -o verify_alt verify_alt.cpp` and run `./verify_alt`. Python needs no non-standard libraries. The domain size, exact tests and termination conditions are fully specified by these source files; no downloaded data or precomputed table is required.

## Relationship to prior work
Connected domination is a standard invariant, equivalent to maximizing the number of leaves in a spanning tree, and is used as a routing backbone (Caro, West and Yuster; Chandran and collaborators). The reconfiguration literature studies transformations between feasible dominating sets, including token addition/removal and parameterized complexity (Suzuki, Mouawad and Nishimura; Lokshtanov and collaborators). These foundational theorems do not determine the present exact fixed-cardinality token-jump graphs.

The 2013 work of Shirkol, Shekharappa and Gudgeri studies **minimum total domination** and PBIB designs on the Shrikhande graph. Total domination is a different condition: it does not require the selected set to be connected, and its configuration space is not the present fixed-cardinality connected-domination exchange graph. Its abstract cannot establish the claimed five- and six-vertex exchange distances, even if some four-vertex configurations overlap. Related studies of the same two strongly regular graphs under independence complexes and other invariants also do not imply these exchange graphs. The novelty claim is restricted to the exact comparison of connected-dominating-set configuration spaces and the different sharp connectivity thresholds, not the known graph parameters or basic connected-domination definitions.

## Limitations
This is an exact finite result about two specified graphs, not an infinite-family theorem. The explicit exhaustive algorithms constitute the proof of the computational counts and graph distances; simple structural reasoning is also provided for the optimum sets and the rook five-set barrier. A separate peer or independent research audit has not been performed. Equivalence to an unindexed previous result remains possible. In particular, a full accessible copy of the most closely related 2013 article on the *different* total-domination invariant was not obtained; its available abstract was read, and no assertion about all its uninspected contents is made. No general relationship between common spectra and connected-domination reconfiguration is claimed beyond this distinguishing pair.
