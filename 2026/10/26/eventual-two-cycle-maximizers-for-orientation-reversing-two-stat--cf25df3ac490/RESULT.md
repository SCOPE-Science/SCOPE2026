# Eventual two-cycle maximizers for orientation-reversing two-state probabilistic automata

## Finding

Let \(\Sigma\) be a finite alphabet of size \(q\ge2\), and fix a complete two-state probabilistic finite automaton with a fixed initial probability distribution and a single accepting state. Assume that the transition matrix of **every** letter has strictly negative determinant. For each \(n\ge1\), call a word a *unique maximizer* if its acceptance probability is strictly greater than that of every other word of length \(n\). Let \(W\) be the set of unique maximizers over all lengths.

**Theorem.** The language \(W\) is regular. More precisely, it differs by only finitely many words from a union of at most two languages of the form \(\{uv^j:j\ge0\}\), where \(|v|=2\). In particular, the uniquely maximizing word (when it exists) is ultimately generated by appending one fixed two-letter block separately on each length parity.

Moreover, in every \(w\in W\), each of the two parity subsequences of positions (positions \(1,3,5,\ldots\), and \(2,4,6,\ldots\)) consists of contiguous runs of **pairwise distinct** letters. Consequently, writing \(B_q(0)=1\) and, for \(m\ge1\),
\[
B_q(m)=\sum_{r=1}^{\min(q,m)}\frac{q!}{(q-r)!}\binom{m-1}{r-1},
\]
the number of length-\(n\) words that can be unique maximizers for *some* such automaton is at most
\[
B_q(\lceil n/2\rceil)B_q(\lfloor n/2\rfloor)=O_q(n^{2q-2}).
\]
This bound is a necessary-condition count, not an exact enumeration or a sharpness assertion.

## Assumptions and scope

Probability distributions are row vectors. Each letter has a \(2\times2\) row-stochastic nonnegative transition matrix, and exactly one state accepts. An initial distribution may be arbitrary. If both states or neither state accepts, there is no unique maximizer at a positive length over an alphabet of size at least two, so the conclusions hold trivially; thus it suffices to consider one accepting state. Determinants may equal \(-1\), so no strict-contraction or mixing assumption is made. Negative determinants are required for **all** letters; this theorem does not address mixed signs or zero determinants. Ties are excluded by the phrase *unique maximizer*.

Represent a state distribution by its accepting-state probability \(x\in[0,1]\). Letter \(a\) induces \(f_a(x)=c_ax+d_a\), with \(c_a=\det(P_a)<0\); stochasticity ensures \(f_a([0,1])\subseteq[0,1]\), and every \(f_a\) is strictly decreasing and injective. Define \(f_w\) by composition in reading order. For \(n\ge0\), put
\[
M_n=\max_{|w|=n}f_w(x_0),\qquad m_n=\min_{|w|=n}f_w(x_0),
\]
with \(M_0=m_0=x_0\).

## Proof

**1. Two-step monotonicity.** Let \(F(x)=\max_{a\in\Sigma}f_a(x)\) and \(G(x)=\min_{a\in\Sigma}f_a(x)\). Both functions are strictly decreasing: every \(f_a\) decreases by at least \(\delta|x-y|\), where \(\delta=\min_a|c_a|>0\), and taking a finite maximum or minimum preserves that inequality. Therefore
\[
M_{n+1}=F(m_n),\qquad m_{n+1}=G(M_n),
\]
and
\[
M_{n+2}=(F\circ G)(M_n),\qquad m_{n+2}=(G\circ F)(m_n).
\]
Each two-step map is nondecreasing (indeed increasing). Iterating an increasing real function from any starting point gives a monotone orbit, since its first forward comparison is preserved at every iterate. Hence the even and odd subsequences of each of \((M_n)\) and \((m_n)\) are monotone, though their directions may depend on parity and on which extremum is considered.

**2. Eventual stabilization of optimal letter sets.** Define
\[
A_n=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{a\in\Sigma}f_a(m_{n-1}),\qquad
D_n=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{a\in\Sigma}f_a(M_{n-1}).
\]
Along either parity, the inputs \(m_{n-1}\) and \(M_{n-1}\) are monotone. For each pair \(a,b\), the comparison \(f_a(x)-f_b(x)\) is affine in \(x\); along a monotone sequence its sign can change at most once, except that the sequence may eventually stay on its unique zero (which also fixes the comparison), or that the affine difference is identically zero. There are finitely many pairs. Thus both \(A_n\) and \(D_n\) stabilize on each parity, including all ties.

**3. Unique extremizers eventually append a fixed pair.** Let \(U_n\) and \(V_n\) denote the *sets* of maximizing and minimizing length-\(n\) words. Strict decrease and injectivity give disjoint optimal-last-letter decompositions
\[
U_n=\{va:v\in V_{n-1},\ a\in A_n\},\qquad
V_n=\{ua:u\in U_{n-1},\ a\in D_n\}.
\]
Consequently \(|U_n|=|V_{n-1}|\,|A_n|\) and \(|V_n|=|U_{n-1}|\,|D_n|\), and neither cardinality can decrease after advancing by two positions. If maximizing uniqueness fails at some length, it cannot be recovered at later lengths of the same parity. Hence, on each parity, \(U_n\) is a singleton either for finitely many initial lengths or eventually for all lengths. In the latter case, every relevant optimal-last-letter set is a singleton, and Step 2 makes the letters \(a_n\in A_n\) and \(d_{n-1}\in D_{n-1}\) constant when \(n\) advances by two. Then
\[
U_n=V_{n-1}a_n=U_{n-2}d_{n-1}a_n,
\]
so for all sufficiently large successful \(n\) in this parity there are fixed letters \(d,a\) with \(U_n=U_{n-2}da\). This produces one length-two repeating suffix ray for that parity. Combining the two parities and the finitely many exceptional shorter words proves regularity and the asserted at-most-two-ray structure.

**4. Parity-subsequence block structure.** Fix any unique maximizer \(w=w_1\cdots w_n\). Every prefix \(w_1\cdots w_i\) must be a *unique* maximizing length-\(i\) word if \(n-i\) is even, and a unique minimizing length-\(i\) word if \(n-i\) is odd. Indeed, the already-fixed suffix defines an injective affine function of sign \((-1)^{n-i}\); substituting a strictly better extremal prefix improves the final score, while substituting a distinct tied extremal prefix contradicts final uniqueness. When the target prefix is maximal, its final letter uniquely maximizes \(f_a(m_{i-1})\); when minimal, it uniquely minimizes \(f_a(M_{i-1})\). Along positions \(i\) of fixed parity, one of these two extremal types is fixed, and the arguments \(m_{i-1}\) or \(M_{i-1}\) progress monotonically by Step 1.

For the upper envelope of finitely many affine functions, unique maximizing letters encountered along a monotone path cannot return after being replaced: if \(a\) beats \(b\) at earlier and later inputs, the affine difference \(f_a-f_b\) is positive throughout the intervening interval, so \(b\) cannot uniquely win in between. The identical assertion holds for unique minimizing letters on the lower envelope. Thus on each parity-subsequence of \(w\), a letter cannot reappear after another letter takes over; each letter occupies at most one contiguous run.

**5. Count.** A length-\(m\ge1\) word over \(q\) letters consisting of \(r\) positive-length runs of pairwise distinct letters is specified by \(q!/(q-r)!\) ordered distinct letters and \(\binom{m-1}{r-1}\) compositions of \(m\) into positive run lengths. Summing gives \(B_q(m)\). Any \(n\)-letter word is uniquely interleaved from its odd and even subsequences, hence the product bound. For fixed \(q\), \(B_q(m)=O_q(m^{q-1})\), completing the proof.

## Verification

The included standalone `check_negative.py` uses exact rational arithmetic. It generated 190 independent two-state ternary stochastic models with negative letter determinants, compared direct word enumeration against the two-step max/min recurrence and multiplicity identities (619,210 directly evaluated words), checked the parity-run necessity for each encountered unique winner, and checked the formula for \(B_q(m)\) by brute enumeration for \(q\in\{2,3,4\}\) and \(1\le m\le7\). It also verified the explicit ternary example below. `check_output.txt` contains the actual execution output. These finite tests corroborate, but do not establish, eventual regularity or the general theorem; the proof above is the general argument.

For a concrete example using three distinct symbols, put \(x_0=2/5\) and
\[
f_0(x)=(4-4x)/20,\qquad f_1(x)=(17-2x)/20,\qquad f_2(x)=(20-14x)/20.
\]
These correspond to valid negative-determinant stochastic matrices with accepting-state transition probabilities \(f_a(1)\) and \(f_a(0)\) in their first column. Among the \(27\) length-three words, the unique maximizer is `102`, with acceptance probability \(4867/5000\); its gap over the runner-up `202` is \(63/5000\). The example demonstrates that the statement genuinely involves a three-letter alphabet, not merely a two-letter renaming.

## Relationship to prior work

Gill's *Probabilistic Automatic Complexity of Finite Strings* (arXiv:2402.13376, Section 4.4, Corollary 4.16) proves regularity of unique maximizers for **arbitrary fixed binary-alphabet two-state automata** and remarks that its argument uses the binary alphabet. Kjos-Hanssen's 2026 *Every String Has Probabilistic Automatic Complexity at Most Three* (arXiv:2607.26275) establishes the universal three-state upper bound and discusses limitations of two-state constructions for larger alphabets. The present theorem obtains regularity for every finite alphabet in the natural, strictly orientation-reversing subclass, and additionally imposes the parity-run restriction and a uniform polynomial candidate count. The prior binary theorem does not imply the claim on alphabets of size at least three; the present theorem does not recover the mixed-sign generality of the binary theorem. A separate orientation-preserving (nonnegative-determinant) classification does not cover strictly negative-determinant letters.

## Limitations

The result is restricted to two-state PFAs in which **every letter's determinant is strictly negative**. Neither a mixed-sign nor a zero-determinant letter is covered. The polynomial upper bound counts a necessary structural class and is not asserted to be sharp. No statement is made about the minimum state complexity of particular words or three-state maximizers. A published or unindexed equivalent characterization could affect historical priority; the present inspection is not a proof of absolute novelty. All assertions are subject to independent mathematical review, which has not yet been performed.
