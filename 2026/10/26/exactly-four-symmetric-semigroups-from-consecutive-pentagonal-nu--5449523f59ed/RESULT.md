# Exactly four symmetric semigroups from consecutive pentagonal numbers

## Finding

Let \(P(n)=n(3n-1)/2\) be the ordinary pentagonal number and, for each integer \(n\ge2\), let
\[
S_n=\langle P(n),P(n+1),P(n+2)\rangle
\]
be the additive numerical semigroup of all nonnegative integer combinations of these numbers. Then
\[
\boxed{S_n\text{ is symmetric}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n\in\{2,3,5,40\}.}
\]
Equivalently, writing \(F(S)\) for the largest missing nonnegative integer and \(g(S)\) for the number of missing nonnegative integers,
\[
2g(S_n)=F(S_n)+1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n\in\{2,3,5,40\}.
\]
The four exceptional triples and their invariants are
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|r|r}
n&(P(n),P(n+1),P(n+2))&F(S_n)&g(S_n)\\\hline
2&(5,12,22)&43&22\\
3&(12,22,35)&133&67\\
5&(35,51,70)&1699&850\\
40&(2380,2501,2625)&258529&129265
\end{array}
\]
The cases \(n=2,5\) have two minimal generators; \(n=3,40\) have three. The exceptional Frobenius values follow from the standard two-generator formula and the three-generator gluing formula:
\[
F(5,12)=5\cdot12-5-12=43,\qquad F(35,51)=35\cdot51-35-51=1699,
\]
\[
F(12,22,35)=2(6\cdot11-6-11)+35=133,
\]
\[
F(2380,2501,2625)=35(68\cdot75-68-75)+34\cdot2501=258529.
\]
Symmetry then gives the displayed genera from \(2g=F+1\).

## Assumptions and scope

The integer \(n\) ranges over all integers at least two, with ordinary (not centered or generalized-sign) pentagonal numbers. The triple has gcd one: consecutive differences are \(3n+1\) and \(3n+4\), so any common divisor divides \(3\); however the adjacent pairwise gcd is at most \(2\), as shown below. The semigroup therefore has finite complement. Symmetric means that, for its Frobenius number \(F\), exactly one of \(z\) and \(F-z\) belongs to the semigroup for every integer \(z\). We use the standard equivalent characterization \(2g=F+1\). For a minimally three-generated numerical semigroup, we use the standard three-generator symmetry criterion: it is symmetric precisely when, after permuting the three generators, some pair has gcd \(d>1\) and the remaining generator lies in the semigroup generated by that pair divided by \(d\). This classical criterion is presented as Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 in Fel's paper [2].

## Proof

Write \(a=P(n)\), \(b=P(n+1)\), and \(c=P(n+2)\). The key identities are
\[
b-a=3n+1,\qquad c-b=3n+4,\qquad c-a=6n+5.
\]
First determine all possible pairwise gcds. Reduction modulo the first difference gives
\[
6a=3n(3n-1)\equiv2\pmod{3n+1},
\]
so \(\gcd(a,b)\mid2\). Likewise \(6c=(3n+6)(3n+5)\equiv2\pmod{3n+4}\), so \(\gcd(b,c)\mid2\). Parity gives
\[
\gcd(a,b)=2\iff n\equiv3\pmod4,\qquad
\gcd(b,c)=2\iff n\equiv2\pmod4.
\]
For the endpoints, \(24a=(6n)(6n-2)\equiv35\pmod{6n+5}\), so \(d=\gcd(a,c)\mid35\). Direct reduction of the factors of \(P(n)\) and \(P(n+2)\) modulo five and seven gives
\[
5\mid d\iff n\equiv0\pmod5,\qquad
7\mid d\iff n\equiv5\pmod7.
\]
Thus a candidate endpoint gcd greater than one is necessarily \(5\), \(7\), or \(35\), with the corresponding congruence restrictions.

For every \(n\ge6\), \(c<2a\): the difference is \((3n^2-13n-10)/2>0\). Hence all three generators are minimal. Apply the three-generator symmetry criterion and exclude its three possible pairs.

**Pair \((a,b)\).** The only possible nontrivial gcd is \(2\). If it were the gluing pair, we would require \(c=x(a/2)+y(b/2)\) for nonnegative integers \(x,y\). At \(n\ge10\) we have \(c>b\) and \(3a/2>c\), so sums of at most two normalized generators are below \(c\), and sums of at least three are above \(c\). The only remaining admissible \(n\ge6\) with \(n\equiv3\pmod4\) is \(n=7\). There the normalized pair is \((35,46)\) and \(c=117\); sums of three are \(105,116,127,138\), while at most two sum to at most \(92\) and at least four sum to at least \(140\). So this pair never works for \(n\ge6\).

**Pair \((b,c)\).** Its only possible nontrivial gcd is \(2\). A required representation \(a=x(b/2)+y(c/2)\) is impossible: if \(x+y\le1\), the sum is at most \(c/2<a\), whereas if \(x+y\ge2\), it is at least \(b>a\).

**Pair \((a,c)\).** Suppose \(d=\gcd(a,c)>1\) and \(b=x(a/d)+y(c/d)\). Then
\[
 db=xa+yc,\qquad x,y\in\mathbb N_0,\qquad d\in\{5,7,35\}.
\]
If \(a>d(3n+1)\) and \(c>d(3n+4)\), then
\[
(d-1)c<db<(d+1)a.
\]
Since \(a\le c\), these bounds force \(x+y=d\). Subtracting \(da\) yields
\[
y=\frac{d(b-a)}{c-a}=\frac{d(3n+1)}{6n+5}
=\frac d2-\frac{3d}{2(6n+5)}.
\]
When \(3d<6n+5\), this number lies strictly between \((d-1)/2\) and \(d/2\), which are consecutive half-integers with the first integral because \(d\) is odd. Consequently \(y\) cannot be integral. All three strict inequalities are valid throughout \(n\ge11\) for \(d=5\), throughout \(n\ge15\) for \(d=7\), and throughout \(n\ge71\) for \(d=35\), as seen by substituting the threshold into the quadratic inequalities and noting their positive increasing differences thereafter.

The congruence restrictions therefore leave only \(n=10\) with \(d=5\), \(n=12\) with \(d=7\), and \(n=40\) with \(d=35\), among \(n\ge6\). For \(n=10\), \((a/d,c/d,b)=(29,42,176)\); reducing \(29x+42y=176\) modulo \(29\) gives \(y\equiv18\pmod{29}\), while \(0\le y\le4\), impossible. For \(n=12\), \((a/d,c/d,b)=(30,41,247)\); modulo \(30\) gives \(y\equiv17\pmod{30}\), while \(0\le y\le6\), also impossible. For \(n=40\), however,
\[
(2380,2501,2625),\qquad \gcd(2380,2625)=35,\qquad
2501=7\cdot68+27\cdot75,
\]
so the criterion proves symmetry.

Finally, for \(n=2\), \(22=2\cdot5+12\), so the semigroup is \(\langle5,12\rangle\) and symmetric. For \(n=3\), \(\gcd(12,22)=2\) and \(35=4\cdot6+11\), proving symmetry. For \(n=4\), the generators are \((22,35,51)\), pairwise coprime and minimal, so the criterion excludes symmetry. For \(n=5\), \(70=2\cdot35\), reducing to \(\langle35,51\rangle\), which is symmetric. This exhausts every integer \(n\ge2\). \(\square\)

## Verification

The included standalone `verify_pentagonal.py` requires only Python's standard library. It computes the full Apéry set modulo \(a\) by exact shortest paths in the residue graph, then independently applies the gcd-and-membership criterion. Exact Apéry formulas give \(F=\max_r w_r-a\) and \(g=(\sum_r w_r)/a-(a-1)/2\), where \(w_r\) is the least represented number congruent to \(r\pmod a\). The checks covered every \(2\le n\le99\), recovering precisely the four exceptional triples and their displayed invariants. It also tested all \(100\le n\le3000\) against the gluing criterion and the strict inequalities. The script printed `VERIFY_OK`. These finite computations corroborate the proof, not replace it.

## Relationship to prior work

OEIS A069757 [1], publicly recorded in 2002, is specifically the Frobenius-number sequence for three consecutive pentagonal numbers; it supplies tabulated values but does not classify when the semigroups are symmetric. The three-generator symmetric-semigroup criterion is classical and reproduced in Fel [2], whose applications concern Fibonacci and Lucas triples, not pentagonal triples. Nari, Numata and Watanabe [3] analyze general three-generator genus and nonsymmetric structure, without identifying the present polygonal family. Robles-Pérez and Rosales [4] analyze consecutive triangular and tetrahedral numbers, not the pentagonal triples considered here. The theorem identifies exactly four symmetric indices, including the isolated three-generator case \(n=40\), by a complete pairwise-gcd argument. Related or equivalent unindexed literature cannot be ruled out.

## Limitations

This is a classification for ordinary pentagonal numbers at exactly three consecutive indices beginning with \(n\ge2\). It does not give closed formulas for all Frobenius numbers or genera, and it does not classify other polygonal-number types, different index gaps, or semigroups with additional generators. Numerical checks alone cannot establish uniqueness or novelty.
