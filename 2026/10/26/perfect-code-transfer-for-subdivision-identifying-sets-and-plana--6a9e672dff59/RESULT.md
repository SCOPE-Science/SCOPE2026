# Perfect-code transfer for subdivision identifying sets and planar subcubic hardness

## Finding
Let \(G=(V,E)\) be any finite simple graph with \(n=|V|\ge2\), and let \(S(G)\) replace each edge \(e=uv\) by the path \(u-w_e-v\). An **open-independent open-locating-dominating set** (abbreviated \(OLD_{oind}\)) is a vertex set \(C\) whose open-neighborhood intersections \(N(x)\cap C\), over *every* vertex \(x\), are nonempty and pairwise different, and for which each vertex of \(C\) has exactly one neighbor in \(C\).

A **perfect code** of \(G\) is a subset \(T\subseteq V\) satisfying \(|N_G[v]\cap T|=1\) for every \(v\in V\). Call it **nonpendant** when \(\deg_G(t)\ge2\) for every \(t\in T\). The entire collection of \(OLD_{oind}\) sets of \(S(G)\) is in bijection with the nonpendant perfect codes of \(G\). Explicitly, for each such \(T\), the corresponding set is
\[
C_T=(V\setminus T)\ \cup\ \{w_{uv}:uv\in E,\ \{u,v\}\cap T\ne\varnothing\}.
\]
The cardinality enumerator is therefore
\[
\mathcal L_{S(G)}(z)=\sum_{T\text{ nonpendant perfect code of }G}z^{2(n-|T|)}.
\]
This includes graphs with no qualifying set (the sum is then zero), and permits disconnected bases.

**Complexity consequence.** Deciding whether an \(OLD_{oind}\) set exists is NP-complete even on the restricted class of one-edge subdivisions of finite planar cubic simple graphs. Every graph in this class is planar, bipartite, of maximum degree three, and of girth at least six. On a cubic base of order \(n\), whenever such sets exist they all have size \(3n/2\), and their number equals the number of perfect codes of the cubic base. (In particular, \(4\) must divide \(n\).)

## Assumptions and scope
Graphs are finite, undirected, simple and unweighted. The vertex labels of \(G\), as well as subdivision vertices indexed by their original edges, are fixed; different vertex sets are counted separately. A perfect code means efficient *closed*-neighborhood domination, not merely a set with exactly one external neighbor. The degree restriction in the word *nonpendant* is essential: a perfect code containing a degree-zero or degree-one vertex never yields an \(OLD_{oind}\) set under the displayed construction. The complexity assertion uses the established NP-completeness of perfect-code existence for planar cubic graphs [3], not a new proof of that earlier hardness theorem.

## Proof
**Step 1 (necessity of a vertex cover).** Fix an \(OLD_{oind}\) set \(C\) in \(S(G)\). Put \(A=C\cap V\), \(T=V\setminus A\), and \(B=C\cap\{w_e:e\in E\}\). Every subdivision vertex \(w_{uv}\) has a nonempty signature contained in \(A\), so at least one endpoint of each edge lies in \(A\). Thus \(A\) is a vertex cover and \(T\) is independent. A selected subdivision vertex must have exactly one selected neighbor, hence every member of \(B\) subdivides an edge between \(A\) and \(T\).

**Step 2 (forced cut and nonpendant condition).** Each \(a\in A\) has exactly one selected subdivision neighbor, so \(a\) has a neighbor in \(T\). Were there two distinct edges \(at,at'\) with \(t,t'\in T\), their subdivision vertices would both have the same open signature \(\{a\}\), which is forbidden even if neither subdivision vertex were selected. Hence each \(a\in A\) has exactly one neighbor in \(T\), and the subdivision vertex of that edge belongs to \(B\). Consequently \(B\) consists *exactly* of all edges crossing from \(A\) to \(T\), represented by subdivision vertices. Since \(T\) is independent and every \(a\in A\) has exactly one neighbor in \(T\), each closed neighborhood of \(G\) contains exactly one vertex of \(T\): \(T\) is a perfect code. For \(t\in T\), all incident edges cross the cut. If \(\deg_G(t)=1\), with sole neighbor \(a\), then the original vertices \(a\) and \(t\) have the same nonempty signature \(\{w_{at}\}\) in \(S(G)\), contradicting location. If \(\deg_G(t)=0\), the signature of \(t\) is empty. Thus all members of \(T\) have degree at least two.

**Step 3 (sufficiency and exactness).** Conversely, let \(T\) be a nonpendant perfect code, put \(A=V\setminus T\), and choose \(C_T\) as displayed. Every \(a\in A\) has exactly one neighbor \(t\in T\), so there is exactly one selected subdivision vertex incident with \(a\), namely \(w_{at}\). Every selected subdivision vertex has its single selected original endpoint \(a\), proving open-independence. Signatures of *original* vertices are subsets of selected subdivision vertices: for \(a\in A\) they are distinct singletons \(\{w_{at}\}\); for \(t\in T\) they consist of all \(\deg_G(t)\ge2\) incident cut subdivision vertices. These sets are nonempty and mutually different because cut edges have distinct endpoints in \(T\). Signatures of *subdivision* vertices are subsets of \(A\): an edge within \(A\) gives a distinct two-element signature \(\{a,b\}\), while a cut edge gives a distinct singleton \(\{a\}\) because each \(a\in A\) has only one \(T\)-neighbor. These signatures are nonempty and pairwise different. No original-vertex signature can equal a subdivision-vertex signature, because the two use disjoint types of selected vertices. Thus \(C_T\) is an \(OLD_{oind}\) set. Its original-vertex intersection recovers \(A\), so the map is injective and, by Steps 1–2, surjective. Exactly one cut edge leaves each \(a\in A\), hence \(|C_T|=|A|+|A|=2(n-|T|)\), yielding the enumerator.

**Step 4 (sharp planar subcubic hardness).** Kratochvíl and Křivánek [3] established that deciding whether a planar cubic graph admits a perfect code is NP-complete. A cubic graph has minimum degree three, so *every* perfect code is nonpendant. Map an input planar cubic graph \(G\) to \(S(G)\), which is computable in polynomial time, planar, bipartite and subcubic. Every cycle in \(S(G)\) arises by doubling an original cycle, hence has length at least six. Steps 1–3 show that the original perfect-code instance is YES exactly when the resulting \(OLD_{oind}\) instance is YES. Direct verification of any proposed vertex set is polynomial, so the restricted problem is NP-complete. For cubic \(G\), the radius-one closed neighborhoods around a perfect code partition \(V\) into four-vertex pieces, giving \(|T|=n/4\) and \(|C_T|=3n/2\); the bijection also proves exact equality of the two code counts.

## Verification
The standalone program `verify_subdivision_old.py` independently enumerates vertex subsets of \(S(G)\) and tests the *original open-neighborhood definition*, then compares them with subsets obtained from perfect-code enumeration on \(G\). It checks **all** \(75\) simple labeled graphs of orders \(1\) through \(4\), another \(75\) distinct order-five graphs under a fixed seed, plus the cubic test graphs \(K_4\), \(K_{3,3}\), and the three-cube \(Q_3\). Every comparison passes; respectively, the three cubic cases have \(4\), \(0\), and \(4\) code sets. Run `python3 verify_subdivision_old.py` using Python 3, with no third-party dependencies. These are finite consistency tests only; Steps 1–4 prove the universal statements.

## Relationship to prior work
Seo and Slater [1], Theorem 3.3, characterized \(OLD_{oind}\) sets of all graphs of girth at least five by their selected/unselected neighborhood counts, and established general NP-completeness. Thus the underlying open-independence criterion and the high-girth characterization are **prior work**. Cappelle, Coelho, Foulds and Longo [2], Theorems 2.3–2.4, proved NP-completeness separately on planar bipartite graphs of maximum degree five and girth six, and on planar subcubic graphs of girth nine; they do not state the simultaneous planar-bipartite-subcubic subdivision restriction or the perfect-code correspondence. Kratochvíl and Křivánek [3] supplied the planar cubic perfect-code hardness premise. The contribution here is the exact parsimonious correspondence and its NP-completeness consequence on an explicitly more constrained intersection class. The general high-girth criterion could also be used as a starting point for deriving the correspondence, but does not itself give the transferred hardness result.

## Limitations
The NP-hardness step relies on a previously published theorem [3], whose abstract and bibliographic record were checked but whose full proof was not rederived here. No counting-complexity claim is made. The polynomial is a transfer identity rather than a closed numerical formula for every base graph. Potential overlap with differently indexed subdivision-code literature remains a priority risk. No independent audit or external formal certification has been performed.
