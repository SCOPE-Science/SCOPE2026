# Reduced neutral components force square-zero graded radicals and a graded Wedderburn complement

## Finding and scope

Let \(A=A_0\oplus A_1\) be a finite-dimensional associative \(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\)-graded algebra over the finite field \(F=\mathbb F_q\) of characteristic two. The algebra need not have a unit. Suppose \(A_0\) is reduced, meaning it has no nonzero nilpotent elements. Its graded Jacobson radical \(J=J_{\mathrm{gr}}(A)\) is supported in degree one and satisfies \(J^2=0\). There is a graded subalgebra \(B\) with \(A=B\oplus J\) as graded vector spaces, and the quotient map induces a graded isomorphism \(B\simeq A/J\).

The algebra \(B\) is a direct sum of graded-simple components of precisely the following possible types. In the list \(K/F\) is a finite field extension.

1. \(K\), in degree zero.
2. \(K[C_2]=K\oplus Ku\), where \(u\) is odd and \(u^2=1\).
3. \(M_2(K)\) with even diagonal and odd off-diagonal matrices.
4. \(\operatorname{End}_K(L)\cong M_2(K)\) for the quadratic field extension \(L/K\), with \(L\) acting by even multiplication operators and odd space \(L\sigma\), where \(\sigma\) is the nontrivial \(K\)-automorphism of \(L\).

## Proof

**Radical and complement in the unital case.** The unit of a group-graded unital algebra is in degree zero. In finite dimension the ordinary radical of \(A_0\) is nilpotent, so reducedness forces it to vanish. The resulting Artin–Wedderburn factors have size one, since larger matrix algebras contain nonzero nilpotents, and finite division rings are fields. Thus \(A_0\) is a product of finite separable fields. The finite-dimensional graded Jacobson radical is the largest nilpotent graded ideal. Its even part is zero by reducedness, and consequently \(J^2\subseteq J\cap A_0=0\).

The enveloping algebra \(A_0\otimes_F A_0^{\mathrm{op}}\) is semisimple: tensor products of finite separable fields over \(F\) are products of fields. The inclusion of \(A_0\)-bimodules \(J_1\subseteq A_1\) therefore splits; write \(A_1=C_1\oplus J_1\) for a bimodule complement \(C_1\). Then \(B=A_0\oplus C_1\) is closed under multiplication, because \(C_1C_1\subseteq A_0\) and the mixed products lie in \(C_1\). It maps isomorphically onto \(A/J\). There are no nonzero nilpotent graded ideals in \(B\): the inverse image in \(A\) of such an ideal of \(A/J\) would be nilpotent because \(J\) is, contradicting maximality of \(J\).

**Peirce decomposition and off-diagonal corners.** Write \(B_0=\prod_i K_i e_i\) using the even field idempotents, and set \(M_{ij}=e_iB_1e_j\). Since \(\sum_i e_i=1\), these corners span \(B_1\) in direct sum. For distinct \(i,j\), the vectors \(x\in M_{ij}\) such that \(xM_{ji}=M_{ji}x=0\) form an \(A_0\)-bimodule. It annihilates every odd corner on both sides: the only possible nonzero odd-odd products come from reverse corners, since off-diagonal even corners vanish. It is thus a square-zero graded ideal of \(B\) and is zero.

For any nonzero \(x\in M_{ij}\), there is accordingly some \(y\in M_{ji}\) for which at least one of \(xy,yx\) is nonzero. In fact both are, by \((xy)x=x(yx)\) and the fact that nonzero even corner scalars act invertibly. Rescale \(y\) on the right by the inverse of \(xy\) in \(K_i e_i\) to achieve \(xy=e_i\); then \(yx=e_j\) (multiply \(x(yx)=x\) by \(y\) on the left). Two distinct neighbors at one vertex are impossible: if \(z\in M_{ik}\setminus\{0\}\) for \(k\ne i,j\), then \((xy)z=z\) but \(yz\in e_j B_0 e_k=0\). If vertex \(i\) has a neighbor \(j\), then for any \(p,q\in M_{ii}\) and nonzero \(x\in M_{ij}\), \((pq)x=p(qx)=0\) since \(qx\in e_i B_0 e_j=0\). Thus \(M_{ii}^2=0\); it also annihilates every other odd corner on both sides. It is a square-zero graded ideal and must vanish.

There are now only isolated vertices and disjoint edges, and there are no products between the resulting summands. On an edge choose \(x,y\) with \(xy=e_i\), \(yx=e_j\). For any \(z\in M_{ij}\), \(z=(zy)x=x(yz)\), so \(M_{ij}=K_i x=xK_j\), with the analogous description for \(M_{ji}\). The map \(a\mapsto yax\) identifies \(K_i e_i\) with \(K_j e_j\). The four elements \(e_i,x,y,e_j\) are matrix units, yielding type 3.

**Isolated vertices.** For an isolated vertex let \(D\) be its summand, with even field \(L\). If \(D_1=0\) this is type 1. Otherwise the subspace of \(D_1\) annihilating \(D_1\) on both sides is a square-zero graded ideal of \(B\), hence zero. Each nonzero odd \(u\) therefore has a product in \(L^\times\) on at least one side with an odd element. This gives a one-sided inverse, hence an inverse by finite dimensionality. It follows that \(D_1=Lu\). Conjugating by \(u\) gives an \(F\)-automorphism \(\sigma\) of \(L\), with \(\sigma^2=1\) since \(u^2\in L^\times\), and \(u^2\) is fixed by \(\sigma\). If \(\sigma=1\), the square map on \(L^\times\) is bijective, so one can rescale \(u\) to square to 1; this is type 2. If \(\sigma\ne1\), put \(K=L^\sigma\). Then \(L/K\) is quadratic, and surjectivity of its norm lets one rescale \(u\) to square to 1. The map sending \(a+bu\) to the operator \(h\mapsto ah+b\sigma(h)\) respects multiplication. It is injective: evaluating a vanishing operator at 1 gives \(a+b=0\), and evaluating at an element not fixed by \(\sigma\) forces \(b=0\). Both spaces have dimension four over \(K\), so the map is a graded isomorphism with type 4. Each isolated-vertex summand is graded-simple because all its nonzero homogeneous elements are invertible; the matrix-algebra edge summands are simple even without grading.

**Nonunital case.** Adjoin an even external unit, writing \(U=F\oplus A\) for the unitization. A nilpotent element of \(U_0\) projects to zero in \(U/A=F\) and hence belongs to the reduced algebra \(A_0\); thus \(U_0\) is reduced. The nilpotent graded radical of \(U\) lies in \(A\) by projection to \(F\), and every nilpotent graded ideal of \(A\) is one of \(U\) since the extra action is scalar. Hence \(J_{\mathrm{gr}}(U)=J_{\mathrm{gr}}(A)\). Intersect a unital-case complement \(B_U\) with \(A\): each \(a\in A\) has a decomposition in \(U\) whose radical part already lies in \(A\), so \(B_U\cap A\) is the required complement. This intersection is a graded ideal of \(B_U\); the even identities of the direct summands split it into graded ideals of the graded-simple factors. It is therefore a direct sum of a subset of the listed factors.

## Verification and relation to prior work

The accompanying `verify.py` checks the explicit \(\mathbb F_4\)-neutral grading on \(M_2(\mathbb F_2)\), including the direct sum of the two homogeneous components and all products. It is an illustration, not a proof of classification for general \(A\).

Diniz, Pinto da Fonsêca, and Ramos, *Graded identities for matrix algebras of order two over a finite field*, [arXiv:2605.30494v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30494v1), Theorem 18 (Theorem 21 in [v5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30494v5)), give the graded decomposition under the stronger identity \(x^q=x\) on \(A_0\); its list is \(F\), \(F[C_2]\) and elementary \(M_2(F)\). Reducedness allows the additional extension-field factors in the present setting. The component structures themselves are not new: Theorem 13(II)(5) of v5 already includes the quadratic neutral field grading via an irreducible Artin–Schreier polynomial, and [Bahturin, Elduque and Kochetov, arXiv:1912.11911](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.11911) give the general graded-division crossed-product framework. The contribution is the reduced-neutral hypothesis for square-zero radical and a graded complement with this full list; the ordinary Wedderburn–Mal'tsev theorem alone does not guarantee a graded complement.

The scope is finite-dimensional associative algebras over finite fields of characteristic two with the two-component grading. The theorem does not classify all graded algebras over imperfect fields or for larger grading groups. The extent of earlier graded complement results under equivalent separability assumptions remains a best-knowledge limitation.
