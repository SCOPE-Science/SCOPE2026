# Scientific review

## Correctness — PASS

The theorem is reconstructed from the endomorphism-idempotent equivalence, identification of the projective support, convex maximization of complementary-subspace covers, exact count and injectivity of maximal line–hyperplane pairs, pairwise-intersection Bonferroni, and a coupling of the conditional line allocations to an exact binomial. For every fixed prime power and sink dimension at least three, the number of possible nonmaximal covers is finite independently of the number of source vertices. In the leading events, full occupancy gives uniquely identifiable complementary summands. The error \(O_{q,r}((H/D)^n)\) follows from intersections and lower supports; it is not inferred from finite trials. The independent computational paths verify rank-support decomposability and idempotent existence in the small cases reported, not the infinite limit.

## Originality — PASS within inspected literature

Kinser–Weist analyze indecomposable subspace-quiver normal forms, with their explicit many-source example centered on sink dimension two; they do not supply the fixed higher-rank labeled probability. Kelly–Oxley prove connectivity in a growing-rank random-matrix regime, including explicit but different union-of-hyperplanes bounds (Section 4); their theorem does not give the fixed-rank sharp constant or conditional splitting profile. Gao–Mausberg–Nelson likewise study growing-rank connectivity evolution; Schofield gives qualitative canonical-decomposition theory rather than the conditional distribution. The asserted novelty is the entire *sharp probability plus dominant splitting and conditional binomial law*, not the elementary connectivity criterion or union-bound idea. Earlier fixed-rank enumerations could indirectly imply part of the conclusion and remain a documented priority risk.

## Value — PASS

The result quantifies the rarity of decomposability in a natural representation-theoretic family, resolves the leading exceptional Krull–Schmidt type and gives the leaf-allocation fluctuations, with a uniform formula across arbitrary fixed finite fields and every sink rank at least three. The line–hyperplane dominance is a structural concentration phenomenon, not an arbitrary finite data slice. This is a complete asymptotic law for a canonical family of finite-field quiver representations, relevant to probabilistic matroid connectivity and representation decompositions.

The paper comparisons concern their actual hypotheses (fixed versus increasing center rank, zero versus nonzero arrows, labeled structures versus isomorphism classes) and resulting implications. Material read includes Kinser–Weist's subspace-quiver section, Kelly–Oxley's Sections 3–4 and connectivity proof, and Gao–Mausberg–Nelson's introductory and connectivity sections.
