# Six two-round theories of finite groups of prime exponent

## Finding

In the first-order language with equality, a named identity \(e\), and a ternary relation \(R(x,y,z)\) meaning \(xy=z\), consider **all finite nontrivial groups whose exponent is a prime** (not necessarily abelian). There are exactly **six** theories of quantifier rank at most two. A complete list of smallest representatives is
\[
C_2,\quad C_2^2,\quad C_3,\quad C_3^2,\quad C_5,\quad C_7.
\]
The classification of a group \(G\) of prime exponent \(p\) is:

| Exponent and size | Representative |
| --- | --- |
| \(p=2, |G|=2\) | \(C_2\) |
| \(p=2, |G|>2\) | \(C_2^2\) |
| \(p=3, |G|=3\) | \(C_3\) |
| \(p=3, |G|>3\) | \(C_3^2\) |
| \(p=5, |G|=5\) | \(C_5\) |
| \(p=5, |G|>5\), or \(p\ge7\) | \(C_7\) |

In particular \(C_5^2\equiv_2 C_7\). Every nonabelian finite group of exponent \(3\) has the rank-two theory of \(C_3^2\), and every nonabelian finite group of exponent at least \(5\) (prime) has the theory of \(C_7\). The sharp largest smallest representative has **nine elements**.

## Assumptions and scope

The group operation is a *relation*, not a function symbol; thus an expression involving iterated products is **not** an atomic formula. The exponent is **exactly a prime** \(p\), and groups are finite and nontrivial. A quantifier-rank-two theory means agreement on all sentences of nesting depth at most two, allowing Boolean combinations and arbitrary repeated occurrences of variables. The notation \(\equiv_2\) always refers to this signature.

## Proof

**Lemma 1 (complete two-element atomic data).** Given a group with identity \(e\), the atomic diagram of the labelled triple \((e,x,y)\) is determined by the truth of
\[
x=e,\ y=e,\ x=y,\ x^2=e,\ y^2=e,\ x^2=y,\ y^2=x,\ xy=e.
\]
Indeed, every equality is one of those listed or follows from them. Every product relation \(R(a,b,c)\) with \(a,b,c\in\{e,x,y\}\) is either an identity relation with \(e\), one of the displayed square equations, or a product equation whose value is \(e,x,y\). The group cancellation law makes \(xy=x\) equivalent to \(y=e\), and \(xy=y\) equivalent to \(x=e\), with the same facts for \(yx\). Also \(xy=e\) and \(yx=e\) are equivalent. This exhausts all 27 possible argument triples of \(R\) and all equality atoms.

**Lemma 2 (prime-exponent extension profiles).** Suppose \(x\ne e\) and \(G\) has exponent \(p\). In a group of exponent \(2\), the second element \(y\) can be \(e\), \(x\), or any other element. In a group of exponent \(3\), it can be \(e\), \(x\), \(x^{-1}=x^2\), or any other element. In a group of exponent \(p\ge5\), it can be
\[
e,\quad x,\quad x^2,\quad x^{-1},\quad x^{(p+1)/2},\quad\text{or any remaining element.}
\]
Within each case the displayed exceptional elements have pairwise distinct atomic diagrams, and every remaining element has one common diagram. For odd \(p\), the unique square root of \(x\) is \(x^{(p+1)/2}\): if \(y^2=x\), then \(y=(y^2)^{(p+1)/2}=x^{(p+1)/2}\). This proof uses only the power law within the cyclic group generated by \(y\), and does not assume commutativity of \(G\). For \(p=3\), the three expressions \(x^2,x^{-1},x^{(p+1)/2}\) coincide. For \(p\ge5\), they are all distinct and distinct from \(e,x\). Lemma 1 proves that no other atomic diagrams occur.

**Lemma 3 (when a generic response exists).** For \(x\ne e\), the generic diagram occurs precisely when \(|G|>2\) for \(p=2\); precisely when \(|G|>3\) for \(p=3\); precisely when \(|G|>5\) for \(p=5\); and always when \(p\ge7\). Indeed, the exceptional sets have respectively \(2,3,5\) elements, and \(|\langle x\rangle|=p\). In particular, for \(p\ge7\), at least two elements of \(\langle x\rangle\) are generic already. For \(x=e\), the second element is \(e\) or nonidentity; its atomic data depend only on whether \(p=2\) or \(p\) is odd.

**Classification.** An exact two-round Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé winning strategy is equivalent to equality of the sets of available *pointed one-step extension profiles*: after the first chosen element \(x\), the second player must match each possible second-element atomic diagram, in either structure. Lemmas 1–3 show these profiles depend only on the six cases in the table. Every row has identical rank-two theory to its representative, for all finite groups including nonabelian ones. A rank-two sentence can express any chosen pointed profile by an existential outer quantifier and Boolean combinations of existential or universal inner tests, so distinct profiles are logically distinguishable.

For explicit separation, the existence of a nonidentity element is common to all considered groups; the rank-one sentence \(\exists x\,\neg R(x,x,e)\) distinguishes exponent \(2\) from odd exponent. The rank-two sentence
\[
\exists x\,\exists y\,[x\ne e\land R(x,x,y)\land\neg R(x,y,e)]
\]
distinguishes exponent \(3\) from all exponents at least \(5\). Among groups of exponent \(2\), \(\exists x\,\exists y\,[x\ne e\land y\ne e\land x\ne y]\) distinguishes order \(2\) from larger size. For exponent \(3\), adding \(\neg R(x,y,e)\) to this last formula distinguishes order \(3\) from larger groups. Finally, in exponent \(5\), the sentence
\[
\exists x\,[x\ne e\land\exists y\,(y\ne e\land y\ne x\land\neg R(x,x,y)\land\neg R(x,y,e)\land\neg R(y,y,x))]
\]
detects a generic \(y\), and distinguishes \(C_5\) from the common sixth class. Hence all six theories are distinct.

The six representatives have orders \(2,4,3,9,5,7\). In the rank-two class of \(C_3^2\), any prime-exponent group has exponent \(3\) and order greater than three; its order is a power of three, hence at least nine. The other five classes have smaller representatives. Thus the sharp maximum minimal representative size is
\[\boxed{9}.\]

## Verification

The included standalone program `verify_prime_exponent.py` constructs the groups \(C_2,C_2^2,C_3,C_3^2,C_5,C_5^2,C_7,C_{11}\), and the nonabelian Heisenberg groups over \(\mathbf F_3\) and \(\mathbf F_5\). It enumerates every ordered pair of elements, checks all equality atoms and all 27 interpretations of \(R\) over \((e,x,y)\), computes the resulting two-round types, and confirms precisely six classes among these calibration groups. The output is reproduced in `verify_output.txt`. The calculations are not used as an infinite-group proof.

## Relationship to prior work

Gomaa's *Model Theory and Complexity Theory* (2007), Chapter 3, uses the **same ternary relational multiplication signature** and studies descriptive complexity of finite abelian groups with Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé games. Its results concern resource bounds and indistinguishability of cyclic and abelian groups; its examined Chapter 3 does not state this complete six-class result for finite prime-exponent groups, especially nonabelian groups. The later article *Descriptive Complexity of Finite Abelian Groups* (2010) likewise concerns finite abelian groups. The present result specializes to earlier familiar prime-cyclic indistinguishability, but the classification crossing primes and including all nonabelian exponent-prime groups is not implied by the stated cyclic/abelian bounds. Comparison with published research-index records and available earlier claims found no covering statement; this is not a proof of global novelty.

## Limitations

The language choice is essential: replacing the ternary relation by a binary multiplication **function** changes atomic expressibility, and adding named elements, counting quantifiers, or more quantifier rounds may distinguish these groups. The result concerns only groups with *prime exponent* and does not classify arbitrary finite \(p\)-groups or composite exponents. Historical literature may contain equivalent formulations not identified by the present searches. Independent validation has not been performed.
