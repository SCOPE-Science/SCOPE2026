# The eccentricity profile of Petersen four-colouring recolouring

## Finding
Let \(P\) be the Petersen graph, and let \(R_4(P)\) be its *single-vertex recolouring graph* with four named colours. Then \(R_4(P)\) is connected and has exactly \(12{,}960\) vertices and \(53{,}280\) edges. Its radius is \(13\), its diameter is \(14\), exactly \(3{,}360\) vertices have eccentricity \(13\), and exactly \(9{,}600\) have eccentricity \(14\). The numbers of degree-\(d\) vertices for \(d=6,7,8,9,10,11\) are, in order, \(720,2880,4560,2880,1440,480\). The colour/graph symmetry group gives eleven orbits of sizes and eccentricities
\[
(240,14),(480,13),(480,13),(720,14),(960,13),(1440,13),
(1440,14),(1440,14),(1440,14),(1440,14),(2880,14).
\]
This exact calculation describes a first extra-colour transition in local recolouring dynamics: the known three-colour graph consists of disconnected pairs, while the four-colour graph is connected with an explicit full distance-to-farthest-configuration profile.

## Assumptions and scope
The underlying Petersen graph is the simple undirected ten-vertex graph with vertices \(u_i,v_i\), where \(i\) ranges over integers modulo \(5\), and edges \(u_i u_{i+1}\), \(u_i v_i\), and \(v_i v_{i+2}\). It is isomorphic to the Kneser graph \(KG(5,2)\) on the ten two-element subsets of \(\{0,1,2,3,4\}\), with two subsets adjacent iff disjoint. A proper colouring maps all ten vertices to the fixed four-element palette \(\{0,1,2,3\}\) and gives different values to adjacent vertices; colours need not all be used. Two proper colourings are adjacent if exactly one coordinate differs, with no restriction on which replacement colour. Distance is shortest-path distance inside this graph, and eccentricity is the maximum distance from the specified colouring to any other colouring. No quotient of the colourings is taken in the claimed numbers.

## Proof
The theorem is an exact finite enumeration with a completeness-preserving symmetry reduction. The complete, inspectable ordinary Python certificates are `verify_kneser.py` and `verify_petersen.py`. The finite domain is small enough that the mathematical justification can be expressed as elementary loops with explicit invariants:

**(1) Exhaustive state universe.** `verify_kneser.py` generates every function from the ten Kneser vertices to the four-colour palette satisfying the fifteen disjointness inequalities, using recursive backtracking that assigns each vertex every colour not already assigned to a previously coloured neighbour. Its recursion covers all proper assignments because every forbidden choice violates one required edge inequality, while every proper assignment follows exactly one branch. Independently, `verify_petersen.py` tests every one of the \(4^{10}=1{,}048{,}576\) functions on the standard outer/inner Petersen graph and retains exactly those satisfying all fifteen edge constraints. These two complete methods each give \(12{,}960\) states.

**(2) Exact adjacency.** For every retained assignment \(c\), each of the ten vertices and each of the four replacement colours is tested. A neighbour is recorded exactly if it differs at that coordinate and conflicts with none of the three neighbours of that coordinate. This enumerates every one-coordinate proper recolouring once and excludes all non-moves. Symmetry of these arcs is explicitly asserted. Both implementations obtain \(53{,}280\) undirected edges and degree-count vector \((720,2880,4560,2880,1440,480)\) indexed by degrees \(6\) through \(11\).

**(3) Exhaustive orbit reduction.** Every proper colouring of the Petersen graph uses three or four colours, since the graph is not bipartite. Normalize each colouring by assigning colour name \(0\) to its first colour encountered, name \(1\) to the next, and so on. Every normalized three-colour partition has \(4\cdot3\cdot2=24\) distinct palette namings; every normalized four-colour partition has \(4!=24\). In its Kneser model, each of the \(5!=120\) permutations of the five underlying points maps disjointness edges to disjointness edges, so acts by a graph automorphism. The codes explicitly generate all 120 such maps, normalize all their images, and partition the full normalized-colouring set into exactly eleven disjoint orbits. Multiplying normalized orbit sizes by \(24\) gives all \(12{,}960\) labelled states. Recolouring adjacency is preserved under both types of permutation, so distance and eccentricity are constant within these orbits.

**(4) Exact distances.** For one representative of each of the eleven orbits, a breadth-first search over the *entire* \(12{,}960\)-state adjacency graph computes its distance to every state (every BFS is asserted to reach all states). For each representative, taking the largest assigned distance gives its true eccentricity, not merely a sampled lower bound. Orbit-weighted eccentricities give the displayed eleven orbit sizes, exactly \(3{,}360\) eccentricity-\(13\) vertices and \(9{,}600\) eccentricity-\(14\) vertices. This proves connectedness, radius \(13\), and diameter \(14\), and exhausts the stated graph-wide invariants. The second implementation uses an independent Petersen realization and direct enumeration, plus an explicit graph isomorphism for its orbit reduction. Both independently return the same invariant values.

All finite assertions are checked by runnable standalone standard-library Python programs; no statistical inference or external service is needed. This computational proof concerns exactly the single finite graph and four-colour palette defined above, not arbitrary graphs or any infinite family.

## Verification
Run `python3 verify_kneser.py` and `python3 verify_petersen.py` in the directory containing the included source files. Compare output with `verify_kneser.out` and `verify_petersen.out`. The first prints the eleven complete distance histograms for its symmetry-orbit representatives; the second independently confirms the asserted counts from the standard Petersen adjacency description. Both verify the stated exact state, edge, degree, orbit, eccentricity, connectivity, radius and diameter totals. These are *exhaustive* graph enumerations: the source makes the exhaustive loops and domain size explicit. Their previously stored outputs were reproduced from the exact packaged code.

## Relationship to prior work
The Petersen three-colouring count and its pairwise recolouring structure were already given by David Eppstein in *Chessboards and colorings* (2010); they motivate the first extra-colour question and are not new here. The count of \(12{,}960\) proper four-colourings is also recorded in OEIS A296913; enumeration alone is not claimed as novel. Work by Cereceda, van den Heuvel and Johnson addresses connectedness of graphs of vertex-colourings; Johnson, Kratsch, Kratsch, Patel and Paulusma (2014) examines shortest-path questions between graph colourings, but does not state the exact Petersen four-colour radius, diameter and complete eccentricity distribution in the material inspected. Daneshpajouh (2022) treats colourings of Kneser graphs using the optimal palette size, which here is three, not the four named colours used in this theorem. The specific eleven-orbit eccentricity certificate and exact \(13/14\) profile were not identified in searches of mathematical research records and accessible primary literature. This absence from searched sources cannot prove publication priority.

## Limitations
The theorem concerns a fixed finite graph, four named colours and the one-vertex change rule only. It does not establish analogous values for other graphs, larger palettes, mixing times, or Kempe-chain moves. The result has only been reviewed within the same mathematical analysis; independent scientific audit remains unperformed. Earlier equivalent results might exist outside the searched literature. The 2008 connectedness article's full author-copy PDF could not be accessed, so its scope was compared using accessible publication summaries. In the 2014 shortest-path article the relevant abstract, introduction and searchable text were checked, not every proof and appendix independently analysed. A finite exhaustive certificate is valid for these precise parameters but is not a theorem about an infinite family.
