# Scientific review

## Correctness — PASS
The proof identifies a necessary-and-sufficient signature criterion: at most one matched pair is unselected on both sides, together with exact singleton-side domination exceptions. The complete four-state enumeration and inclusion–exclusion, including the sixfold exceptional overlap for three matching pairs, yield the bivariate formula. The minimum classification and halving statement follow algebraically. Direct adjacency-based exhaustive checks with independent bivariate coefficient computations agree for every graph with three through eight matched pairs. These computations are independent finite spot-checks, not an infinite proof. Risk: implementation bugs cannot replace the proof, although the closed symbolic derivation does not rely on code.

## Originality — PASS with documented residual risk
The closest published polynomial treatment concerns complete multipartite graphs, rather than their matching-deleted bipartite counterparts (Fazil et al., 2022, DOI:10.48129/kjs.13175). Its relevant indexed theorem concerns twin classes; it does not logically imply the matching-deletion result. Bousquet et al. (arXiv:2112.01910) develop locating domination and optimal orientations, not this bivariate census. Indexed mathematical findings on crown graphs involve restrained domination or other identifying/coloring parameters; their claims neither contain nor dominate the present classical locating-domination enumerator. The full 2022 polynomial paper was not accessible; unindexed overlap remains a live scientific priority risk.

## Mathematical value — PASS
The theorem identifies all valid detector placements, not only the smallest one, in a natural regular bipartite family obtained by deleting a perfect matching. The exact factor-of-two improvement over the undeleted graph for every \(n\\ge5\), the small exceptional cases, and the nontrivial binomial distribution of optimal left/right placements provide a structural explanation of symmetry-breaking by deletion. The full bivariate count is useful for distinguishing load-balanced optimum solutions without additional enumeration.
