# A sharp three-element test for universal product–quotient balance in finite groups

## Finding

Let \(G\) be any finite group. For \(A\subseteq G\), set
\[
P(A)=\{xy:x,y\in A\},\qquad Q(A)=\{xy^{-1}:x,y\in A\}.
\]
Define \(w(G)\) to be the least cardinality of a subset \(A\) satisfying \(|P(A)|\ne |Q(A)|\), or \(\infty\) if no such subset exists. Then the following is a complete classification:

\[
 w(G)=\begin{cases}
 \infty,&G\cong C_2^r\ (r\ge0),\ \text{or }G\cong C_3,C_4,C_5,\\
 2,&\text{some noncommuting }a,b\in G\text{ satisfy }a^2\ne b^2,\\
 3,&\text{otherwise.}
 \end{cases}
\]

The three cases are mutually exclusive: the second clause applies only to groups outside the first, while the third applies to all remaining groups. Equivalently, a finite group has equal-size product and right-quotient sets for **every** subset precisely when it is an elementary abelian \(2\)-group or cyclic of order \(3\), \(4\), or \(5\). In every other finite group a witness of size at most three exists, and that cardinality bound is sharp (for example \(G=C_6\) and \(G=Q_8\) require three).

## Assumptions and scope

The group \(G\) is finite, its multiplication need not be commutative, and the empty subset is permitted. The right-quotient convention is \(AA^{-1}\), not \(A^{-1}A\). A singleton has product and right-quotient sets of size one; the empty subset has both sizes zero. Group isomorphism types include \(C_2^0\), the trivial group. The expression \(C_2^r\) denotes a direct product of \(r\) cyclic groups of order two.

## Proof

**1. Every two-point witness is detected by unequal squares.** For distinct commuting \(a,b\), put \(t=ab^{-1}\). The product set \(\{a^2,ab,b^2\}\) has two points exactly when \(t^2=1\) and three otherwise. The quotient set \(\{1,t,t^{-1}\}\) has the same cardinality. Now suppose \(ab\ne ba\). The four products \(a^2,ab,ba,b^2\) have four distinct values except that \(a^2\) may equal \(b^2\); neither mixed product can equal a square without implying \(a=b\), and \(ab\ne ba\). Thus the product cardinality is four if \(a^2\ne b^2\), and three if \(a^2=b^2\). The quotient set always contains at most \(\{1,t,t^{-1}\}\), hence at most three elements. When \(a^2=b^2\), it has exactly three: if \(t\) were an involution, writing \(a=tb\) and comparing \((tb)^2=b^2\) would give \(tbt=b\), hence \(tb=bt\), contrary to \(ab\ne ba\). Consequently a two-element subset is unbalanced if and only if its elements do not commute and have different squares.

**2. The equal-square nonabelian case contains a quaternion witness.** Suppose \(G\) is nonabelian but has no two-element witness. Choose noncommuting \(a,b\). By Step 1, \(a^2=b^2=c\). This element is central in \(H=\langle a,b\rangle\). The pairs \((a,ab)\) and \((b,ba)\) do not commute, because otherwise \(a,b\) would commute. The absence of two-element witnesses therefore gives \((ab)^2=(ba)^2=c\). Using \(a^2=b^2=c\) and \((ab)^2=c\), one gets
\[
 ba=a b^{-1}=c^{-1}ab.
\]
Since \(c\) is central in \(H\), this yields \((ba)^2=c^{-2}(ab)^2=c^{-1}\). Hence \(c=c^{-1}\), or \(c^2=1\). Further \(c\ne1\): otherwise \(a,b\) and \(ab\) would all be involutions, implying \(ab=ba\). Thus \(c\) is a central involution, \(a^2=b^2=c\), and \(ba=cab\). The eight elements
\[
 1,c,a,ca,b,cb,ab,cab
\]
are pairwise distinct: \(H/\langle c\rangle\) is generated by the two distinct nonidentity commuting involutions represented by \(a,b\), and dependence would force \(a,b\) to commute. These relations identify \(H\cong Q_8\). Taking \(A=\{1,a,b\}\), direct multiplication gives
\[
 P(A)=\{1,a,b,c,ab,cab\},\quad
 Q(A)=\{1,a,ca,b,cb,ab,cab\}.
\]
Therefore \(|P(A)|=6\) and \(|Q(A)|=7\). So every nonabelian group has an unbalanced subset of size two or three.

**3. Every nonexceptional abelian group has a three-point witness.** Write \(G\) additively. If it has an element \(g\) of order \(m\ge6\), set \(A=\{0,g,3g\}\). Its sumset has cardinality five when \(m=6\) and six when \(m\ge7\); its difference set has cardinality six when \(m=6\) and seven when \(m\ge7\). For \(m\ge7\), the residues \(0,g,2g,3g,4g,6g\) occurring as sums and \(0,\pm g,\pm2g,\pm3g\) occurring as differences are all distinct within their respective sets. When \(m=6\), precisely the displayed expected collisions occur.

If every element has order at most five, the fundamental decomposition of finite abelian groups says that either \(G\) is one of \(C_2^r,C_3,C_4,C_5\), or it contains a subgroup isomorphic to one of \(C_3\times C_3\), \(C_5\times C_5\), or \(C_4\times C_2\). Indeed, a mixed prime support would produce an element of order at least six; otherwise the exponent is \(2\), \(3\), \(4\), or \(5\). Rank at least two in the exponent-three or exponent-five cases produces the first two subgroups. An exponent-four group other than \(C_4\) contains \(C_4\times C_2\). For independent standard generators \(a,b\) of each displayed subgroup, let \(A=\{0,a,b\}\). In \(C_3^2\) and \(C_5^2\) it has six sums and seven differences. In \(C_4\times C_2\) it has five sums and six differences. The same counts hold inside the ambient group. No abelian group has a two-point witness by Step 1, so its least witness size is three outside the stated exceptions.

**4. The listed exceptions really balance every subset.** In an elementary abelian \(2\)-group every element is its own inverse, so \(P(A)=Q(A)\) for all \(A\). For a cyclic group of order \(n\in\{3,4,5\}\), every subset of size at most two has equal sumset and difference-set cardinalities by Step 1. If \(|A|\ge3\), then \(2|A|>n\); for every \(g\in G\), the sets \(A\) and \(g-A\) intersect, proving \(g\in A+A\). Similarly \(A\cap(g+A)\ne\varnothing\), proving \(g\in A-A\). Both sets therefore equal \(G\). The empty set and singleton case were handled above. This establishes the classification and the sharp witness-size formula.

## Verification

The accompanying `verify.py` uses only the Python 3 standard library. Run `python3 verify.py` from the extracted package directory. It exhaustively tests every subset in the cyclic groups through order nine, the elementary abelian \(2\)-groups through rank three, small dihedral groups, and \(Q_8\). It also searches all one-, two-, and three-point subsets in cyclic groups through order sixteen, elementary abelian \(2\)-groups through rank six, selected rank-two and rank-three products, and dihedral groups through order twenty-four. A separate check verifies the explicitly claimed three-point cardinalities for cyclic orders from six through one hundred and for each exceptional subgroup type. Successful execution prints `VERIFY_OK`. Exhaustive small cases do not replace the all-group proof above.

## Relationship to prior work

Amit et al. (2026, arXiv:2610.12912, first public October 9, 2026) study distributional product-versus-quotient dominance and moment bounds across families of finite groups. Their results concern the number or proportion of unbalanced subsets, not the universal equal-cardinality property or a sharp three-point detection criterion. Miller and Vissuet (arXiv:1308.2344) show that almost all subsets of large finite groups are balanced; an asymptotic density-one statement does not classify which finite groups have **every** subset balanced. Mandal and Neetu (arXiv:2602.09073) obtain small-subset counts for dicyclic groups, including the known quaternion example, but do not give a classification over all finite groups. Mouli and Saldin (arXiv:2509.04717) classify groups with \(|AA^{-1}|=|A^{-1}A|\) for every \(A\), an inequivalent left/right-quotient property that holds for every abelian group, including \(C_6\), and hence cannot imply the present classification. Duvivier et al. (arXiv:2509.00611) investigate the same distinct left/right-quotient comparison. The cited finite-group product/quotient literature supports the motivation but does not supply the universal sharp witness theorem under the inspections described in the accompanying review. Independent priority evaluation remains outstanding.

## Limitations

This theorem classifies whether a group admits any product/quotient size imbalance and the least possible witness cardinality; it does not count witnesses in each group, determine maximal imbalance, or distinguish statistical product-dominance from quotient-dominance. The result is for finite groups; it can be extended to certain infinite groups by separate local arguments, but no infinite-group classification is claimed. Prior-literature coverage can be incomplete despite searches, and independent scientific assessment has not been performed.
