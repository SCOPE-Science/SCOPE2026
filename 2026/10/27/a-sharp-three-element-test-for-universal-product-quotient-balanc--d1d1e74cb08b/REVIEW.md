# Assessment of the universal-balance classification

## Correctness — PASS
The cardinality-one and cardinality-two cases are checked exactly. The structural nonabelian proof identifies an eight-element quaternion subgroup whenever no two-point imbalance exists, and the explicit three-element subset has six products and seven quotients. The finite abelian case uses the standard primary-decomposition theorem and exhibits concrete three-point witnesses in every nonexception. All listed exceptions have all subsets balanced. The standalone deterministic verifier returned `VERIFY_OK`; its finite checks are corroboration, not a replacement for the universal proof.

## Originality — PASS, with residual risk
The 2026 source studies moments, proportions, and selected families, rather than the universal all-subsets equality boundary. The 2013 paper proves asymptotically almost all subsets balanced, which is compatible with rare three-point counterexamples. Existing dicyclic tables include quaternion small witnesses but do not cover arbitrary finite groups. The superficially close Mouli–Saldin classification compares left and right quotient cardinalities; it is a different condition satisfied by every finite abelian group, including examples excluded here. Searches of related published research records identified no equivalent all-group result. This is a self-review originality judgment, not proof of priority: implicit or unindexed earlier coverage remains possible; the Ascoli et al. generalized-dihedral article was available only at abstract level for this comparison.

## Mathematical value — PASS
The classification resolves the natural exact boundary of the balanced-subset statistic and proves a sharp universal three-point detection principle. The proof exposes two mathematically different obstructions: unequal squares of noncommuting pairs and quaternion or abelian three-point configurations. Exact global classification and optimal witness cardinality are not entailed by near-total balance asymptotics. The result makes no claim about group-wise frequencies or largest discrepancy.
