# Binomial syntactic monoids of two-run subsequence ideals

## Finding
For every pair of integers \(r,s\geq 1\), let \(L_{r,s}\subseteq\{0,1\}^*\) consist of the words containing \(0^r1^s\) as a **scattered subsequence** (the selected positions need not be adjacent). Its minimal complete deterministic finite automaton has \(r+s+1\) states. The **syntactic monoid, including the empty-word identity**, has exactly
\[
 |M(L_{r,s})|=\binom{r+s+2}{r+1}-1
\]
elements. The corresponding **syntactic semigroup generated by nonempty words** has exactly \(\binom{r+s+2}{r+1}-2\) elements. In particular, for balanced two-run patterns the monoid size is exponential, of order \(2^n/\sqrt n\), in the \(n=r+s+1\) DFA states.

More precisely, each transformation admits a unique **signature** consisting of an integer \(c\in\{0,\ldots,r\}\) and a weakly increasing tuple \(0\leq t_c\leq t_{c+1}\leq\cdots\leq t_r\leq s\). Conversely every such signature occurs. This bijection is the source of the exact count.

## Assumptions and scope
The alphabet is exactly \(\{0,1\}\). A word contains \(0^r1^s\) as a subsequence if there are \(r+s\) strictly increasing positions spelling that pattern. All parameters are positive integers. The syntactic monoid includes the identity induced by the empty word; the syntactic semigroup here excludes that identity when it is not induced by a nonempty word. No assertion is made for patterns with more than two constant runs or for the contiguous-factor language.

The canonical automaton has state set \(Q=\{0,1,\ldots,r+s\}\), start state \(0\), final state \(r+s\), and transition rules:
\[
 \delta(i,0)=\begin{cases}i+1,&i<r,\\i,&i\geq r,\end{cases}
 \qquad
 \delta(i,1)=\begin{cases}i+1,&r\leq i<r+s,\\i,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
\]
Thus state \(i\) represents the length of the greedily matched prefix of the target. The final state is absorbing.

## Proof
**1. Minimality of the prefix automaton.** Reading a word from state \(0\) greedily advances the longest matched prefix of \(0^r1^s\); hence the automaton recognizes \(L_{r,s}\). Every state \(i\) is reachable by reading the first \(i\) symbols of the target pattern. If \(i<j\), append the suffix of the target after its first \(j\) symbols: from state \(j\) this reaches \(r+s\), while from \(i\) it cannot, since its length is \(r+s-j<r+s-i\) and one symbol can advance at most one state. All \(r+s+1\) states are pairwise distinguishable. Therefore the transition monoid of this automaton is the syntactic monoid.

**2. Every word determines an admissible signature.** Let \(w\in\{0,1\}^*\), let \(z\) be the number of zeros in \(w\), and set \(c=\max\{r-z,0\}\). Starting at any state \(i<c\) leaves too few zeros in \(w\) to reach the second phase, so
\[
 \delta(i,w)=i+(r-c)\quad(0\leq i<c).
\]
If \(i\in\{c,\ldots,r-1\}\), starting at \(i\) reaches state \(r\) at the \((r-i)\)-th zero of \(w\). Let \(t_i\) be the number of ones **strictly after that zero**, capped at \(s\). Define \(t_r\) to be the total number of ones, capped at \(s\). For all \(c\leq i\leq r\),
\[
 \delta(i,w)=r+t_i.
\]
Higher initial states reach phase two no later than lower ones, so \(0\leq t_c\leq\cdots\leq t_r\leq s\). For \(r<i\leq r+s\),
\[
 \delta(i,w)=\min\{i+t_r,r+s\}.
\]
These three formulas show that \((c;t_c,\ldots,t_r)\) determines the transformation on **every** state. They remain valid when \(z>r\) and when the actual number of ones exceeds \(s\), because all relevant counts are capped.

**3. Different signatures are distinct transformations.** For \(c>0\), \(\delta(0,w)=r-c<r\); for \(c=0\), \(\delta(0,w)\geq r\). In the former case the image of state \(0\) recovers \(c\); in the latter case \(c\) is recognized by the image being at least \(r\). For fixed \(c\), each \(t_i\) is recovered from the image of state \(i\), namely \(\delta(i,w)-r\). Thus signatures inject into the set of transformations.

**4. Every admissible signature occurs.** Fix \(c\) and \(0\leq t_c\leq\cdots\leq t_r\leq s\). For \(c=r\), take the word \(1^{t_r}\). Otherwise take the word containing exactly \(r-c\) zeros whose numbers of ones (from left to right) are \(t_r-t_{r-1}\) before the first zero, \(t_{r-1}-t_{r-2}\) between the first two zeros, and so on, ending with \(t_{c+1}-t_c\) immediately before the last zero and \(t_c\) after the last zero. Equivalently, its alternating block expression is
\[
 1^{t_r-t_{r-1}}0\,1^{t_{r-1}-t_{r-2}}0\,\cdots\,0\,1^{t_{c+1}-t_c}0\,1^{t_c},
\]
where the middle range is empty when there is only one zero. Exactly \(t_i\) ones follow the \((r-i)\)-th zero, so this word has the prescribed signature. This proves surjectivity as well as a representative-length bound \(r+s\).

**5. Enumeration.** For a given \(c\), the tuple has \(r-c+1\) entries, weakly increasing in \(\{0,\ldots,s\}\). Stars and bars gives \(\binom{s+r-c+1}{r-c+1}\) possibilities. Summing over \(c\) and using the hockey-stick identity yields
\[
 \sum_{c=0}^{r}\binom{s+r-c+1}{r-c+1}
 =\sum_{j=1}^{r+1}\binom{s+j}{j}
 =\binom{s+r+2}{r+1}-1.
\]
Finally, no nonempty input induces the identity: any input containing a zero moves state \(0\), while an input with no zeros but at least one one moves state \(r\). Hence deleting the empty-word identity gives the asserted nonempty-word semigroup count.

## Verification
The standalone program `verify.py` constructs the transformation monoid by breadth-first closure under the two letter transformations and independently constructs the whole set from monotone signatures. It confirms set equality and the binomial count for every \(1\leq r,s\leq6\) (36 parameter pairs). For \(1\leq r,s\leq4\) it additionally enumerates all words through length \(r+s\), obtaining the same transformations. Direct subsequence membership testing agrees with greedy recognition for 4,599 binary instances. Sample monoid sizes are \(5,19,69,251,3431\) for \((r,s)=(1,1),(2,2),(3,3),(4,4),(6,6)\). These finite checks are supporting evidence; the five proof steps establish the theorem for all positive \(r,s\).

## Relationship to prior work
Brzozowski, Davies and Madan (arXiv:1806.04645, first public 2018-06-12) study state complexity of matching patterns as subsequences of regular texts, including single-word matching (their Section 7.4). The present exact invariant is different: the **number of all distinct transformations** of the minimal automaton of a specific two-run principal shuffle ideal. Angluin, Aspnes, Eisenstat and Kontorovich (JMLR 14, 2013) exhibit the canonical DFA of a principal shuffle ideal and investigate learnability, without the two-run transition-monoid census. Brzozowski and Ye (arXiv:1010.3263, 2010) study broad syntactic-semigroup extremal bounds for ideal and closed languages (not this special all-sided ideal family); these bounds do not imply the displayed binomial equality. Halfon and Schnoebelen (arXiv:1810.02953, first public 2018-10-06) study shuffle closure of piecewise-testable languages, not the cardinality of this syntactic monoid. The monoid-versus-semigroup identity distinction is essential.

## Limitations
The result is confined to a single **two-run** binary subsequence pattern; extensions to three or more runs are not established. The search of relevant indexed literature and the published research database did not identify this exact equality, but cannot rule out an unindexed or differently described derivation, especially in algebraic automata or finite semigroup literature. The review is not an independent audit. The asymptotic statement concerns the balanced parameters only, not a universal maximum among regular languages of equal DFA size.
