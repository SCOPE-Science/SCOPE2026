# Scientific review

## Correctness — PASS
The formula for the Zak transform is derived by absolutely convergent geometric series. At nonreal phase the real and imaginary parts force either the spatial seam or the half-cell, and the remaining phase-only zeros are solved by a factored hyperbolic-cosine identity. Three exact thresholds and endpoint inclusions follow by monotonicity. The accompanying script checks 420 direct series/formula comparisons with maximum error below \(2\times10^{-15}\) and ten zero configurations. The analytic argument establishes exhaustiveness for all real parameters.

## Originality — PASS (bounded comparison)
The closest primary theorem, Kloos (2014, arXiv:1411.1539v1, Theorems 3.6, 3.7 and 3.12), concerns totally positive functions and a unique Zak zero. The present windows at the nontrivial thresholds have a Fourier transform changing sign, hence fall outside that class. Janssen (2003) reports a unique zero for even unimodal positive windows; all phase transitions here occur for non-unimodal windows. Kloos–Stöckler (2013) establishes the former total-positive setting. Direct topical database searches and comparisons with relevant existing claims found no mathematical implication of the five-zero classification.

The Janssen full PDF was inaccessible through the institutional link, and the 2013 predecessor was not read in complete form. These are originality access limitations. Absence from searched records is not a proof of global uniqueness.

## Value — PASS
A positive two-exponential window can undergo two distinct five-zero regimes, separated by a three-zero regime, with a sharp two-zero onset. The exact locations distinguish seam zeros, fixed-phase spatial zeros, and fixed-position frequency zeros. This determines a natural and previously non-obvious boundary for the one-zero phenomenon important in critical Gabor analysis.

## Limitations
The theorem does not assert full Gabor frame-set boundaries, multiplicities of coalescing zeros, or properties for arbitrary exponential mixtures. Numerical tests do not prove the infinite-domain claim; the explicit case exhaustion does. Independent verification is outstanding.
