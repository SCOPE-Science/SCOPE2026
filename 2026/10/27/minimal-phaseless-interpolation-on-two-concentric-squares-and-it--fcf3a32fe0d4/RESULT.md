# Minimal phaseless interpolation on two concentric squares and its exact lifted spectrum

## Finding

Let \(p(z)=a_0+a_1z+a_2z^2\) be any complex polynomial of degree at most two. Fix radii \(r_1,r_2>0\), angular offsets \(\theta_1,\theta_2\in\mathbb R\), and the eight sampling positions
\[
\Omega=\{r_j e^{i(\theta_j+k\pi/2)}:j=1,2,\ k=0,1,2,3\}.
\]
The eight values \(\{|p(z)|^2:z\in\Omega\}\), with their sampling positions retained, determine **every** such polynomial uniquely up to a constant unimodular multiplier if and only if
\[
\boxed{r_1\ne r_2\quad\text{and}\quad \sin(2(\theta_2-\theta_1))\ne0.}
\]
This number of measurements is minimal among **all** possible complex linear-intensity measurement systems for the three-dimensional coefficient space.

In particular, the eight nearest nonzero Gaussian-integer sampling positions
\[
\Omega_*=\{1,-1,i,-i,1+i,1-i,-1+i,-1-i\}
\]
are phase retrieving for every complex quadratic polynomial. Let \(\operatorname{Herm}_3\) be the real Hilbert space of Hermitian \(3\times3\) matrices with Frobenius norm, and define
\[
Q_H(z)=\sum_{j,k=0}^{2}H_{jk}z^j\overline z^{\,k},\qquad
\mathcal L(H)=\bigl(Q_H(z)/2\bigr)_{z\in\Omega_*}.
\]
The kernel of this real-linear map is exactly the one-dimensional space generated by \(\operatorname{diag}(2,-3,1)\). Its eight nonzero squared singular values, with multiplicities, are
\[
\boxed{12\pm\sqrt{130}\ (\text{once each}),\quad
6\pm\sqrt{34}\ (\text{twice each}),\quad 2,\ 8\ (\text{once each}).}
\]
As an explicit global intensity stability consequence, for arbitrary coefficient vectors \(a,b\in\mathbb C^3\), with \(p_a(z)=a_0+a_1z+a_2z^2\), one has
\[
\boxed{
\left(\frac14\sum_{z\in\Omega_*}
\bigl(|p_a(z)|^2-|p_b(z)|^2\bigr)^2\right)^{1/2}
\ge \sqrt{\frac{6-\sqrt{34}}{14}}\,
\|aa^*-bb^*\|_F.}
\]
The displayed stability factor is rigorous but is **not asserted optimal** for differences of rank-one positive matrices. The eight-point singular spectrum itself is exact.

## Assumptions and scope

All polynomials have arbitrary complex coefficients, including vanishing coefficients and lower degree. Measurements are labeled squared moduli of point evaluations, not derivatives and not Fourier-transform magnitudes of the polynomial. Both radii must be strictly positive. The family theorem concerns the union of two regular four-point angular orbits with the same center and arbitrary relative rotation. If their radii agree, coincident or noncoincident sample points on the common circle are allowed as an unsuccessful measurement configuration. There is no generic-signal restriction.

## Proof

**1. The complete real-linear measurement kernel.** For any Hermitian matrix \(H=(H_{jk})_{0\le j,k\le2}\), put \(u_j=r_j^2\). For \(z=r e^{i\phi}\), direct expansion gives
\[
Q_H(re^{i\phi})=A(u)+2\operatorname{Re}(r B(u)e^{i\phi})
+2\operatorname{Re}(u H_{20}e^{2i\phi}),
\]
where
\[
A(u)=H_{00}+uH_{11}+u^2H_{22},\qquad B(u)=H_{10}+uH_{21}.
\]
Sampling at four consecutive quarter-turns separates exactly three Fourier components: the constant \(A(u_j)\), the complex first-harmonic coefficient \(r_jB(u_j)\), and the real alternating coefficient \(2u_j\operatorname{Re}(H_{20}e^{2i\theta_j})\). Therefore \(Q_H\) vanishes at all eight samples if and only if all these components vanish for \(j=1,2\).

If \(u_1\ne u_2\), the equations \(B(u_1)=B(u_2)=0\) give \(H_{10}=H_{21}=0\). If in addition \(\sin(2(\theta_2-\theta_1))\ne0\), the two real equations for \(H_{20}\) are independent and give \(H_{20}=0\). The two independent equations \(A(u_1)=A(u_2)=0\) leave precisely
\[
(H_{00},H_{11},H_{22})
=t\bigl(u_1u_2,-(u_1+u_2),1\bigr),\qquad t\in\mathbb R.
\]
Thus the entire kernel is \(\mathbb R\operatorname{diag}(u_1u_2,-u_1-u_2,1)\); all three diagonal entries are nonzero for positive radii.

**2. Phase retrieval and necessity.** For a coefficient vector \(a\), write \(H_a=aa^*\); then \(Q_{H_a}(z)=|p_a(z)|^2\). If \(a,b\) yield identical measurements, the Hermitian difference \(H_a-H_b\) belongs to the above one-dimensional kernel, but has rank at most two. Every nonzero member of the computed kernel has rank three. Hence \(aa^*=bb^*\), which is equivalent to equality of the coefficient vectors up to a unimodular constant (also treating the zero vector).

Conversely, if \(r_1=r_2=r\), the nonequivalent polynomials \(p(z)=z^2\) and \(q(z)=r^2\) have the same modulus at every point on that circle. If \(\sin(2(\theta_2-\theta_1))=0\), take
\[
p(z)=1+i e^{-2i\theta_1}z^2,\qquad
q(z)=1-i e^{-2i\theta_1}z^2.
\]
On both sampled square orbits, \(e^{-2i\theta_1}z^2\) is real, so the two polynomials have identical squared moduli, yet are not constant unimodular multiples. This proves the stated if-and-only-if criterion.

**3. Eight is the absolute minimum in coefficient dimension three.** Take any at most seven complex linear measurement vectors in \(\mathbb C^3\). If they fail to span \(\mathbb C^3\), zero and a nonzero common orthogonal vector already produce identical zero intensities. Otherwise their real Hermitian lifting map has a real kernel of dimension at least two because \(\dim_{\mathbb R}\operatorname{Herm}_3=9\). Inside the real plane generated by independent kernel matrices, the determinant is a continuous odd homogeneous cubic. Restrict to the unit circle in that plane: antipodal determinants have opposite signs, so some nonzero kernel matrix is singular. A nonzero positive- or negative-semidefinite matrix cannot lie in the kernel of a spanning rank-one intensity system. Consequently the singular matrix is rank-two indefinite; it is a difference of two rank-one positive semidefinite matrices. Those matrices exhibit inequivalent vectors with identical intensities. No system of seven or fewer measurement vectors works universally.

**4. Exact spectrum at the nearest Gaussian-integer samples.** Set \((r_1,r_2)=(1,\sqrt2)\) and \((\theta_1,\theta_2)=(0,\pi/4)\). The generic kernel formula becomes \(\mathbb R\operatorname{diag}(2,-3,1)\). With the normalization of \(\mathcal L\), the four-point discrete Fourier transform of each real sample orbit is an orthogonal change of output coordinates, and \(\operatorname{Herm}_3\) decomposes orthogonally into diagonal (dimension three), nearest-off-diagonal (dimension four), and far-off-diagonal (dimension two) subspaces.

On the diagonal block the two nonzero squared singular values are the eigenvalues of
\[
D D^T=\begin{pmatrix}3&7\\7&21\end{pmatrix},
\]
namely \(12\pm\sqrt{130}\). On the nearest-off-diagonal block, a complex \(2\times2\) radial matrix
\[
N=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\\sqrt2&2\sqrt2\end{pmatrix}
\]
acts on each of the two real components, so its squared singular values are those of
\[
N N^T=\begin{pmatrix}2&3\sqrt2\\3\sqrt2&10\end{pmatrix},
\]
namely \(6\pm\sqrt{34}\), each twice. The final block acts on the real and imaginary components of \(H_{20}\); the two concentric squares give mutually orthogonal coefficient-phase directions. Its squared singular values are \(2\) and \(8\). Together these are all eight positive squared singular values, with a one-dimensional kernel as claimed.

**5. Explicit global stability.** For the canonical sample set, the smallest positive singular value of \(\mathcal L\) is \(\sqrt{6-\sqrt{34}}\). Put \(K=\operatorname{diag}(2,-3,1)\). Its Frobenius norm is \(\sqrt{14}\), and its singular values are \(3,2,1\). By the rank-two matrix approximation inequality (or von Neumann's trace inequality), for every Hermitian matrix \(H\) of rank at most two,
\[
\left|\left\langle H,\frac K{\sqrt{14}}\right\rangle_F\right|
\le \sqrt{\frac{13}{14}}\,\|H\|_F.
\]
The orthogonal projection \(H_\perp\) onto \(K^\perp\) therefore has \(\|H_\perp\|_F\ge \|H\|_F/\sqrt{14}\). Since \(\mathcal L(K)=0\),
\[
\|\mathcal L(H)\|_2
=\|\mathcal L(H_\perp)\|_2
\ge\sqrt{6-\sqrt{34}}\,\|H_\perp\|_F.
\]
Taking \(H=aa^*-bb^*\) proves the displayed stability inequality.

## Relationship to prior work

Chalendar and Partington (arXiv:2403.16255, first posted 2024-03-24), Section 2.4 and Theorem 2.16, establish uniqueness from **full continuous data** on two nested circles for holomorphic functions. Their paper does not make the finite-eight-sample assertion for quadratic polynomials or give the present entire eight-point Hermitian-lift spectrum. The passage from continuous circular data to optimal discrete sampling is not a direct corollary of that theorem.

Bodmann and Hammen (arXiv:1302.5487), Section 2, give low-redundancy polynomial phase-retrieving measurements on suitably intersecting circles. Their displayed construction has different geometry and does not cover arbitrary concentric rotated four-point orbits. Philipp (arXiv:1403.4769), Theorem 1.1, gives \(4N-4\) measurements using a mixture of polynomial and derivative values on a circle, rather than eight direct point values of the same quadratic polynomial on two concentric squares. Both are important prior minimal-size constructions; minimal cardinality alone is not claimed new here.

## Limitations

The if-and-only-if geometry is only asserted for two concentric four-point orbits and degree at most two. The explicit squared singular values and global bound are for the particular eight Gaussian-integer positions. The global stability bound need not be best possible among differences of rank-one positive matrices; no optimal inverse algorithm or noise distribution is claimed. Known general phase-retrieval theorems establish minimal-size existence, and an undiscovered equivalent finite-sampling construction remains a bibliographic risk. Priority has not been independently certified.
