# Scientific review

## Correctness — PASS

For every unary function on at most \(d\) points, the orbit of each point has preperiod less than \(d\) and period dividing \(d!\); hence its \(2d\)-th and \(2(d+d!)\)-th iterates agree. A finite ordinary matrix therefore assigns the same value to both disjunction formulas under any valuation. Conversely, the primary source's dynamic table has \(1\widetilde\vee k=\{1,k\}\). With \(p=1\), \(q=k\), the two distinct formulas can be assigned independently, first designated and second undesignated. The valuation extends by the nonempty choice condition. For a finite family, take its largest size. The quantifiers and designated-value condition match the source's multiple-conclusion semantics. A verifier checks the small-carrier ingredients, not the general theorem.

## Originality — PASS, qualified

The general phenomenon that finite nondeterministic matrices may escape finite deterministic characterizations is prior art (Avron–Lev 2005). The accessible Avron–Zamansky survey's Theorem 24 is **restricted to two-valued Nmatrices**, so it does not literally entail this three-valued case. Avron–Konikowska 2005 introduces dynamic/static semantics and completeness schemes, not this exact 2026 operation. In Aldini et al. 2026, Theorem 8 supplies only a one-way five-valued projection; the paper does not assert an impossibility of all finite deterministic characteristic families. The argument provides a uniform explicit depth witness for the newly defined choice table. The broader historical literature remains an acknowledged possible priority risk; one closely relevant older primary article was accessible only through its abstract.

## Value — PASS

The fresh model's deterministic five-valued *refinement* could otherwise be mistaken for an exact truth-functional semantics for dynamic consequence. The result gives a sharp qualitative boundary (no finite deterministic characteristic family) and a concrete finite-size obstruction for every proposed semantics, while preserving a natural dynamic/static distinction important to reasoning about computational errors. It is not merely a relabelling of the author's valuation table; it establishes an impossibility theorem across arbitrary finite carriers and arbitrary deterministic operations.
