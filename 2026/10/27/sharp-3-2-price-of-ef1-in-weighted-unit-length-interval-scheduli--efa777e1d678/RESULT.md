# Sharp \(3/2\) price of EF1 in weighted unit-length interval scheduling on two machines

## Finding
For any finite instance of **identical-weight, two-machine, unit-length interval scheduling**, an EF1-feasible allocation achieves at least \(2/3\) of the maximum unconstrained total weight. The optimal universal ratio of unconstrained welfare to the best EF1 welfare is therefore \(3/2\) (the supremum over instances with positive optimum). There is a polynomial-time constructive guarantee. The sharp three-job obstruction attaining the limiting lower bound was previously given by Borst, Shahkarami, and Vaish; the asserted new contribution is the matching upper bound for arbitrary common item weights.

## Assumptions and scope
Let \(J\) be any finite set of jobs. Each job \(j\) specifies the half-open interval \([s_j,s_j+\ell)\) on the real line for one common duration \(\ell>0\), and a weight \(w_j\ge0\). There are exactly two identical machines, which also constitute the two agents; both value job \(j\) by \(w_j\). An assignment \((A,B)\) is feasible if each job is assigned at most once and intervals assigned to the same machine are pairwise disjoint. Write \(w(C)=\sum_{j\in C}w_j\), and \(\operatorname{OPT}=\max_{(A,B)\text{ feasible}}(w(A)+w(B))\). EF1 means \(w(A)\le w(B)+\max_{j\in A}w_j\) and \(w(B)\le w(A)+\max_{j\in B}w_j\), with the maximum of an empty set interpreted as zero. These are the ordinary additive EF1 inequalities, not inequalities conditional on the ability to reschedule the envied bundle. If \(\operatorname{OPT}=0\), the approximation inequality is vacuous.

**Theorem.** There exists a feasible EF1 allocation \((A,B)\) satisfying \(w(A)+w(B)\ge \frac23\operatorname{OPT}\). Such an allocation can be computed in polynomial time for rational input data. The coefficient \(\frac23\) cannot be raised universally.

## Proof
**1. Obtain an unconstrained optimum.** Sort the distinct interval endpoints and build a directed acyclic time-expanded network: consecutive endpoint arcs have capacity two and cost zero; each interval has an arc from its start to its finish, capacity one, and cost \(-w_j\). A minimum-cost flow of value two produces an optimum set \(S\) of jobs schedulable on two machines. Equivalently, choose any weight-maximizing two-machine schedule. Every time point is covered by at most two intervals from \(S\).

**2. The selected conflict graph is a disjoint union of paths.** Connect two jobs of \(S\) if their half-open intervals intersect. This is an interval graph, hence chordal. A triangle would give three intervals with common intersection, by the Helly property for intervals, contradicting two-machine feasibility. Thus the graph has no triangle, and chordality makes it acyclic. Unit-length intervals cannot induce a claw: if one interval meets three pairwise disjoint intervals of the same length, the earliest and latest of those three cannot both meet it. Consequently every vertex has degree at most two; an acyclic graph of maximum degree two is a disjoint union of paths and isolated vertices.

**3. Lose at most one third while destroying all paths of length two.** For each path, enumerate its vertices consecutively as \(v_0,\ldots,v_{t-1}\). Partition these vertices into the three residue classes of their indices modulo three and delete the class of smallest total weight. The deleted weight is at most one third of this path's weight. Every three consecutive path vertices contain a deleted vertex, so the induced graph on surviving vertices has maximum degree at most one: it is a matching together with isolated vertices. Summing over components gives a surviving set \(T\) with \(w(T)\ge\frac23 w(S)=\frac23\operatorname{OPT}\).

**4. A weighted matching conflict graph admits an EF1 orientation without discarding more jobs.** Process the components of the surviving conflict graph in arbitrary order. In a singleton component of weight \(x\), assign its item to either machine, changing the difference \(D=w(A)-w(B)\) by \(\pm x\). In an edge component with weights \(x,y\), put one item on each machine; the difference changes by \(\pm(x-y)\). Always choose the orientation with smaller absolute new difference (equivalently, choose the available sign opposite the present difference). This preserves feasibility because different components have no conflicting pairs.

We prove by induction the **witness invariant**: whenever \(D>0\), the currently heavier machine \(A\) contains an individual item of weight at least \(D\); whenever \(D<0\), the heavier machine \(B\) contains an item of weight at least \(-D\). Initially it is vacuous. Let \(\delta\ge0\) be the absolute increment attainable in the current component. For a singleton, \(\delta=x\) and the component contributes one item of that weight to the machine that receives the positive increment. For an edge, \(\delta=|x-y|\), and the machine receiving the positive increment obtains the larger of the two weights, which is at least \(\delta\). If the sign of the updated difference is unchanged, its magnitude does not increase and the previous witness remains. If the sign changes, the newly received item has weight at least \(\delta\ge |D_{\rm new}|\), providing the witness. If the difference becomes zero, EF1 holds trivially. After all components, the witness invariant is precisely both EF1 inequalities. Hence all jobs of \(T\) are assigned EF1, completing the upper bound.

**5. Sharpness.** For any \(0<\varepsilon<1\), consider three jobs on intervals \([0,1)\), \([1/2,3/2)\), \([1,2)\), with respective weights \(1,1-\varepsilon,1\). The unconstrained optimum assigns both outer jobs to one machine and the middle job to the other, giving \(\operatorname{OPT}=3-\varepsilon\). This sole feasible placement of all three jobs is not EF1: the machine with the middle job has value \(1-\varepsilon\), but the other bundle retains value \(1\) after deletion of either single outer job. An EF1 assignment can keep both outer jobs, one per machine, of total value \(2\); any other EF1 subset has value at most \(2\). Therefore \(\operatorname{OPT}/\operatorname{OPT}_{\rm EF1}=(3-\varepsilon)/2\to 3/2\) as \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\). This three-job lower-bound construction is established in the motivating literature, not claimed as new here.

## Verification
The self-contained Python standard-library program `check.py` verifies the constructive path-deletion/orientation step in exact rational arithmetic, exhausts all \(3^t\) positive integer weight assignments for consecutive half-overlapping paths of lengths \(1\le t\le8\), and checks 520 bounded random-seed instances of up to eight jobs against independent brute-force optimal and EF1 optimal allocations. Five rational sharpness instances are also checked. The test produces `PASS 10365 finite assertions (not an exhaustive proof)`. The universal result follows from the analytical argument above, not from this finite experiment.

## Relationship to prior work
Borst, Shahkarami, and Vaish (arXiv:2608.15159, public August 15, 2026), *Fair Division Meets Scheduling: Approximately Envy-Free Interval Scheduling*, prove the \(3/2\) offline price of fairness for unweighted two-machine unit-length jobs (their Theorem 1) and exhibit the \(3/2\) lower bound for weighted unit-length jobs (their Theorem 3), but do not state the matching weighted upper bound; their general weighted performance guarantee is weaker. The present theorem supplies this weighted upper bound, using the conflict-path structure and a weighted EF1 matching-orientation invariant. Equbal et al. (arXiv:2605.09930, public May 11, 2026), *Fair Allocation under Conflict Constraints*, establish a different existence objective for general conflict graphs: maximal feasible EF1 allocations, without the present welfare approximation bound. These statements do not imply the weighted two-machine result. Other studies of equitable scheduling and conflict graphs use distinct fairness or welfare metrics.

## Limitations
Nonidentical valuations, unequal job lengths, more than two machines, and strategic misreporting are outside the statement. The ratio \(3/2\) is a supremum, approached rather than attained by the displayed strictly positive three-job family. Equality at zero optimum does not define a price-of-fairness ratio. Literature searches do not prove global novelty; equivalent results may exist in papers not indexed or inspected. The computational program covers only finite examples and is not an infinite proof. Independent review has not been performed.
