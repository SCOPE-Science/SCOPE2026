# Scientific review

## Correctness — PASS

The exact benefit-from-distance formula gives the candidate's expected vote share. For every \(q>1\), strict convexity on both half-intervals forces each best response into the three observed ideal-point locations, so the six possible asymmetric anchor profiles reduce to an exact payoff comparison \((1-e)2^{-q}\gtreqless e\). The \(q=1\) piecewise-affine best-response analysis identifies every flat interval at the critical cost law and excludes all other interior-policy equilibrium profiles. Direct exact turnout evaluation proves the discontinuity. The reference verifier passed 13,750 finite rational equilibrium checks and 189 finite midpoint-convexity inequalities; the analytical steps, not the enumeration, prove arbitrary real inputs.

## Originality — PASS, qualified

The underlying costly-participation mechanism and power-cost cases are present in Wolf and Meir (2026); the exact three-point voter configuration and policy-divergence theme appear much earlier in Kamada and Kojima (2014), so neither is claimed as new. The earlier work's three-*policy* candidate action set and probabilistic costless-voting payoffs with winning preferences do not imply the current continuous-policy, unnormalized vote-share, zero-co-location-turnout equilibrium geometry or the jump formula. Bhaskar and Pyne (2024) handle discrete electorates but use compulsory nearest-candidate ballots. Comparative searches found no equivalent result; historical-priority uncertainty remains and the search's absence of a match is not a proof of novelty.

## Mathematical value — PASS

The distinction between majority policy preference and endogenous participation has a sharp quantifiable consequence: for \(q=2\), \(1/5<e<1/4\) yields strict centrist majorities but both pure equilibria place candidates at opposite endpoints. The complete continuous-action equilibrium classification, critical continuum at \(q=1\), and positive turnout discontinuity are motivated mathematical descriptions of a natural minimal polarized electorate, not arbitrary parameter sampling. They supply an explicit boundary case against smooth-density conclusions, without claiming smooth-perturbation robustness.

## Closest work and risks

Kamada–Kojima (2014) is a particularly close three-ideal-point predecessor but has a different candidate action set and payoff; Wolf–Meir (2026) supplies the behavioral model but imposes positive continuous voter density. The main remaining scientific risk is historical priority under another turnout or probabilistic-voting parameterization; results do not extend by assertion beyond the specified atomic model.
