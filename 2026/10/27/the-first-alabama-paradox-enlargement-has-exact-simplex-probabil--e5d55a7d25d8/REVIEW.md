## Correctness — PASS
The shift representation of largest-remainder apportionment proves that a losing party must have two larger parties gain one seat each. This rules out any loss before a three-seat house and identifies the only possible three-to-four transition as \((1,1,1)\to(2,2,0)\). Quotient and fractional-remainder inequalities give the iff chamber \(w-u<1/3\), \(v-u>1/4\). Two exact rational triangular integrations contribute \(1/1728\) each; six label permutations with simplex density \(2\) give \(1/72\). The executable independent arithmetic implementation reproduced \(153762\) tie-free rational profiles and both exact areas. The continuum proof does not depend on the finite check.

The closest fully inspected scientific source, Janson–Linusson (arXiv:1104.2137), analyzes the large-house Cesàro frequency at fixed population shares and its average \(1/36\); that is different in quantifiers and does not imply the finite first-expansion value. ## Value — PASS
The three-party, three-to-four-seat transition is the first possible Alabama-paradox enlargement, not an arbitrary parameter slice. Its exact incidence under a canonical uniform-simplex model quantifies the smallest nontrivial house-monotonicity defect and supplies a complete open-region geometry, making the result useful as a baseline for finite-size apportionment comparisons. The older asymptotic population-average probability cannot substitute for this fixed finite transition.

## Limits
The result concerns three states, deterministic largest-remainder allocations away from ties, and uniform random shares. It is not a general formula for more states or arbitrary house sizes, nor a statement about periodic frequencies for a fixed rational population vector. Historical completeness of the Bradberry comparison is unverified.
