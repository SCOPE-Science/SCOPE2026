# A single torus from short-period triangular strips, with an isolated four-cycle exception

## Finding
For an integer \(n\ge3\), let \(G_n=K_3\mathbin{\square}C_n\) be the Cartesian graph product, with the usual simple cycle \(C_n\). Let \(\mathcal N(G_n)\) be the **open** Lovász neighborhood complex: a nonempty vertex set is a face exactly when all its vertices share a graph neighbor. Then
\[
\boxed{\mathcal N(G_n)\searrow T_n\cong_{\mathrm{PL}}S^1\times S^1\quad(n=3\text{ or }n\ge5),}
\]
where \(T_n\) is a connected, orientable, triangulated closed surface having \(3n\) vertices, \(9n\) edges, and \(6n\) triangles. The collapse uses exactly \(6n\) elementary free-face pairs. The **only excluded cycle length** \(n=4\) has
\[
\chi(\mathcal N(G_4))=6\ne0,
\]
so its neighborhood complex is not homotopy equivalent to a torus. Consequently, for \(n=3\) and all \(n\ge5\), the integral homology is \(\mathbb Z,\mathbb Z^2,\mathbb Z\) in degrees \(0,1,2\), the fundamental group is \(\mathbb Z^2\), and the product of the two integral degree-one generators is a generator of degree-two cohomology. This last ring statement is for the stated toroidal cases only.

For every multiple \(n\ge6\) of \(3\), \(G_n\) is not a circulant graph: these infinitely many cases are outside earlier four-regular-circulant classifications, not just new labels for covered cyclic cases.

## Assumptions and scope
Take vertices \(v_{a,i}\) for \(a\in\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z\), \(i\in\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z\). Two vertices are adjacent precisely when their first coordinates differ and second coordinates agree, or their first coordinates agree and second coordinates are consecutive in \(C_n\). Thus
\[
N(v_{a,i})=\{v_{a+1,i},v_{a-1,i},v_{a,i+1},v_{a,i-1}\}.
\]
The center \(v_{a,i}\) does **not** belong to its own open neighborhood. Every vertex has degree four, including \(n=3\). We identify simplices with unordered vertex sets; all free-face removals are ordinary elementary simplicial collapses. A PL torus refers to the resulting two-dimensional core, not to the original tetrahedral complex.

## Proof
**1. Canonical tetrahedra and unique horizontal pairs.** Write \(A=v_{a+1,i}\), \(B=v_{a-1,i}\), \(U=v_{a,i+1}\), and \(D=v_{a,i-1}\). The neighborhood complex is generated by tetrahedra \(Q_{a,i}=\{A,B,U,D\}\). The two distinct vertices \(A\) and \(B\), lying in the same triangular column, have the **unique** common graph neighbor \(v_{a,i}\). Indeed, a neighbor of both cannot leave that column, because an edge changing columns preserves the first coordinate, while \(A\) and \(B\) have different first coordinates. Hence their only common neighbor is the third member of the column. Every face containing \(\{A,B\}\) belongs to exactly this neighborhood tetrahedron. In particular, all \(3n\) tetrahedra are distinct.

**2. Uniform explicit collapses.** For each center \((a,i)\), remove the triangle-tetrahedron free pair \((\{A,B,U\},Q_{a,i})\). Every such triangle is free by uniqueness of the center of \(\{A,B\}\); the operations at distinct centers are disjoint. The only remaining triangle containing \(\{A,B\}\) is then \(\{A,B,D\}\). Remove the edge-triangle free pair \((\{A,B\},\{A,B,D\})\). Again distinct centers have disjoint pairs. Thus exactly \(6n\) elementary collapses leave precisely
\[
T_n=\left\langle\{v_{a+1,i},v_{a,i+1},v_{a,i-1}\},\{v_{a-1,i},v_{a,i+1},v_{a,i-1}\}:a\in\mathbb Z/3,\ i\in\mathbb Z/n\right\rangle.
\]
This collapse argument is valid even for \(n=4\); the distinction lies in the topology of its residual complex.

**3. A two-sheeted planar model.** On the universal square lattice \(\mathbb Z^2\), define for each center \(c\) the diamond with vertices \(c\pm e_1,c\pm e_2\), triangulated by the vertical diagonal joining \(c+e_2\) to \(c-e_2\). Diamonds whose centers have even coordinate sum form an edge-to-edge triangulation of one plane; centers of odd sum triangulate a second, disjoint plane (their vertex parities differ). Translation by the period lattice
\[
L_n=\langle(3,0),(0,n)\rangle
\]
acts on this disjoint union. The even-period subgroup
\[
L_n^{\mathrm{even}}=\{(3r,ns):r,s\in\mathbb Z,\ 3r+ns\equiv0\pmod2\}
\]
preserves each plane. If \(n=3\) or \(n\ge5\), **every nonzero element of this subgroup has Manhattan length at least six**. To see this, a nonzero horizontal-only period has length at least six because \(r\) must be even; a nonzero vertical-only period has length at least six because \(ns\) must be even; and a mixed period has length at least \(3+n\ge6\). Consequently the quotient of either triangulated plane by \(L_n^{\mathrm{even}}\) is a genuine simplicial triangulation: distinct vertices of a triangle stay distinct, and two triangles of diameter two cannot acquire the same vertex set unless they are deck translates (differences of the required deck translations would have length at most four). The quotient is a PL orientable torus, since it is a plane triangulation modulo a rank-two lattice of orientation-preserving translations.

The period \((3,0)\) has odd coordinate sum, so it swaps the two planar sheets. Therefore the quotient of both sheets by the full \(L_n\) is exactly the single torus obtained from either sheet by \(L_n^{\mathrm{even}}\). The triangles in this quotient are exactly the two survivors per neighborhood center in Step 2, and their vertices are exactly the vertices of \(G_n\). This identifies \(T_n\) simplicially with the claimed torus. Since each of the \(3n\) diamond centers supplies two distinct triangles and every edge has two incident triangles, \(f(T_n)=(3n,9n,6n)\).

**4. Why four is exceptional.** At \(n=4\), the original tetrahedral neighborhood complex has twelve vertices and twelve distinct tetrahedra. It has twelve pairs inside the triangular columns, six opposite pairs of the form \(\{v_{a,i-1},v_{a,i+1}\}\) (each pair arises from two opposite cycle centers), and twenty-four cross-column pairs: hence forty-two edges. Each tetrahedron has four distinct triangular faces not repeated as triangles of another neighborhood tetrahedron, giving forty-eight triangles. Its face vector is therefore \((12,42,48,12)\), and its Euler characteristic is \(12-42+48-12=6\). Since the torus has Euler characteristic zero, the four-cycle case cannot be toroidal. The short even period \((0,4)\) explains why the plane-quotient injectivity argument in Step 3 fails there.

**5. Noncirculant cases not covered by the circulant theorem.** For \(n\ge6\), every graph triangle is a single three-vertex column, since \(C_n\) has no triangles. Thus any automorphism permutes the columns and induces a dihedral automorphism on the quotient cycle. Cross-column edges are perfect matchings, forcing the permutation of the three rows to be the same in every column. Therefore \(\operatorname{Aut}(G_n)\cong S_3\times D_n\), where \(D_n\) is the dihedral group of the \(n\)-cycle. If \(3\mid n\), no element has order \(3n\): possible element orders are least common multiples of an order \(1,2,3\) from \(S_3\) and an order dividing \(n\), or order \(2\), from \(D_n\), all strictly less than \(3n\). A circulant graph on \(3n\) vertices would have a cyclic regular automorphism of order \(3n\). Thus these cases are not circulants.

## Verification
The standalone script `verify_triangular_strip.py` reconstructs neighborhood facets directly from graph adjacency, checks the **individual current-complex free-face condition** for every one of the \(6n\) deletions, tests the exact remaining triangle set, and checks core edge incidences, all vertex links, connectivity, orientability, and Euler characteristic. It exhaustively verifies \(n=3\), and every \(n\) from \(5\) through \(40\), totaling 4,878 verified elementary collapse pairs. It separately checks the exceptional \(n=4\) face vector. The universal statement follows from Steps 1–5, not from finite sampling. Python 3 and its standard library suffice.

## Relationship to prior work
Kozlov [1] supplies a classical simple-homotopy framework for neighborhood complexes but does not give this product-family classification. Shukla [2, Theorems 2.2–2.6] classifies the neighborhoods of four-regular **circulant** graphs; in particular, some of the coprime rectangular-product cases belong to that earlier coverage. They are not claimed new here. The new infinite subfamily with \(3\mid n\), \(n\ge6\), is proved noncirculant in Step 5. The publicly available high-systole square-grid classification [3] assumes every nonzero period has Manhattan length at least five, so it excludes **every** graph here because \((3,0)\) is a period. The published product classification [4] assumes \(K_m\mathbin{\square}C_n\) with \(m\ge4\) and does not cover the \(m=3\) family, where the topology changes to a torus with a nontrivial degree-one cup product. Both [3] and [4] have related local collapses; the present contribution is the exact treatment of the short triangular period, the isolated four-cycle obstruction, and the noncirculant infinite subfamily. No originality claim is made for the general idea of the free-face toggle.

## Limitations
Only the **open** neighborhood complexes of these graph products are classified. For \(n=4\) only the Euler characteristic obstruction is claimed, not a full homotopy type. The torus result is not an equivariant classification. Although the checked indexed results and cited original papers do not imply the new noncirculant family, unindexed equivalent work may exist; independent verification has not been performed.
