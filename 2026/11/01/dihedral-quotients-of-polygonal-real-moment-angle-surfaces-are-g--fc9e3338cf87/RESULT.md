# Dihedral quotients of polygonal real moment-angle surfaces are graphs up to homotopy

## Finding
For every integer \(n\geq5\), let \(C_n\) be the simplicial one-dimensional cycle on \(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z\). Define its real moment-angle surface
\[
X_n=(D^1,S^0)^{C_n}\subseteq[-1,1]^n,
\]
where a point is allowed to have coordinates in \((-1,1)\) only on a face of \(C_n\), and \(S^0=\{-1,1\}\). Let \(D_n\) be the order-\(2n\) dihedral group acting by coordinate permutations induced by symmetries of the cycle. Its **ordinary topological quotient** \(Q_n=|X_n|/D_n\) is a compact connected surface with nonempty boundary, and
\[
\boxed{\displaystyle Q_n\simeq\bigvee^{r_n}S^1,\qquad
r_n=1+2^{n-3}-\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)2^{n/d}.}
\]
In particular, \(\pi_1(Q_n)\) is a free group of rank \(r_n\), \(H_1(Q_n;\mathbb Z)\cong\mathbb Z^{r_n}\), and \(\widetilde H_i(Q_n;\mathbb Z)=0\) for \(i\ne1\).

Moreover, **for odd \(n\ge5\)** the boundary has exactly \(2^{(n-3)/2}\) connected components. No assertion about the orientability or exact boundary-component count for even \(n\) is needed for, or implied by, the homotopy classification.

## Assumptions and scope
The cycle is the boundary of a combinatorial polygon, not its filled two-simplex. Every \(X_n\) uses the cubical polyhedral-product construction, not the complex moment-angle space \((D^2,S^1)^{C_n}\). A cell of \(X_n\) has \(j\) free coordinates, \(0\le j\le2\); for \(j=2\) the coordinates must be adjacent in the cycle. The dihedral action permutes coordinates, without changing their signs. All quotient spaces below are ordinary topological quotients, not Borel homotopy quotients or orbifold classifying spaces. All displayed statements hold simultaneously for every integer \(n\ge5\).

## Proof
**1. Surface and cell structure.** Each vertex of the cubical complex is a sign vector in \(\{\pm1\}^n\), and its link is the \(n\)-cycle, so its neighborhood is a disk. The same is clear at interiors of edges and squares. The cube graph is connected and is contained in \(X_n\); thus \(X_n\) is a compact connected closed surface. Its cell numbers in degrees zero, one and two are \(2^n\), \(n2^{n-1}\), and \(n2^{n-2}\). Therefore
\[
\chi(X_n)=(4-n)2^{n-2}.
\]
The usual polygonal orientation is respected by rotations of the indices and reversed by reflections: orient each square in the ordered adjacent coordinates and multiply by the product of the signs of its other \(n-2\) coordinates. Along a shared cubical edge the induced boundary orientations cancel. Cyclic index shifts preserve this fundamental cycle, whereas reversal of cyclic order negates it.

**2. Nonidentity rotations.** For \(1\le k<n\), a point fixed by the index shift \(r^k\) cannot have a free coordinate: its set of free coordinates is rotation invariant, but no nonempty face (singleton or adjacent pair) of \(C_n\) is invariant under a nonidentity rotation when \(n\ge5\). Hence its fixed points are precisely periodic sign vectors, constant on the \(\gcd(n,k)\) coordinate orbits. In particular,
\[
\chi(X_n^{r^k})=2^{\gcd(n,k)}.
\]

**3. Reflection-fixed circles.** Fix a reflection \(s\). Each of its index orbits has size one or two. An orbit consisting of two nonadjacent vertices is necessarily at an endpoint sign in every \(s\)-fixed point of \(X_n\). There are exactly two exceptional orbits whose coordinates can vary. If \(n\) is odd, they are the one fixed vertex and the opposite swapped adjacent pair. If \(n\) is even, a vertex-axis reflection has two singleton orbits, whereas an edge-axis reflection has two swapped adjacent pairs. In every case the two exceptional supports are nonadjacent to each other (this uses \(n\ge5\)); hence their two free real parameters must lie in the boundary of a square. Every assignment of signs to the remaining orbits produces an independent copy of \(\partial[-1,1]^2\cong S^1\). Therefore
\[
X_n^s\cong\begin{cases}
\bigsqcup^{2^{(n-3)/2}}S^1,&n\text{ odd},\\
\bigsqcup^{2^{(n-2)/2}}S^1,&n\text{ even and }s\text{ fixes two indices},\\
\bigsqcup^{2^{(n-4)/2}}S^1,&n\text{ even and }s\text{ fixes no indices}.
\end{cases}
\]
Thus \(\chi(X_n^s)=0\) for all reflections.

**4. The ordinary quotient is a surface with boundary.** The square metric on \(X_n\) is invariant under the finite dihedral action. Its local models are disks or Euclidean cone disks; a finite point stabilizer acts through rotations and possibly reflections of the local link circle. Accordingly the orbit neighborhood is topologically a disk or half-disk (a sector is homeomorphic to a half-disk). The quotient is a compact connected topological surface. A point chosen in the interior of one of the reflection-fixed intervals has stabilizer generated by that reflection only: its free-coordinate support is not invariant under a nonidentity rotation. Its image has a half-disk neighborhood. Hence \(\partial Q_n\ne\varnothing\).

**5. Euler characteristic and homotopy.** Subdivide the squares equivariantly to obtain an admissible finite triangulation. Burnside's Euler-characteristic formula then gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\chi(Q_n)
&=\frac{1}{2n}\left((4-n)2^{n-2}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}2^{\gcd(n,k)}\right)\\
&=-2^{n-3}+\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)2^{n/d}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The second equality uses \(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}2^{\gcd(n,k)}=\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)2^{n/d}\). Every compact connected surface with nonempty boundary admits a graph spine, hence is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of \(1-\chi\) circles. Applying that classification to \(Q_n\) proves the displayed formula for \(r_n\), its fundamental group, and its integral homology.

**6. Boundary count for odd \(n\).** Let \(Y_n=X_n/\langle r\rangle\) and let \(\bar s\) be the involution induced by a reflection. Suppose \(n\) is odd. Every reflection \(r^a s\) is conjugate to \(s\) by some rotation, because multiplication by two is invertible modulo \(n\). This makes \(X_n^s\to Y_n^{\bar s}\) surjective. It is injective as well: if \(x,y\in X_n^s\) and \(y=r^a x\), then \(r^{2a}x=x\); since \(n\) is odd this implies \(r^a x=x\), so \(y=x\). Compactness makes the continuous bijection a homeomorphism. Rotation preserves the orientation of \(X_n\), while \(\bar s\) reverses the orientation of the quotient surface \(Y_n\). The boundary of \(Q_n=Y_n/\langle\bar s\rangle\) is therefore exactly the image of \(Y_n^{\bar s}\), a disjoint union of \(2^{(n-3)/2}\) circles. This proves the supplementary boundary count.

## Verification
`verify.py` independently enumerates the orbit triangles, edges and vertices in an equivariant diagonal triangulation of the full cubical surface for \(5\le n\le10\). It directly checks the resulting Euler characteristics against the formula, and constructs the mirror-edge graph to count boundary components. Its recorded output is in `verify_output.txt`; a clean invocation is `python3 verify.py`. Examples of the pair (free rank, number of boundary circles) are \((1,2)\), \((2,2)\), \((7,4)\), \((15,4)\), \((35,8)\), \((75,10)\) for \(n=5,6,7,8,9,10\), respectively. The algorithmic checks are finite corroboration, not a substitute for the uniform argument above.

## Relationship to prior work
Grujić and Welker (2012) study polyhedral products and their homotopy types. Das (first preprint 2018) determines the **cyclic rotation quotient** \(X_n/C_n\) as an orientable closed surface and calculates its genus via binary necklaces. In fact, the free rank \(r_n\) in this finding equals that previously established cyclic-quotient genus; that numerical identity is fully acknowledged. The **new statement** is that imposing the additional polygon reflections turns the *ordinary quotient* into a bordered surface homotopy equivalent to a free graph, with the explicit reflection-mirror and odd-boundary classification. It is not implied by the cyclic quotient genus alone: the topology of an involution quotient depends on its fixed-set and orientation behavior.

## Limitations
This theorem is not a homeomorphism classification of the even-polygon quotient surfaces: their numbers of boundary components and orientability are not asserted. It gives the exact homotopy type, integral homology, and fundamental-group isomorphism type in all polygon sizes \(n\ge5\), and the number of boundary circles when \(n\) is odd. No conclusions are claimed for arbitrary group actions, other simplicial spheres, Borel quotients, or coefficients that modify the group action. Literature equivalence outside the identified references remains a residual originality risk; no independent mathematical audit has been performed.
