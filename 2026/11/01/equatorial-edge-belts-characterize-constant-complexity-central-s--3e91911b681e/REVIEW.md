# Scientific review: equatorial edge belts and constant central-section complexity

## Correctness
**PASS.** For a three-dimensional convex polytope and a generic central plane, section vertices are precisely the crossed original edges. At a wall \(u\cdot v=0\) of normal space, central symmetry makes the change in the section vertex count twice the signed imbalance of neighbors of \(v\). Vanishing across every wall forces antipodal balance of projected incident edge directions by the planar sign-jump lemma. This pairing produces two edges per vertex in each origin-spanned plane; convexity forces their cycles to be full equatorial section polygons. Conversely each polygon belt contributes exactly two crossings, so a partition into \(r\) belts gives \(2r\) sides. All directions, degenerate-wall exclusions, noncollinearity of neighboring vertices with the origin, and affine invariance are addressed in the proof.

The standalone exact-arithmetic program reconstructs the six decagonal belts of the icosidodecahedron, four hexagonal belts of the cuboctahedron, and three square belts of the octahedron; it checks 960, 720 and 1,000 generic integer normals, respectively, and the two cube counterexamples. Finite checks corroborate examples, not the infinite theorem.

## Originality
**PASS under inspected-source evidence, with historical-priority risk.** The closest 2026 publication by Cousaert proves an edge–2-face compensation characterization for constant-size orthogonal **projections** (arXiv:2601.13095, Theorem 1.2), which has a different quantifier and geometric operation. The present local vertex-sign criterion and equatorial decomposition concern fixed-size **central sections**, and neither theorem implies the other. For example, a cube has constant generic shadow complexity but nonconstant central section complexity. Brandenburg–De Loera–Luo–Meroni (arXiv:2603.25643, Section 2.1) give the generic edge-cut description of a slice and a sweep-arrangement framework; those methods do not assert that globally constant section counts are equivalent to an equatorial edge partition. Birkemeyer–Brose–Brandenburg–Prün (arXiv:2609.16195) address reconstruction from section-combinatorics and generic simple polytopes; only their abstract could be inspected, so potential hidden equivalences are a residual risk. Earlier equatorial polygons of the Archimedean examples are not claimed as original.

Searches of published mathematical records using both precise formulations and terms such as 'equisectable', 'equatorial cycles', 'antipodal projected neighbors' and 'constant central polygon count' returned adjacent papers on the icosahedron, cubical hyperplane sections and projections, but none with an equivalence for every centrally symmetric convex 3-polytope. Search failure alone is not treated as a proof of priority; the mathematical implication comparisons, not keyword overlaps, support the provisional originality assessment.

## Value
**PASS.** This is a necessary-and-sufficient finite structural criterion for an entire natural class of three-dimensional polytopes, rather than another calculated percentage or an isolated numeric example. It reduces a continuum of central-section tests to finitely many local antipodal checks or planar edge cycles and explains the constant hexagonal, octagonal and dodecagonal section laws of three canonical uniform polyhedra. The distinction from equiprojectivity connects geometric tomography with the current characterization problem for projections. Applications outside centrally symmetric dimension three are unproved.
