# Scientific review

## Correctness
The iid circle points induce exchangeable \(\operatorname{Dirichlet}(1,\ldots,1)\) cyclic gaps when one sample is held as a rotational root. The one-gap beta density and the two-gap Dirichlet marginal yield the exact first and second moments with fully specified normalizations. The variance decomposition is a count of diagonal and ordered off-diagonal terms. The Taylor remainder and Dirichlet factorial moments justify each asymptotic correction; the numerical checker independently corroborates finite sample instances.

## Originality
The mean law of Nikitin--Polevaya and classical leading-variance central limit results are prior work and explicitly excluded from the novelty claim. The 2023 Wang--Li--Xu open full text includes the Dirichlet moment identity and asymptotic powers but was inspected for finite-size formulas: it does not state the complete finite-\(n\) perimeter variance or the two next coefficients. Related 2018 and 2011 primary texts were only partially accessible, so historical priority remains provisional rather than certified. Searches for a corresponding exact moment formula in relevant mathematical records did not return a covering claim. The exact finite-\(n\) result is mathematically derivable from classical identities, but those identities alone do not give its explicitly evaluated finite-size law or the cancellation-sensitive higher-order coefficients.

## Mathematical value
Random polygon perimeters are classical estimators of circle length. Their exact sampling dispersion is useful at moderate finite sample sizes, where the leading \(n^{-5}\) approximation has a substantial correction. The two derived correction terms quantify that discrepancy and the exact second moment accounts for dependence among consecutive chords; the quantity is intrinsic to the model rather than an arbitrary statistic. There is no claim of a new central limit theorem.

## Limitations and nearest literature
The distribution is iid and arclength uniform on the unit circle. The full variance expression is a one-parameter finite-sample law, not a full distribution or a general-curve result. The 2011 Bélisle and 2018 Wang--Xu full texts were not available for comprehensive comparison, and the 2020 full text had incomplete rendering, leaving residual historical-priority risk. Computational examples do not substitute for the proof.
