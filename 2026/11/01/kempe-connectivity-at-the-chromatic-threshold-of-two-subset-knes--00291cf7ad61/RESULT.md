# Kempe connectivity at the chromatic threshold of two-subset Kneser graphs

## Finding
For every integer \(n\ge4\), all proper vertex colourings of the two-subset Kneser graph \(KG(n,2)\) using its chromatic palette of \(n-2\) **individually named** colours belong to a single Kempe-equivalence class. A Kempe move interchanges two colour names on one connected component of their bichromatic induced graph, without using an additional colour. In contrast, the single-vertex recolouring graph is a disjoint union of \(\binom n3(n-2)!\) hypercubes of dimension \(\binom{n-3}{2}\). Thus Kempe-chain moves restore global accessibility even when every single-site component is small.

## Assumptions and scope
Write \([n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}\), and identify a vertex \(\{i,j\}\) of \(KG(n,2)\) with the corresponding edge of \(K_n\); two Kneser vertices are adjacent exactly when these edges are disjoint. Colourings are functions to a fixed labelled palette \([n-2]\), without quotienting by vertex or colour permutations. The induced two-colour graph uses only Kneser edges. A Kempe chain is one of its connected components, including an isolated vertex. A Kempe move swaps the two colours on that component. \(Q_d\) is the standard \(d\)-cube and \(Q_0=K_1\). Statements include \(n=4\) with a zero-dimensional single-site component. The earlier classification of optimal colourings is credited below; the main assertion is connectivity under Kempe moves, not a new classification of static colourings.

## Proof
**(1) Static normal form, from the established classification.** Every proper \((n-2)\)-colouring has exactly one triangular colour class, the three edges on a unique triple \(Q\subset[n]\). Write \(S=[n]\setminus Q\). Each \(s\in S\) is the centre of one of the remaining \(n-3\) distinct colour classes, with colour \(c_s\), and the triangle has colour \(c_Q\). The edges \(\{s,q\}\), \(q\in Q\), all have colour \(c_s\); every edge \(\{s,t\}\) with \(s,t\in S\) has independently either \(c_s\) or \(c_t\). The colours \(c_Q,(c_s)_{s\in S}\) are all distinct. Conversely, every such assignment is proper. This is the optimal colouring structure given in the work on locating colourings cited below; the same form also follows inductively from the elementary fact that pairwise-intersecting edges of a complete graph lie in a star or triangle. At \(n=4\), the same description follows directly because \(KG(4,2)\) consists of three disjoint edges.

**(2) Independent singleton switches.** For any distinct \(s,t\in S\), the Kneser vertex \(\{s,t\}\) may be recoloured between \(c_s\) and \(c_t\), leaving every other vertex unchanged; the target colour class is star-centred at the corresponding endpoint. This is a Kempe move: in the bichromatic \(c_s,c_t\) induced graph, this vertex is isolated, because every other vertex of the opposite colour contains that opposite centre, and hence intersects \(\{s,t\}\). Consequently, all assignments to the \(\binom{n-3}{2}\) edges within \(S\) communicate at fixed \(Q\) and fixed assignment of palette colours to centres.

**(3) Transposing two star-centre labels.** For distinct \(s,t\in S\), in the bichromatic subgraph on colours \(c_s,c_t\), the six spoke vertices \(\{s,q\},\{t,q\}\) with \(q\in Q\) form a connected cycle of length six (each spoke is disjoint from precisely two opposite-colour spokes). Every other vertex of either colour, except \(\{s,t\}\), is adjacent to an opposite-colour spoke: its second endpoint is in \(S\setminus\{s,t\}\). Thus there is one large Kempe component containing all these vertices, while \(\{s,t\}\) is an isolated other component. Swapping the large component transposes the star-centre colours \(c_s,c_t\), with the unchanged colour on \(\{s,t\}\) still assigned to one of its two endpoint colours. All star-centre palette labels can therefore be permuted.

**(4) Moving the triangular triple.** Select \(q\in Q\) and \(s\in S\). By singleton switches, arrange that no edge from \(s\) to another element of \(S\) has colour \(c_s\). The \(c_Q,c_s\) induced graph then consists of exactly three disjoint edges: a triangle-edge \(\{a,b\}\subset Q\) is adjacent to just its complementary spoke \(\{s,q\}\), where \(Q=\{a,b,q\}\). Swap this two-vertex Kempe component. Its new triangular colour class is the triangle on \(\{s,a,b\}\) with colour \(c_s\); the excluded vertex \(q\) becomes a star centre with colour \(c_Q\). Every remaining class remains centred at the same element of \(S\setminus\{s\}\). Hence the allowable changes in \(Q\) include every replacement \(Q\mapsto(Q\setminus\{q\})\cup\{s\}\). The Johnson graph on three-subsets is connected, so all triples are mutually accessible.

**(5) Exchanging the triangle colour with a star-centre colour without changing the triangle.** Assume \(n\ge5\), so \(|S|\ge2\), and choose distinct \(s,t\in S\) and \(q\in Q\). First make \(s\) a sink for the centre-edge assignments as in (4), then perform the triangle replacement \(q\leftrightarrow s\). Now the new centre \(q\), carrying the old triangle colour \(c_Q\), is a sink: its edges to other centres have those other centres' colours. Perform the large-component exchange of the two star-centre labels at \(q\) and \(t\), as in (3). The edge \(\{q,t\}\), formerly coloured \(c_t\), has remained unchanged and is now coloured with the new centre colour at \(q\). Change this isolated edge to the new centre colour at \(t\); then \(q\) is again a sink. Reverse the triangle replacement \(s\leftrightarrow q\) as in (4). The original \(Q\) is restored, the triangle's new colour is the old \(c_t\), and the centre \(t\) has the old triangle colour \(c_Q\); all other centre labels are unchanged. Thus any triangle colour can be transposed with any star-centre colour, while preserving \(Q\). Together with (3), all palette assignments are accessible at each \(Q\).

From (2), (4) and (5), any two optimal labelled colourings can be joined by Kempe moves: align their triples, align their palette-label placement, and align their independent centre-edge choices. For \(n=4\), the Kneser graph is three disjoint edges and independently swapping the two colours on any edge connects all eight proper 2-colourings.

For the contrast statement, an ordinary single-site recolouring can alter only an edge \(\{s,t\}\subset S\), toggling its two possible endpoint colours: every triangle edge or spoke fails to intersect all three mandatory spokes of every alternative colour class. Thus its components are exactly the cubes of dimension \(\binom{n-3}{2}\), indexed by \(Q\) and the bijection of the labelled colours to the triangle and centres, giving \(\binom n3(n-2)!\) components.

## Relationship to prior work
The star/triangle form of optimum two-subset Kneser colourings is **previously known**: see the structural theorem quoted as Theorem 2.2 in Jalilolghadra and Behtoei (2023), credited there to the earlier locating-chromatic-number work. Javadi and Omoomi (2009) study the related star/triangle colour-class distinction for \(b\)-colourings. Hajiabolhassan (2009) develops other \(b\)-chromatic results. The Kempe-equivalence literature includes a general theorem for three-colourings of connected cubic graphs other than two exceptional graphs; it covers the Petersen instance \(n=5\), but not this infinite family when \(n\ge6\), because \(KG(n,2)\) then has degree \(\binom{n-2}{2}\), rather than degree three, and its chromatic palette has \(n-2\) colours. The assertion here concerns the global effects of multi-vertex Kempe chain moves on all optimum configurations, whereas the static normal form alone does not directly establish connectivity. No matching general result was found in the inspected sources or mathematical claim searches; this is a best-knowledge assessment, not a claim of exhaustive literature uniqueness.

## Limitations
The theorem concerns \(KG(n,2)\) and its minimum **labelled** palette, not higher-rank Kneser graphs or nonoptimal palettes. It establishes Kempe connectivity, not exact Kempe diameter or mixing time. Its single-site cube decomposition depends on preserving properness with exactly the minimum palette. A covering result under different terminology could exist and would require independent scholarly comparison. No independent audit has been performed.
