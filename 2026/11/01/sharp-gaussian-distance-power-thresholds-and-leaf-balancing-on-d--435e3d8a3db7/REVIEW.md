# Scientific review

## Correctness
PASS. The claim is the exact all-scale Gaussian threshold for the shortest-path metric of double-star trees, including fixed-leaf-count ordering and critical embedding dimensions. Explicit completion of squares proves the necessary-and-sufficient reduction to a two-by-two matrix. Dividing by \(2^p\) makes its derivative positive definite for \(p\ge1\), eliminating multiple-crossing ambiguities. A determinant comparison with rigorously signed factors establishes strict leaf balancing. The balanced root and asymptotics follow from a monotone scalar equation. The embedded checker provides finite corroboration, not infinite certification.

## Originality
PASS under bounded historical comparison. Doust–Weston (arXiv:0705.0411) explicitly compute the single-star exponent, not the two-hub double-star balancing theorem. Li–Weston (arXiv:0901.0695) give general finite-tree bounds and strictness principles; Sánchez (arXiv:1108.0451) gives a general matrix criterion and computes complete bipartite graphs. The general matrix criterion could be used to rederive individual cases, but an explicit two-hub classification and strict balancing ordering were not located in the inspected original results. Remaining risk: an equivalent special-case statement may appear in unindexed graph-metric or tree-kernel literature.

## Mathematical value
PASS. Negative-type exponents govern universal Gaussian-kernel positivity and Euclidean snowflake embeddings. This is a complete infinite-family classification for the simplest trees with two branching hubs, together with a non-obvious strict extremal principle when total leaf count is fixed, one-dimensional critical degeneracy, and a sharp large-size law. It extends well-motivated star calculations beyond a routine parameter substitution; different leaf allocations genuinely alter the critical exponent.

## Limitations
No claim about indefinite kernels at **every** individual bandwidth beyond the threshold. Literature coverage is bounded; the general Sánchez matrix test subsumes the mere ability to compute any fixed finite example, not necessarily the stated leaf-balancing formula. Numerical checks cover finitely many parameters. Independent mathematical validation is outstanding.
