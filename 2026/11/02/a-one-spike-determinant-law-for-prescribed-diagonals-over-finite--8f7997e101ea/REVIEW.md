# Self-review

## Correctness — PASS

For arbitrary prime-power field size and all dimensions at least one, the argument reconstructs a determinant-twisted matrix Gauss sum. Singular test matrices vanish by an arbitrary-determinant stabilizer; full-rank tests reduce by a change of variables to the identity, then unitriangular averaging yields the exact Gauss power. n-dimensional additive Fourier inversion imposes the diagonal, elementary one-dimensional Gauss products yield the full-rank moment, and multiplicative Fourier inversion isolates each nonzero determinant. The characteristic-two and dimension-one boundaries are explicit. A standalone exhaustive finite prime-field checker independently agrees on nine examples, and its bounded scope is not mistaken for a universal proof.

## Originality — PASS, with residual priority risk

The closely matching 2026 Gorodetsky Theorem 1.4 gives totals for prescribed diagonals, but neither its statement nor inspected Section 3.4 supplies the determinant-resolved one-spike law. Previous Kim and Li–Hu matrix Gauss sums target trace-exponential statistics and special/general linear groups, not an explicit all-size, all-diagonal determinant-fiber theorem in the materials accessible here. A generic complete Fourier transform of the special linear group could yield this distribution after nontrivial inversion, creating an important residual implicit-coverage risk; the original full texts of some historical sources were not inspected. published-finding corpus comparisons covered matching determinant-one Fourier-frame work but did not show all-n determinant-fiber coverage. These searches do not prove priority and an independent audit remains required.

## Mathematical value — PASS

The result solves the natural determinant-refinement of a recent exact enumeration uniformly for every finite field and size, revealing a striking dichotomy based only on zero coordinates and a single exact exceptional fiber. The sharp gap is \(q^{\binom n2}\), not an asymptotic error or a mere relabeling of a single observed count. It separates conditional determinant independence in the zero-containing case from a structural bias in the all-nonzero case.
