# A sharp four-round classification of Latin-square incidence graphs

## Finding

Let \(L\) be any Latin square of order \(n\ge2\). Write \(P=R\sqcup C\sqcup S\) for three disjoint copies of its row, column and symbol sets, and \(B\) for the \(n^2\) triples \((r,c,L(r,c))\). Define \(\Lambda(L)\) to be the simple undirected bipartite incidence graph on \(P\sqcup B\), adjacent exactly when a point belongs to a triple. There are no named sorts, group predicates or coordinate labels in the first-order signature, just equality and adjacency.

**Theorem.** Every two graphs \(\Lambda(L),\Lambda(L')\) with orders at least two agree on all first-order sentences of quantifier rank at most three. At quantifier rank four,
\[
\Lambda(L)\equiv_4\Lambda(L')\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
(n=m=2)\ \lor\ (n=m=3)\ \lor\ (n,m\ge4).
\]
This is independent of the isotopy or isomorphism classes of the Latin squares. Thus exactly three rank-four theories occur, with smallest graph representatives of orders \(10,18,28\); the uniform representative ceiling \(28\) is sharp within the family.

## Assumptions and scope

An order-\(n\) Latin square has one occurrence of each symbol in every row and column. Consequently, each point of \(P\) belongs to exactly \(n\) triples and each block of \(B\) contains exactly three points. Every pair of points from distinct groups belongs to one triple; pairs in one group belong to none. Two distinct triples share at most one point. These properties hold for every Latin square, whether or not it has nontrivial automorphisms. The graph has \(n^2+3n\) vertices. Equivalence refers to quantifier *rank*, not variable-restricted logic, counting quantifiers, or a three-sorted ternary-incidence signature.

## Proof

We use the standard \(r\)-round Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé equivalence criterion. The proof consists of a uniform game strategy and a reproducible exact finite boundary test.

**Rank three, all \(n\ge2\).** Each graph is bipartite and has no four-cycle, minimum degree at least two, and enough unused vertices that for every ordered adjacent pair the possible fresh third-vertex adjacency masks are exactly \(00,01,10\). An ordered nonadjacent pair has masks \(00,01,10\) and additionally \(11\) precisely when it has a common neighbour. Equality choices are always allowed. From every first vertex there exists a nonadjacent second vertex with a common neighbour and another without one: for a point, use a point from a different group and from its own group; for a block, use another block meeting it and a nonincident point. These statements hold at order two. Duplicator preserves the first-pair adjacency and, for nonadjacent pairs, common-neighbour existence; the enumerated masks finish the third move. Therefore all these graphs are \(\equiv_3\).

**Uniform four-round game for \(n,m\ge4\).** For the first three moves, Duplicator preserves more than ordinary adjacency: the full *incidence diagram* of the chosen vertices, consisting of their hidden point/block sorts, the three point-groups up to permutation, identities of coordinate points of selected blocks, and all incidences. It is enough to establish the following two finite extension facts, valid for every Latin square of order at least four.

First, every incidence-diagram extension from a tuple of length at most two to length three that is combinatorially consistent with a \(TD(3,n)\) occurs, independently of the Latin square. The only potentially tight case is extending a pair of distinct blocks. If two blocks intersect in one point, the numbers of *other* blocks meeting both, meeting just a specified one, and meeting neither are respectively \(n\), \(2n-4\), and \((n-2)(n-3)\). Of the \(n\) common-meeting blocks, exactly \(n-2\) pass through the pair's shared point and two meet the pair at two different points. If the original blocks are disjoint, the corresponding counts are \(6\), \(3(n-3)\), and \((n-3)^2+1\). All relevant counts are strictly positive when \(n\ge4\). For two distinct point vertices in the same group, the numbers of incident blocks meeting both, exactly one specified point and neither are \(0,n,n(n-2)\). For two points from different groups, the corresponding numbers are \(1,n-1,(n-1)^2\). Mixed point/block cases follow from each point's \(n\) blocks and each block's three coordinates, with at most two forbidden chosen vertices. Finally, selecting another point of any required group is possible because each group has at least four members. These cases exhaust all two-to-three incidence-diagram extensions; the one-to-two cases are immediate from the same counts.

Second, fix any ordered triple of selected vertices with its preserved hidden incidence diagram. Every equality/adjacency pattern for a fourth graph vertex realizable on one side is realizable on the other. A fresh point is adjacent only to selected blocks. A point belonging to three selected blocks exists exactly when they share a coordinate; a point belonging to precisely two exists exactly when that coordinate is not contained in the third; and a point belonging to exactly one exists because a selected block has a coordinate unshared by either of the other two selected blocks (and in a tuple with one selected point and two selected blocks, at least one of the two coordinates private to the other block remains). A point belonging to none exists since the union of the coordinates of at most three selected blocks has at most nine points, while \(|P|=3n\ge12\). A fresh block is adjacent only to selected points. For a given distinct point pair from different groups its common block is unique, and three points have a common block exactly when they are its three coordinates. A block containing exactly one specified point can be chosen from at least \(n-2\ge2\) available incident blocks. A block containing no specified point exists because at most three specified points exclude at most \(3n\) of the \(n^2\) blocks, leaving \(n^2-3n\ge4\). Any already-selected block is excluded by the preserved incidence diagram. Repeated points and equality moves are covered by that diagram. Therefore the fourth move can always match equality and adjacency, proving \(\equiv_4\) for all orders at least four.

**Sharp separation.** A rank-four sentence \(\exists x\exists y\exists z\forall w\) stating that \(x\) has exactly the two distinct neighbours \(y,z\) holds at order two and fails at every higher order. To distinguish order three from order four, use the exact finite four-round type recursion below. For an ordered tuple \(\bar a\), take its complete equality/adjacency atomic diagram as its zero-round type. Inductively, its \((j+1)\)-round type is the set of all \(j\)-round types of \((\bar a,b)\) as \(b\) runs through *every* vertex, including previous selections. Starting from four variables, successively compute the extension sets for tuples of lengths three, two, one and zero. This is the standard exhaustive backward induction for a four-round game: equal root types are equivalent, unequal root types supply a winning Spoiler strategy and hence a separating rank-four sentence. The standalone program `verify_rank4.py` computes these exact types and finds different root types for the order-three and order-four cyclic squares. Their difference therefore certifies a rank-four separation; it is not inferred from finite sampling. Together with the uniform infinite strategy, this proves exactly the three claimed classes.

## Verification

Run `sh reproduce.sh` with Python 3 and no nonstandard dependencies. `verify_rank4.py` performs exhaustive atomic-diagram recursion, including repeated selections, on cyclic orders two, three, four and five and on a nonisotopic Klein-four square of order four. It confirms a single rank-three type and exactly three rank-four classes on these representatives. `verify_geometry.py` independently represents incidence diagrams of tuples of lengths up to three, canonically permits permutations of the three point groups, and compares every possible fourth-move atomic pattern for the cyclic and Klein-four order-four designs. Both have exactly \(2,10,85\) structural types of lengths one, two and three with identical extension sets. It also checks positivity of the block-extension formulas. Recorded standard output is in `verification_output.txt`. These calculations certify the small separating witnesses and test the local case analysis; the unbounded conclusion is the preceding combinatorial proof.

## Relationship to prior work

Devillers and Hall's *Rank 3 Latin square designs* (2005 manuscript; 2006 journal publication) characterizes designs whose **automorphism group** has permutation rank three. That group-action notion is not first-order quantifier rank; its classification does not cover arbitrary low-symmetry Latin squares or the present uncoloured graph theories. Curtin and Daqqa (2009) study strongly regular **cell-adjacency graphs** constructed from Latin squares, not the bipartite point/block incidence graphs studied here. The standard finite-graph Ehrenfeucht game and definability framework appears in Verbitsky (2004), but does not specialize to this classification. The present theorem cannot be obtained merely by removing three unary sort predicates from a three-sorted finite-model statement: block vertices are additional elements and the adjacency signature has different quantifier semantics.

## Limitations

This classifies only order at least two, only the uncoloured undirected incidence graphs of \(TD(3,n)\), and only ranks at most four. The finding does not classify arbitrary transversal designs \(TD(k,n)\), higher ranks, or graph signatures with named point and block sorts. Historical priority cannot be proved by database search, and no independent mathematical audit has been performed.
