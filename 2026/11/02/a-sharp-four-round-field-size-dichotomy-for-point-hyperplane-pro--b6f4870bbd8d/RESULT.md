# A sharp four-round field-size dichotomy for point-hyperplane projective incidence

## Finding
Let \(d,e\geq 3\), and let \(q,r\geq 2\) be prime powers. Write \(\mathcal P(d,q)\) for the finite relational structure whose two distinguished sorts are the points and the hyperplanes of \(\operatorname{PG}(d,q)\), with equality and the point-hyperplane incidence predicate \(I\). No lower-dimensional flats, coordinates, or arithmetic operations are named. Then
\[
\mathcal P(d,q)\equiv_4\mathcal P(e,r)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\bigl(q=r=2\bigr)\ \text{or}\ \bigl(q\geq3\text{ and }r\geq3\bigr).
\]
Thus all such projective spaces have exactly **two** rank-four first-order theories, independently of their projective dimensions at least three. The equivalence holds in the stronger sense of a winning four-round Ehrenfeucht--Fraïssé strategy. All members agree through rank three, and rank four is sharp for the binary/nonbinary distinction.

The two classes have minimum representatives \(\operatorname{PG}(3,2)\), with 15 points and 15 hyperplanes, and \(\operatorname{PG}(3,3)\), with 40 points and 40 hyperplanes. Hence 80 total elements is the sharp universal representative bound **within this family**.

## Assumptions and scope
The underlying vector space is \(V=\mathbb F_q^{d+1}\) with \(d\ge3\). A point is a one-dimensional subspace \(\langle v\rangle\), a hyperplane is the kernel of a nonzero linear form \(f\), modulo scalar multiples, and \(I(\langle v\rangle,[f])\) means \(f(v)=0\). The relational language has unary predicates \(P,H\) naming the disjoint sorts and the binary incidence predicate \(I\) directed from \(P\) to \(H\); alternatively, use a genuine two-sorted language. All quantifiers are unrestricted with explicit sort conditions when required. Equality is ordinary equality on the disjoint union. The statement concerns ordinary first-order quantifier rank, not a restriction on total variables.

Denote the number of projective points in a vector space of dimension \(j\) by \([j]_q=(q^j-1)/(q-1)\) for \(j\ge1\). In the proof set \(m=d+1\ge4\).

## Proof
**One- and two-object extension counts.** A hyperplane contains \([m-1]_q\) points; its complement contains \(q^{m-1}\). If two distinct points \(x,y\) are fixed, their representing vectors are independent. The number of hyperplanes that contain both is \([m-2]_q\), that contain precisely the first or precisely the second is \(q^{m-2}\) in each case, and that contain neither is \((q-1)q^{m-2}\). These follow by prescribing respectively zero/nonzero values of a linear form on two independent vectors and counting up to nonzero scaling. The minimum among these numbers for \(m\ge4,q\ge2\) is \([2]_2=3\). Consequently all four prescribed incidence patterns remain realizable even after excluding one previously named hyperplane. By point-hyperplane duality, the analogous assertion holds for realizing points with prescribed incidences to two distinct hyperplanes, even after excluding one previously named point. Also, each side and each complement of one incidence neighborhood has enough elements to avoid any two already selected objects.

**The three-same-sort extension lemma.** Fix three pairwise distinct points. Their representing vectors have rank two (collinear projective points) or three (noncollinear). In the rank-three case, every one of the eight possible incidence patterns with a new hyperplane is realized: prescribe to the three independent vectors values zero on the desired incidence subset and nonzero on its complement. When all three values are zero, a nonzero linear form still exists because \(m\ge4\). In the rank-two case, a hyperplane either contains their whole projective line or intersects it in exactly one point. Each of the three singleton patterns and the all-three pattern is realizable. The all-zero pattern is realizable if and only if \(q\ge3\), by intersecting the line at a fourth point. Patterns containing exactly two of the three points are impossible. This is the complete extension table; it depends only on collinearity and whether \(q=2\). The dual argument applies to three distinct hyperplanes: either their defining forms are linearly dependent (members of a pencil) or independent, and the available fourth-point incidence patterns are exactly the dual patterns.

**Four-round strategy.** Suppose both spaces lie in the same binary/nonbinary field-size class. Duplicator responds to the first two selections so as to preserve equality, sorts and incidence. The preceding counts guarantee every required two-object atomic diagram is realized. On the third move Duplicator preserves the atomic diagram and, if all three selected objects are distinct and have the same sort, additionally preserves whether their defining vectors (or dual forms) have rank two or three. Indeed, through any two distinct points there are at least three points on their line and also points off the line; the corresponding assertion holds for distinct hyperplanes in their dual pencil. If the third move makes a mixed-sort triple, only the two-object incidence counts are needed. Repeated selections are mirrored exactly.

On the fourth move only the atomic diagram must be preserved. If the first three selections all have the same sort, the three-same-sort extension lemma gives exactly matching opposite-sort patterns; a further same-sort object only needs to be new or repeat one already named, and there are ample fresh objects. If the preceding triple has both sorts, a new object must satisfy at most two incidence constraints to previously named opposite-sort objects, and at most two inequalities to previously named same-sort objects. The one- and two-object counts guarantee an exact match. Thus after four selections the partial map preserves every relation and Duplicator wins. By the Ehrenfeucht--Fraïssé theorem the two structures agree on all sentences of rank at most four. Since the same argument works without the field-size class assumption for three moves, all spaces under consideration agree at rank three.

**Sharp separating sentence.** Let \(\Psi\) assert that three distinct points meet every hyperplane:
\[
\exists x\,\exists y\,\exists z\;\bigl(P(x)\land P(y)\land P(z)\land x\ne y\land x\ne z\land y\ne z\land
\forall h\,(H(h)\rightarrow(I(x,h)\lor I(y,h)\lor I(z,h)))\bigr).
\]
This has quantifier rank four. If \(q=2\), the three points of any projective line meet every hyperplane because every hyperplane intersects every line. If \(q\ge3\), any three noncollinear points can simultaneously be avoided by a hyperplane (choose nonzero evaluations on three independent representatives); three collinear points can also be avoided because their line has at least a fourth point, through which a hyperplane meeting the line only there can be taken. Thus \(\Psi\) is false for every \(q\ge3\), proving the classes distinct. Finally \(|P|=|H|=[d+1]_q\), which is strictly increasing in \(d\ge3\) and in prime power \(q\); the smallest representatives in the two classes have respectively 30 and 80 total elements. The latter bound is sharp.

## Verification
The standalone C++17 source `verify_pg_rank4.cpp` constructs all projective points and hyperplanes as normalized nonzero vectors, computes incidence by the finite-field dot product, and recursively calculates **complete** atomic-extension types for four rounds. It uses unrestricted quantifier moves, equality and the two named sorts; it neither assumes the asserted classification nor merely enumerates existential four-tuples. Compile with `g++ -O3 -std=c++17 verify_pg_rank4.cpp -o verify_pg_rank4` and run `./verify_pg_rank4`. The captured output is in `VERIFY_OUTPUT.txt`.

An additional independent script `check_extension_table.py` exhaustively checks the three-point extension table in projective dimension three over fields of sizes 2, 3, 4 and 5; its output is `EXTENSION_OUTPUT.txt`. The complete recursive-type computation compares \(\operatorname{PG}(3,2)\), \(\operatorname{PG}(4,2)\), and \(\operatorname{PG}(3,3)\). It finds identical complete rank-four roots for the first two and a distinct root for the third, with 48 and 50 distinct rank-one ordered-triple types respectively in their respective populations. This finite computation is a reproducibility check, not the basis for extrapolating to all prime powers and all dimensions; the preceding proof handles the infinite family.

## Relationship to prior work
Siemons and Smith [1] investigate a quite different invariant on the same class of finite projective spaces: incidence homology modules and their representation-theoretic decomposition over cross-characteristic coefficient fields. Their definitions include all projective subspace ranks, but their Theorems 1--3 concern homology and \(\mathrm{GL}(n,q)\) representation theory, not first-order point-hyperplane equivalence. Venema [2] develops two-sorted **modal** logic for projective planes, rather than unrestricted first-order rank-four equivalence of spaces with projective dimension at least three. Classical Ehrenfeucht--Fraïssé methods [3] supply the logical comparison framework but not the projective extension table or the exact binary/nonbinary cutoff. In particular, the present claim cannot be obtained from the projective-plane case alone: its objects are point-hyperplane incidences in projective dimension at least three, where pairs of hyperplanes share many points instead of a unique point.

## Limitations
This is a bounded-rank classification in a **reduct**: if lines, other flats, coordinates, a distinguished polarity, or algebraic operations are added to the signature, the conclusion need not remain true. The argument does not classify rank five, non-Desarguesian higher-dimensional incidence structures, or arbitrary symmetric designs. The logical result has been checked internally and computationally on three representative finite structures; independent mathematical audit and exhaustive historical-priority verification have not been performed. Some relevant historical literature is accessible only by metadata or abstract, so an independently discovered equivalent formulation remains possible.
