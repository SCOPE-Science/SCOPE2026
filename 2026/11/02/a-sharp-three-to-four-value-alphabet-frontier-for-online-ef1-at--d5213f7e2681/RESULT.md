# A sharp three-to-four value-alphabet frontier for online EF1 at the first surplus good

## Finding
Consider the first surplus-good horizon: exactly \(n+1\) indivisible goods are assigned irrevocably on arrival to \(n\ge2\) agents. Each additive single-good valuation belongs to a *common*, publicly known alphabet of positive real levels, but the arriving value vectors are revealed only upon each good's arrival. There is no forecast of future values or of their frequencies; the item count is known. The fairness requirement is exact envy-freeness up to one good (EF1) of the final allocation, not of every intermediate allocation.

**Theorem (value-alphabet frontier).** (1) With two levels \(\{a,b\}\), where \(0<a<b\), a deterministic online allocator achieves EF1 for every number \(n\ge2\) of agents. (2) With three levels \(\{a,b,c\}\), where \(0<a<b<c\), a deterministic allocator achieves EF1 for two agents and three goods. (3) With four levels \(\{a,b,c,d\}\), where \(0<a<b<c<d\), no deterministic allocator achieves EF1 against every adaptive adversary for two agents and three goods. Thus, at the first nontrivial two-agent horizon, four is the sharp minimum common alphabet size for a deterministic EF1 obstruction.

## Assumptions and scope
An instance consists of agents \(N=\{1,\ldots,n\}\), a known horizon \(m=n+1\), and goods \(g_1,\ldots,g_m\). Agent \(i\) has nonnegative additive valuation \(v_i(S)=\sum_{g\in S}v_i(g)\), with every single-good value selected from a fixed common finite positive alphabet. A deterministic online algorithm knows the alphabet, \(n\), and \(m\) but at time \(t\) sees only \((v_i(g_t))_{i\in N}\) and previous arrivals before irrevocably assigning \(g_t\). An adaptive adversary can select later vectors based on earlier allocations, subject only to the common alphabet. Terminal EF1 means that, for every ordered pair of distinct agents \(i,j\) with nonempty \(B_j\), some \(g\in B_j\) satisfies \(v_i(B_i)\ge v_i(B_j\setminus\{g\})\). No claims concern strategyproofness, Pareto optimality, predictions, or longer horizons.

## Proof
**Two-level case, arbitrary \(n\).** For the first \(n\) goods, give the current good to an as-yet-unassigned agent who values it at the higher level \(b\), if such an agent exists; otherwise give it to any as-yet-unassigned agent. Each agent now has exactly one good. If among these recipients some received her good at the lower value \(a\), let \(j\) be the first such recipient; give the final good to \(j\). If all initial recipients obtained value \(b\), give the final good to any agent.

Every agent except the final recipient holds one good; the final recipient holds two. Any singleton holder with own value \(b\) is EF1 toward the two-good holder, since the other bundle after deleting its more valuable good is worth at most \(b\) to her. If no low-value initial recipient existed, this proves EF1. Otherwise the final recipient is the first low-value recipient \(j\). Consider any *later* low-value singleton recipient \(i\). When \(g_j\) arrived, agent \(i\) was unassigned. Because \(g_j\) was given to a low-value recipient under the high-value-first policy, every then-unassigned agent (including \(i\)) valued \(g_j\) at \(a\). Consequently, \(v_i(\{g_j\})=a\), so removing the final good from \(j\)'s bundle leaves value exactly \(a=v_i(B_i)\). There is no earlier low-value singleton. The two-good holder automatically satisfies EF1 toward every singleton, since deleting that single good leaves the empty bundle. This completes the proof for all \(n\ge2\).

**Three-level case, two agents.** Write the levels as \(a<b<c\). At the first arrival \(g_1\): if some agent values it \(c\), assign it to such an agent \(i\) and give both later goods to the other agent \(j\). The singleton holder \(i\) values her own good \(c\), at least either later good, establishing EF1. Otherwise, if some agent \(j\) values \(g_1\) at \(a\), give \(g_1\) to the other agent \(i\), then \(g_2\) to \(j\) and \(g_3\) to \(i\). Agent \(j\) receives a singleton worth at least \(a\) and sees \(g_1\) in \(i\)'s bundle at exactly \(a\), so EF1 holds. In the remaining case both agents value \(g_1\) at \(b\). Give it to agent \(i\), and let \(j\) be the other agent. If \(j\) values \(g_2\) at \(a\), give \(g_2\) to \(i\) and \(g_3\) to \(j\): the singleton of \(j\) is worth at least \(a\), while she values the two items of \(i\) at \(b,a\). Otherwise give \(g_2\) to \(j\) and \(g_3\) to \(i\): \(j\)'s singleton is worth at least \(b\), while she values \(g_1\) at \(b\). In all cases the two-good recipient automatically satisfies EF1 toward the singleton recipient, and the singleton recipient has the stated removable-good guarantee.

**Four-level impossibility, two agents.** Let \(0<a<b<c<d\). The adversary makes both agents value \(g_1\) at \(c\); name its recipient \(i\), and the other agent \(j\). For \(g_2\), the adversary reveals values \(v_i(g_2)=d\) and \(v_j(g_2)=b\). If the algorithm gives \(g_2\) to \(i\), the adversary reveals \(v_i(g_3)=v_j(g_3)=a\). Assigning \(g_3\) to \(i\) leaves \(j\) with nothing and at least two positive goods in \(i\)'s bundle; assigning it to \(j\) leaves her singleton worth \(a\), while she values \(i\)'s two goods at \(c\) and \(b\), both strictly more than \(a\). Either way EF1 fails.

If the algorithm instead gives \(g_2\) to \(j\), reveal \(v_i(g_3)=d\) and \(v_j(g_3)=c\). Giving \(g_3\) to \(i\) leaves \(j\) with singleton value \(b\) but valuing both goods of \(i\) at \(c\), while giving it to \(j\) leaves \(i\) with singleton value \(c\) but valuing both goods of \(j\) at \(d\). Both violate EF1. The adversary adapts only after the second assignment; ties and all deterministic choices are covered. This proves the four-level impossibility, independent of the numerical ratios between the levels. With at most two goods, giving distinct goods to the two agents is always EF1, so three goods is the first horizon where a negative example is possible.

## Verification
A standalone standard-library exact-integer checker `verify.py` enumerates every binary common-alphabet arrival profile for \(n\in\{2,3,4\}\) and \(m=n+1\): exactly \(64\), \(4096\), and \(1{,}048{,}576\) complete profiles. It separately checks all \(729\) ternary two-agent three-good profiles. For the four-level adversary it checks both first recipients, both second recipients, and both possible last assignments against the resulting value vectors. All tests passed. The exhaustive finite checks validate the stated algorithms on these finite domains; the unrestricted quantifiers follow from the foregoing proofs, not from extrapolation of enumeration.

Run `python3 verify.py` from the directory containing this report and the embedded verifier.

## Relationship to prior work
Neoh, Peters and Teh (arXiv:2505.24503; first public 2025-05-30) establish broad online impossibility without future information and favorable results with predictions, but their general impossibility does not imply the exact common-alphabet short-horizon guarantee and threshold here. Neoh and Teh (arXiv:2610.34625; first public 2026-10-25), whose introduction, formal model, and Sections 3–4 were examined, show powerful impossibilities for general value ranges even with frequency predictions and \(n+1\) goods; their four-scale construction for five or more agents and their binary-positive EFX obstruction concern different quantifiers and fairness notions. Their EFX impossibility must not be confused with EF1. The sharp all-positive common-alphabet EF1 frontier here isolates what extra discrete-value complexity is necessary in a particularly small market. Neoh and Teh (arXiv:2609.05310) also prove broader online PROP-fairness impossibilities without this three-good alphabet analysis. A complete priority search, including inaccessible full texts, is not claimed.

## Limitations
The result assumes known fixed horizon, strict positive common value levels, additive utility, deterministic policies, and terminal EF1. It does not establish any nontrivial all-horizon guarantee or EFX guarantee. The precise three-versus-four alphabet threshold is only asserted for two agents and three goods, whereas the two-level positive result extends to all \(n\) at the first surplus-good horizon. An earlier unindexed or inaccessible equivalent special-case argument remains a possible priority risk; no independent scientific validation has been performed.
