# Scientific review

## Correctness (PASS)

The proof splits the complete monic finite-field cubic factorization census into five disjoint classes and derives two independent conditional lifting laws. In the double-root class the quadratic discriminant is uniform, and the repeated-discriminant branch has a separate uniform next-digit test. In the triple-root class the successive divisibility tests isolate an unconstrained monic cubic modulo \(p\). The resulting probabilities sum to one and agree with the exact exhaustive \(p=3\) enumeration of all \(531441\) coefficient triples. The argument applies to each odd prime, not by extrapolation. Risk: finite checks do not independently certify the general proof; the proof remains subject to independent review.

## Originality (PASS, best knowledge)

The closest general coverage is the 2017/2019 root-counting algorithm of Cheng–Gao–Rojas–Wan, especially its fourth-power pointwise branching analysis (Theorem 4.1), and Nir's 2026 weighted lifting-tree method. Both supply mechanisms to compute a given polynomial's roots, but their inspected text does not state or evaluate the full probability generating function over uniform monic cubic coefficients. Jain's finite-ring theorem determines only the expectation. Caruso's distribution concerns genuine \(p\)-adic roots. The known \(p^3\) cubic law does not imply the fourth-level branch probabilities. The principal new item is the evaluated *complete coefficient-averaged fourth-level law*, not the existence of an algorithm or the general tree-width bound. Searches against indexed mathematical records and recoverable research claims revealed no implication covering the full law. Risk: an equivalent historic law may exist in other terminology, or a general unpublished table may specialize to it; novelty is best-knowledge, not independently certified.

## Value (PASS)

Counting temporary modular roots is mathematically distinct from counting \(p\)-adic roots. The fourth power is the first level at which a cubic's double and triple branches simultaneously yield new large root clusters: \(p^2+1\), \(2p^2\), and \(3p^2\). The exact law, not merely its first moment, classifies every coefficient stratum and the rare extreme event for every odd prime; the equality-class census is a natural, unknown-by-prior-statements extremal invariant. This is a finite-level closed distribution, not a promised all-exponent theorem.
