# Exact three-square obstructions and sharp dyadic fluctuations for superfactorials

## Finding

Write \(S(n)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}j!\), with \(S(0)=1\). Let \(E(X)\) count indices \(0\le n<X\) for which \(S(n)\) is **not** a sum of three integer squares. For any \(m\ge0\), write its binary expansion \(m=\sum_{i\ge0}b_i2^i\), extending the digits by zeros, and set
\[
\begin{aligned}
 A(m)&\equiv\sum_{i\ge2}b_i+\sum_{i\ge0}b_ib_{i+1}\pmod2,\\
 B(m)&\equiv\sum_{i\ge1}b_ib_{i+1}+\sum_{i\ge0}b_ib_{i+2}\pmod2.
\end{aligned}
\]
**Theorem.** For every integer \(n\ge0\), \(S(n)\) fails to be a sum of three integer squares if and only if one of these disjoint conditions holds:

1. \(n\equiv5\pmod8\);
2. \(n=2m\), \(s_2(m)\) is even, and \(A(m)=B(m)=1\), where \(s_2\) is the binary digit sum.

The counting function satisfies
\[
 E(X)=\frac{3X}{16}+O(\sqrt X)\qquad(X\to\infty).
\]
The error exponent is sharp. More precisely, for every integer \(j\ge0\),
\[
 \boxed{E(2^{8j+5})=3\cdot2^{8j+1}+2^{4j}.}
\]
In particular, precisely \(13/16\) of the indices, in natural density, give superfactorials representable by three squares. The subsequence equality gives a positive discrepancy of order \(\sqrt X\) for infinitely many \(X\).

## Assumptions and scope

All products and exponents refer to ordinary nonnegative integers. The three squares may include zeros, as in Legendre's three-square theorem. The odd part of a positive integer \(t\) is \(u(t)=t/2^{\nu_2(t)}\), reduced modulo \(8\) when needed. Binary digit sums are finite. The asymptotic is for real \(X\ge1\), with \(E(X)\) defined using integer indices in \([0,X)\). The coefficient \(3/16\) and dyadic identity are exact assertions, not fitted numerical estimates.

## Proof

**1. Reduction to the odd part.** Legendre's three-square theorem says that a positive integer is not a sum of three squares exactly when its \(2\)-adic valuation is even and its odd part is \(7\pmod8\). The identity
\[
 S(n)=\prod_{t=1}^n t^{n+1-t}
\]
implies that, modulo \(8\), only factors with odd exponent matter, since every odd unit squares to \(1\pmod8\). Also
\[
 \nu_2(S(n))=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\bigl(t-s_2(t)\bigr).
\]

For \(n=2m+1\), terms with odd exponent have odd \(t\). Thus the odd part of \(S(2m+1)\) is
\[
 O(m+1)=\prod_{t=1}^{m+1}(2t-1)\pmod8.
\]
Its value depends only on \(m+1\pmod4\), taking values \(1,1,3,7\) in residue order \(0,1,2,3\). Pairing \(s_2(2t)+s_2(2t+1)=2s_2(t)+1\) shows \(\nu_2(S(2m+1))\) is even. Hence the odd indices obstructed are precisely \(n\equiv5\pmod8\).

For \(n=2m\), the terms with odd exponent instead have even \(t\); therefore the odd part is
\[
 f(m)=\prod_{t=1}^{m}u(t)\pmod8.
\]
The same binary digit-sum pairing shows
\[
 \nu_2(S(2m))\equiv s_2(m)\pmod2.
\]
Thus the even-index obstruction amounts to \(s_2(m)\) even and \(f(m)=7\pmod8\).

**2. An explicit local binary expression.** Let \(O(t)=\prod_{i=1}^t(2i-1)\pmod8\). Splitting factorials into their even and odd factors gives, for every \(t\ge0\),
\[
 f(2t)=f(t)O(t),\qquad f(2t+1)=f(t)O(t)(2t+1)\pmod8.
\]
Here \(O(t)\) depends on \(t\pmod4\) as above. The group of odd units modulo \(8\) is generated by \(3\) and \(5\), both of order two. Applying the two recurrences successively to the binary digits of \(m\) gives
\[
 f(m)\equiv3^{A(m)}5^{B(m)}\pmod8.
\]
For clarity, the contribution from the least significant digit, with \(m=2t+b_0\), is \(b_2+b_0b_1\) to the exponent of \(3\) and \(b_1b_2+b_0b_2\) to the exponent of \(5\). Iterating the recurrence shifts all binary positions and gives exactly the displayed formulas for \(A\) and \(B\). As \(3\cdot5\equiv7\pmod8\), the equality \(f(m)\equiv7\) holds precisely when \(A(m)=B(m)=1\). This proves the individual-index criterion, including \(m=0\).

**3. Finite transfer matrix and spectral bound.** A state is a triple \((r,p,u)\) with \(r\in\{0,1,2,3\}\), \(p\in\{0,1\}\), \(u\in\{1,3,5,7\}\). Start at \((0,0,1)\). After reading a binary digit \(d\in\{0,1\}\), most significant first, update it by
\[
 (r,p,u)\longmapsto\left((2r+d)\bmod4,\ p\mathbin{\mathrm{xor}}d,\ uO(r)(2r+1)^d\bmod8\right).
\]
The final state records \((m\bmod4,s_2(m)\bmod2,f(m)\bmod8)\). In the \(32\)-by-\(32\) nonnegative integer matrix \(M\), entry \(M_{st}\) counts digits that move state \(s\) to \(t\). Let \(e\) be the row concentrated on \((0,0,1)\) and \(a\) the column indicating the four accepting states with \(p=0,u=7\). The number of acceptable \(m<2^k\) is exactly
\[
 C_k=eM^ka.
\]

For reproducibility, the supplied standalone verifier generates all \(32\) states and all matrix entries from the update rule and checks the following **finite exact certificates**. Every row and every column sums to \(2\); the directed graph is strongly connected and has a self-loop; and \(P(M)=0\), with
\[
\begin{aligned}
P(z)={}&z^3(z-2)(z^2-2)(z^2+2)(z^2-2z+2)\\
 &\cdot(z^4-2z^2+4)(z^4-2z^3+2z^2-4z+4).
\end{aligned}
\]
These assertions require just \(32\)-state breadth-first searches and ordinary integer matrix multiplication, not heuristic numerical eigenvalue estimation. All roots of \(P\), except \(2\) and \(0\), have modulus exactly \(\sqrt2\). Indeed the first quartic splits over \(\mathbb R\) into quadratics \(z^2\pm\sqrt6z+2\), and the second into \(z^2+(-1\pm\sqrt3)z+2\); all these quadratics have negative discriminant and constant term \(2\). The nonzero roots are simple. Strong connectivity and the self-loop make \(M\) primitive, so \(2\) is its simple Perron eigenvalue; constant row and column sums show the Perron projection is \(\mathbf1\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}/32\). Consequently, uniformly over the \(32\) possible initial states,
\[
 e_sM^ka=\frac{2^k}{8}+O(2^{k/2}).
\]
Decomposing every initial interval of binary integers into aligned dyadic cylinders (at most one per size) and summing the geometric error gives
\[
 \#\{0\le m<Y:s_2(m)\text{ even},\ f(m)=7\}=\frac Y8+O(\sqrt Y).
\]
Combining this with the exact odd-index progression \(n\equiv5\pmod8\) proves \(E(X)=3X/16+O(\sqrt X)\).

**4. Exact infinite dyadic subsequence and optimality.** The verifier checks, with unbounded integer arithmetic, all \(34\) identities
\[
 eM^{8j+4}a=2\cdot256^j+16^j\qquad(0\le j<34).
\]
This **finite calculation proves the identity for all** \(j\ge0\), not by extrapolation: put \(T=M^8\). The difference between the left side and right side is an output sequence of the block diagonal \(34\)-dimensional operator \(\operatorname{diag}(T,256,16)\). Cayley--Hamilton supplies a monic recurrence of order \(34\). Since its first \(34\) terms vanish exactly, every term vanishes. Thus \(C_{8j+4}=2^{8j+1}+2^{4j}\).

Among the indices \(0\le n<2^{8j+5}\), exactly one eighth are odd indices congruent to \(5\pmod8\), and the even indices correspond to the \(m\) counted by \(C_{8j+4}\). Therefore
\[
 E(2^{8j+5})=2^{8j+2}+2^{8j+1}+2^{4j}
 =3\cdot2^{8j+1}+2^{4j},
\]
proving the exact formula and the impossibility of an \(o(\sqrt X)\) general error bound.

## Verification

Run `python3 verify.py` with Python 3.10 or later. It independently accumulates the \(2\)-adic valuation and odd part of successive factorials, then of successive superfactorials, and compares the three-square test with the digit predicate for indices \(0\le n\le4096\). It verifies the binary odd-factor identity for \(0\le m<16384\), constructs the complete transfer matrix, checks its row/column sums and graph connectivity, evaluates \(P(M)\) exactly, and checks the \(34\) Cayley--Hamilton initial identities with arbitrary-precision integers. These finite statements, explicitly joined with the mathematical deductions in the proof, establish the infinite claims. The code neither enumerates infinitely many values nor treats numerical sampling as proof.

## Relationship to prior work

Burns' 2021 paper, *Factorials and Legendre's three-square theorem*, gives a binary criterion for whether an individual factorial is a sum of three squares. Burns' 2022 sequel gives exact dyadic counts and a \(1/8\)-density with a square-root error for factorials; its joint-state discussion is strong enough, combined with the even-superfactorial reduction above, to imply the **density and error rate** of the present theorem. These are not claimed as independently new consequences. Neither inspected paper states the superfactorial-specific binary obstruction or the exact dyadic subsequence with its positive square-root fluctuation. The present proof supplies those statements and an independent explicit \(32\)-state certificate.

Aliyev's 2022 article in *Crux Mathematicorum* derives valuations of superfactorials and related Vandermonde determinants, but does not classify three-square representability or compute the exact dyadic obstruction count. OEIS A000178 records the superfactorial values without the stated three-square theorem. A complete bibliographic uniqueness claim is not asserted.

## Limitations

No representation of a representable superfactorial as an explicit triple of squares is constructed. No statement is made about sums of two squares, four-square representation counts, or more general products of factorials. The exact counting identity applies to the displayed dyadic subsequence; the asymptotic applies to all \(X\). Absolute bibliographic priority is unverified.
