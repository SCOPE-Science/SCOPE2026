# Scientific review

**Correctness — PASS.** The arbitrary competing distinct-prime set is reduced to its largest prime and a residual product \(R<4M^2\). A fixed threshold excludes \(R>M\), unique factorization identifies the equality case, and the remaining \(R<M\) cases are in exact bijection with the finite even-shift prime obstructions. The converse constructs an explicit strictly lower-product competitor. The sieve step uses the standard unconditional upper bound for primes at a fixed nonzero difference. Finite exact computations on 867 instances provide additional checks but not proof.

**Originality — PASS, subject to priority risk.** Campbell's original 2026 Lemma 2 supplies a sufficient local prime-gap isolation condition and explicitly poses the extension-prime growth question, but does not give an exact finite criterion for failure of product minimality. The new equivalence replaces a sufficient condition by a complete eventual obstruction classification. The density-one corollary is not by itself a strong novelty claim, because the sufficient isolation construction and classical prime-pair sieve suggest it already. Searches of current indexed research records and related literature found no equivalent finite-shift theorem. Hidden or unindexed coverage remains possible.

**Mathematical value — PASS.** The explicit finite obstruction set is a reusable structural classification for every fixed minimum-product distinct-prime partition, answering precisely when extending by a sufficiently large prime fails. It also gives an effective decision rule and a quantitative asymptotic for the growing sequence of successful primes asked about in the source paper.

**Closest prior literature and limitations.** Campbell, arXiv:2610.19534v1 (Lemma 2 and concluding question); Selberg's classical fixed-shift pair sieve; Deléglise–Nicolas's bounded-sum maximum product (a different problem). The threshold is conservative. The exceptional-prime upper bound is not an asymptotic equality; stronger lower bounds concern open prime-tuple questions. Independent priority assessment remains appropriate.
