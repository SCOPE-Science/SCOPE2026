# Five minimum-zero-forcing orbits and exchange–sliding rigidity of circular ladders

## Finding

For every integer \(n\ge7\), let \(Y_n=C_n\square K_2\) be the circular ladder (prism), with vertices \((i,\varepsilon)\), where \(i\in\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z\) and \(\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}\). The ordinary zero-forcing rule permits **only a blue vertex** with exactly one white neighbor to turn that neighbor blue. Then the zero-forcing number is \(Z(Y_n)=4\). The \(13n\) minimum zero-forcing sets form exactly five orbits under dihedral rotations/reflections and interchange of the two layers. Representatives, with all first coordinates interpreted modulo \(n\), are

\[
\begin{aligned}
 A&=\{(0,0),(1,0),(2,0),(3,0)\},\\
 B&=\{(0,0),(1,0),(2,0),(0,1)\},\\
 C&=\{(0,0),(1,0),(3,0),(1,1)\},\\
 D&=\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)\},\\
 E&=\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(-1,1)\}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Their respective orbit sizes are \(2n,4n,4n,n,2n\). In the **token-exchange** graph, vertices are minimum zero-forcing sets and two are adjacent exactly when one selected vertex can be replaced by any unselected vertex. This graph is connected, has \(13n\) vertices and \(48n\) edges, and its vertex-degree distribution is: \(4n\) vertices of degree \(5\), \(8n\) of degree \(8\), and \(n\) of degree \(12\). In the **token-sliding** graph, the exchange must additionally move the token along a graph edge; this graph is edgeless, consisting of \(13n\) isolated vertices.

## Assumptions and scope

The graph is finite, simple, undirected, with \(n\ge7\). Vertices in one layer are cyclically adjacent; the two vertices in each column are also adjacent. A zero-forcing process starts with selected blue vertices; a blue vertex with precisely one white neighbor forces it blue. The order of legal forces is immaterial to whether a set eventually colors every vertex: simultaneously applying all currently legal forces gives the same closure. All counts concern labeled vertices. The orbit group is generated by cyclic translation of columns, reflection of column indices, and switching layers. Reconfiguration graphs are simple, with no loops. The result does not address shorter prisms or positive-semidefinite/skew variants of zero forcing.

## Proof

**1. A local necessity lemma and the minimum size.** Every vertex has degree three, and there are no triangles. An initial force from a four-vertex set requires its forcing vertex to have two blue neighbors. Thus every four-vertex zero-forcing set contains an induced three-vertex path. Up to prism symmetries, its three vertices are either the horizontal path
\(H=\{(0,0),(1,0),(2,0)\}\) or the horizontal–vertical path
\(V=\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\}\).

For \(H\), the triple alone has the unique legal initial force \((1,0)\to(1,1)\). Accounting for all additional or blocked initial forces when the fourth vertex is inserted, and then checking the neighbors of the enlarged blue set, yields successful propagation **if and only if** the fourth vertex belongs to
\[
\{(3,0),(-1,0),(0,1),(2,1)\}.
\]
For \(V\), the triple alone has the unique legal initial force \((0,0)\to(-1,0)\). The analogous examination, including changes to the first force caused by the fourth blue vertex, shows that the successful fourth vertices are exactly
\[
\{(2,0),(-2,0),(1,1),(-1,1)\}.
\]
To see why these short lists are exhaustive, check the three neighbors of each vertex in the initially blue three-path, and again after the first available forces. In either three-path type, each unlisted fourth vertex produces a final closure of only four or five blue vertices: some nearby fourth vertices suppress the central force, and every other unsuccessful choice allows the central force but no continuation. Distant fourth vertices have no effect on the local force and cannot force themselves. All possibly influential fourth-vertex positions are within two prism edges of the three-path; examining these bounded positions gives the two four-element success lists. In the eight listed cases, within at most two parallel forcing rounds the configuration contains two consecutive fully blue columns. The two layer endpoints at either end of these columns then force outward together, repeatedly, until the entire cycle is blue. This proves sufficiency as well as necessity.

With at most three initial blue vertices, an initial force again requires one of \(H\) or \(V\); after its sole first force no further force is possible. Thus \(Z(Y_n)\ge4\), and the preceding constructions establish equality. All successful size-four sets are obtained by transforming the eight local cases under graph symmetries; reducing these cases gives precisely the five representatives above. No arbitrary global configuration has been omitted, since an initial force is necessary.

**2. Distinct orbits and exact counts.** Apply \((i,\varepsilon)\mapsto(\pm i+t,\varepsilon\oplus f)\) for each \(t\in\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z\) and \(f\in\{0,1\}\). Each representative is supported on at most four consecutive columns. For \(n\ge7\), the five resulting orbit families are pairwise disjoint: their layer occupancies, counts of fully selected columns, and cyclic inter-column gaps distinguish the five patterns. Within each family the stabilizers have respective orders \(2,1,1,4,2\) in the order-\(4n\) symmetry group. Orbit–stabilizer yields \(2n,4n,4n,n,2n\), totaling \(13n\).

**3. The reconfiguration adjacency calculation.** A one-token exchange between size-four sets is equivalent to sharing exactly three vertices. Comparing each of the five patterns with all reflected, shifted, and layer-swapped instances of the five patterns yields the following **neighbor counts per source representative**; columns and rows are ordered \(A,B,C,D,E\):

\[
\begin{array}{c|ccccc|c}
 &A&B&C&D&E&\text{degree}\\\hline
 A&2&4&2&0&0&8\\
 B&2&1&2&1&2&8\\
 C&1&2&0&1&1&5\\
 D&0&4&4&0&4&12\\
 E&0&4&2&2&0&8
\end{array}
\]

This is an exhaustive **finite local comparison**, not a numerical extrapolation. Each motif occupies an interval of at most four columns. To share three labeled vertices with a fixed motif, a transformed motif must place at least three of its vertices in that fixed interval; its remaining column can be at most three columns farther away. Thus all candidates reduce to bounded relative shifts, reflections, and layer switches. For \(n\ge11\), the involved relative column labels have no extra collisions modulo \(n\); the finitely many wraparound cases \(7\le n\le10\) give the same matrix by direct representative comparisons. The standalone checker independently reproduces the matrix for six consecutive cycle lengths, including values on both sides of the wraparound cutoff. The table can also be reproduced symbolically by testing each of the five four-vertex templates for three-element intersections with their transforms.

The orbit-weighted sum of degrees is \((2n)8+(4n)8+(4n)5+n12+(2n)8=96n\). Hence the number of token-exchange edges is \(48n\), with the claimed degree distribution.

For connectivity, every type \(B\) or \(C\) vertex has a type \(A\) neighbor, and every type \(D\) or \(E\) vertex has a type \(B\) neighbor, as shown by the adjacency table and symmetry. The same-layer type \(A\) sets of four consecutive vertices join into a cycle under one-token exchange. The two layers connect via the explicit sequence
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\{(0,0),(1,0),(2,0),(3,0)\}\\
\to{}&\{(0,0),(1,0),(2,0),(0,1)\}\\
\to{}&\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)\}\\
\to{}&\{(1,0),(0,1),(1,1),(3,1)\}\\
\to{}&\{(0,1),(1,1),(2,1),(3,1)\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
All intermediate sets are among the five orbit families. Thus the exchange graph is connected.

Finally, a token slide must replace a selected vertex by an adjacent unselected vertex of the original prism and retain membership in one of the five orbit families. Inspecting the three possible neighbors for each of the four vertices of each representative shows that **none** of these at most twelve candidate slides per representative is a minimum forcing set. Symmetry extends this finite calculation to every minimum set, and the same relative-shift and wraparound analysis above applies to every \(n\ge7\). The token-sliding graph therefore has no edges.

## Verification

The included standalone `verify.py` independently computes the zero-forcing closure from the original neighbor rule, exhaustively enumerates all three-vertex and four-vertex subsets for \(7\le n\le12\), tests the five symmetry orbits, counts and classifies exchange neighbors, verifies exchange connectivity and absence of sliding edges, and checks the two exact local necessity lists through \(n=24\). The run terminated with `VERIFY_OK`. For \(n\ge11\), the finite local-case argument in the proof, not the bounded experiment, supplies the extension to every larger \(n\). Run `python3 verify.py` using a standard Python 3 interpreter.

## Relationship to prior work

The scalar value \(Z(C_n\square K_2)=4\) is known; see the circular-ladder connected-forcing calculation in Premodkumar, Dominic and Chacko (2020), and earlier results on generalized Petersen prisms in Alameda et al. (2017). Geneson, Haas and Hogben (2020) give the general minimum-zero-forcing-set reconfiguration framework. Their inspected graph families and propositions do not enumerate the five prism orbits or establish both reconfiguration graphs. The result concerns **ordinary** zero forcing, not the skew or positive-semidefinite variations considered in other papers. The novelty assessment is a literature comparison, not a claim that unindexed equivalent work cannot exist.

## Limitations

The theorem is proved only for \(n\ge7\); smaller prism orders may have different local coincidences. It supplies no classification of nonminimum zero-forcing sets. The proof's reconfiguration counts depend on elementary finite neighborhood comparisons, documented as an explicit matrix and reproducible code. A bounded computational check alone would not establish the infinite claim; the necessary-path argument and bounded relative-shift reasoning do so. Publication priority remains subject to earlier unindexed equivalent enumerations or an unexamined section of relevant graph literature. Independent audit is not asserted.
