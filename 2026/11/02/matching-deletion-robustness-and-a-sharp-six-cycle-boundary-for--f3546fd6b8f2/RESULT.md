# Matching-deletion robustness and a sharp six-cycle boundary for three-colouring reconfiguration

## Finding
Let \(3\le m\le n\) and \(0\le t\le m\) be integers. From the complete bipartite graph \(K_{m,n}\), delete any matching of size \(t\), and let \(\mathcal R_3(G)\) be the graph of all proper colourings with the three *named* colours \(\{0,1,2\}\), adjacent exactly when they differ at one vertex. Except when \((m,n,t)=(3,3,3)\), this reconfiguration graph consists of a cyclic necklace of six hypercubes (three each of dimensions \(m\) and \(n\)) augmented, for each deleted matching edge, by six distinct degree-two vertices, each closing a square at a necklace junction.

Writing \(N=m+n\), its complete order, edge count and metric are
\[
|V(\mathcal R_3(G))|=3(2^m+2^n)-6+6t,\quad
|E(\mathcal R_3(G))|=3(m2^{m-1}+n2^{n-1})+12t,\quad
\operatorname{rad}(\mathcal R_3(G))=\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal R_3(G))=\lfloor 3N/2\rfloor.
\]
Every vertex has eccentricity \(\lfloor3N/2\rfloor\). It is bipartite exactly when \(N\) is even; for odd \(N\) it has chromatic number three and odd girth \(3N\). The six-cube subgraph is isometrically embedded.

At the unique excluded parameter \((m,n,t)=(3,3,3)\), the underlying graph is the six-cycle. Its recolouring graph has a 60-vertex connected component (the six-cube necklace with all 18 square vertices, of radius and diameter nine) and six additional isolated proper three-colourings. Thus deleting disjoint edges preserves the entire metric up to a sharp small-order exception.

## Assumptions and scope
The underlying graph is simple; the removed edges are pairwise vertex-disjoint and join opposite bipartition classes. Both parts have at least three vertices. Colour labels are distinguished, and a move recolours one vertex with no intervening improper colouring. The theorem gives exact finite combinatorial graphs for all integer parameters in the specified range. It makes no claim for arbitrary, nonmatching edge deletions, palettes of more than three colours, or mixing times.

A *necklace of six cubes* means a union of six induced hypercubes, in circular order, whose consecutive cubes share exactly one vertex, nonconsecutive cubes are disjoint, and the two shared vertices of a cube are its antipodal corners. A *square vertex* has exactly two neighbours, one step inside each of the two cubes incident at a specified junction; the junction and these three vertices form a four-cycle. There are six such square vertices for each missing edge. Different square vertices are never adjacent.

## Proof
**1. Restriction on shared colours.** Write the parts as \(A=\{a_1,\ldots,a_m\}\) and \(B=\{b_1,\ldots,b_n\}\), with precisely \(a_ib_i\), \(1\le i\le t\), missing. In a proper colouring, a colour appearing on both sides can appear only at \(a_i,b_i\) for one deleted pair: any other cross pair is an edge. There cannot be two different such shared colours, say at missing indices \(i\ne j\), unless \((m,n,t)=(3,3,3)\). Indeed, every other left and right vertex must use the third colour. Since each side has at least three vertices, a remaining vertex exists on both sides; each remaining left/right pair is adjacent unless it is the same deleted matched pair. Consequently each side must contain exactly one remaining vertex and those two must be the third deleted matched pair. In that exceptional graph there are exactly \(3!=6\) colourings in which all three colours are shared, one on each missing pair. Every vertex in such a colouring sees the other two colours, so each of these six states is isolated.

**2. The six cubes.** Colourings without a shared colour have disjoint colour palettes on the two sides. As only three colours are allowed, at least one part is monochromatic. For every choice of colour for the monochromatic left part, the right part may be coloured freely with the other two colours, giving an \(n\)-cube; similarly the three right-monochromatic choices give three \(m\)-cubes. The six pure two-colour states, with one colour per part, belong to exactly two cubes each. Those intersections form a six-cycle of blocks, and each cube's two pure states are opposite corners. Every one-vertex recolouring that stays within this class is a cube edge. There are no other base edges.

**3. Precisely six square vertices per deletion.** If one colour \(\alpha\) is shared, it occurs exactly on \(a_i,b_i\), for a missing index \(i\). All remaining vertices of \(A\) must be monochromatic in some colour \(\beta\) and all remaining vertices of \(B\) monochromatic in the third colour \(\gamma\): otherwise one of the two sides would meet a forbidden repeated cross-colour. The ordered choices of \((\beta,\gamma)\) number six for each \(i\). Recolouring \(a_i\) from \(\alpha\) to \(\beta\), or \(b_i\) from \(\alpha\) to \(\gamma\), produces the two and only two neighbours of this state. Both neighbours are themselves adjacent to the pure junction \((\beta^m,\gamma^n)\), by recolouring their single exceptional vertex. They lie inside different cubes incident to that junction. Thus every shared-colour state closes exactly the described square; the four-cycle vertices and edges are all distinct across \(i\) and the six ordered choices. All other recolourings are improper because a third-colour occurrence would conflict with another vertex across a present edge. In particular, no two square vertices are adjacent.

**4. Isometry, counts and the circle coordinate.** A path through a degree-two square vertex runs through its two cube neighbours. Replace that two-edge subpath by the two-edge path through its junction. Hence the six-cube base is isometric in the full recolouring graph. Summing the sizes of the six cubes and subtracting the six doubly counted corners gives \(3(2^m+2^n)-6\) base vertices; adding \(6t\) gives the stated order. The base edge count is \(3(m2^{m-1}+n2^{n-1})\), and each new square vertex contributes two further edges, giving the stated total.

Let \(C=3(m+n)\). Around the six cubes assign their successive junctions the circle coordinates \(0,d_1,d_1+d_2,\ldots\) modulo \(C\), where the six dimensions \(d_i\) alternate between \(m\) and \(n\). A vertex inside the cube starting at coordinate \(s\) has coordinate \(s+h\) modulo \(C\), where \(h\) is its Hamming distance from that cube's starting junction. This assignment agrees at both shared corners, and every base edge changes the coordinate by one unit in either direction. Every circle coordinate is attained by a base vertex. For any junction \(p\) and any base vertex \(x\), following the appropriate clockwise or counterclockwise sequence of cube-geodesics achieves the circle distance \(d_{C}(p,x)\). Thus \(d(p,x)=d_C(p,x)\). For arbitrary base vertices in different cubes the same two-arc construction yields \(d(x,y)\le \lfloor C/2\rfloor\); if they lie in a common cube their Hamming distance is at most \(\max(m,n)\le\lfloor C/2\rfloor\). Since every base vertex has another vertex at opposite circle distance \(D=\lfloor C/2\rfloor\), the coordinate's one-Lipschitz property proves all base eccentricities equal \(D\).

**5. Metric with squares.** A square vertex \(z\) belongs to one junction \(p\), is distance two from \(p\), and has two neighbours, one step into each cube incident to \(p\). For a base vertex \(x\) in either incident cube, \(d(z,x)\le 2+\max(m,n)\le D\). If \(x\) is in another cube, take the shorter circular route from \(p\) to \(x\): it starts by traversing an incident cube, and the corresponding first step can instead be made through \(z\)'s neighbour. Consequently \(d(z,x)\le d(p,x)\le D\). For two square vertices at the same junction, the distance is at most four. For square vertices whose junctions are adjacent in the six-block circle, it is at most \(\max(m,n)+4\le D\), by going through those junctions. If their junctions are nonadjacent, take a shortest arc between the junctions, which traverses at least two complete cubes. The two square-neighbour steps can substitute for the first and last steps of that arc, giving distance no greater than its length, at most \(D\). The inequalities hold because \(m,n\ge3\). Finally, for any square vertex \(z\) at junction \(p\), choose a base vertex \(x\) with \(d(p,x)=D\). Every neighbour \(u\) of \(z\) satisfies \(d(p,u)=1\), hence \(d(u,x)\ge D-1\) and \(d(z,x)\ge D\). Therefore every square vertex also has eccentricity \(D\).

**6. Odd girth and the exceptional cycle.** If \(C\) is even, the circle coordinate modulo two properly colours all base edges; each square vertex receives its junction's parity and is opposite to both its neighbours. If \(C\) is odd, following a shortest path from one junction to the next through each of the six cubes yields an odd cycle of length \(C\). Any odd closed walk in the base must wind an odd multiple of \(C\) in its circle coordinate, and therefore has at least \(C\) edges. An odd cycle through square vertices can be rerouted through their junctions with no length increase, yielding an odd base walk. Hence odd girth is exactly \(C\). Since \(C\) is divisible by three, the circle coordinate modulo three also gives a proper three-colouring of the base and extends over each square vertex by using its junction's colour. For \((3,3,3)\), Step 1 supplies exactly the six isolated extra states; the other 60 states form the model of Steps 2–5, with \(D=9\).

## Verification
Two independent standalone Python scripts accompany this result. `verify_direct.py` enumerates all \(3^{m+n}\) ternary assignments, filters directly against the edge set of the matching-deleted graph, generates every one-vertex move, and runs breadth-first search from all configurations. It verifies all allowed \(t\) for the parameter pairs \((m,n)=(3,3),(3,4),(3,5),(4,4),(4,5),(4,6),(5,5)\), including the six isolated states for \((3,3,3)\). `verify_constructive.py` independently generates the six cubes and square vertices without brute-force colour-assignment filtering, then verifies the stated intersection pattern, degrees, graph order, edges and all eccentricities for 31 further parameter selections including \((m,n)=(4,7),(5,6),(6,6),(5,8)\). Both scripts have been executed successfully; their finite checks corroborate but do not replace the all-parameter proof.

## Relationship to prior work
Cereceda, van den Heuvel and Johnson's early investigation established the general framework of three-colouring graphs and their connectivity for bipartite graphs; it does not provide these exact matching-deletion graphs or metrics. Johnson and collaborators supplied a general polynomial-time shortest-path algorithm for three-colouring reconfiguration, which does not classify the present family. Celaya, Choo, MacGillivray and Seyffarth explicitly decompose the complete bipartite three-colouring graph into six hypercube blocks, and determine Hamiltonicity for this zero-deletion case. Neither the zero-deletion case nor the previously analysed one-deleted-edge case is proposed as new here. The present result is the **simultaneous matching-deletion theorem for \(t\ge2\)**, showing no new interaction between distinct missing edges except at the sharp \(C_6\) exception, and determining the exact global metric and odd-girth behaviour of those larger families.

## Limitations
Only deletions forming a matching, with part sizes at least three, are covered. There is no claim of independent scholarly validation or an absolute historical novelty certificate. An equivalent result could exist in unindexed literature. The direct computer checks cover finitely many small sizes; the unrestricted result rests on the proof. The theorem does not address mixing time, Hamiltonicity after deletion, or more than three named colours.
