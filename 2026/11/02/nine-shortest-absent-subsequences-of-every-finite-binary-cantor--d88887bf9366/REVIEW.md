# Scientific assessment

## Correctness — PASS
The proof reduces the infinite family to the explicitly presented periodic subsequence \(G_m\), establishes the full shortest-absence language of \(G_m\) using a backward shortest-missing-distance table, and then shows each of the nine candidate strings is impossible in the Cantor word because of the two singleton boundary gaps and the exact number of ones. The transition table establishes all lengths simultaneously. The standalone verifier recomputes finite instances without serving as the induction proof.

## Originality — PASS (bounded literature priority)
The 2016 Cantor-like paper concerns permutation and contiguous-factor complexity, whereas the present claim classifies all shortest *scattered* missing strings of finite iterates. The 2021/2023 shortest-absent-subsequence literature provides algorithms and general characterizations but no inspected Cantor-specific closed formula; the 2017 Cantor work addresses \(k\)-abelian factor complexity. Earlier general algorithms can compute finite instances, but the closed all-rank language, its constant cardinality, and the structural comparison are not implied by the numerical complexity results inspected. Searches of research records and accessible historical claim ledgers found no matching language or dominating special-family statement. A broader unlocated priority claim remains possible; this is not an assertion of exhaustive novelty.

## Value — PASS
The exact language gives a complete, natural infinite-family invariant for a classical nonuniformly recurrent automatic word. That its shortest absent strings always number exactly nine while the word grows exponentially is a structural phenomenon explained by the singleton boundary gaps and the periodic subsequence comparison; this is not a small numerical gain or an arbitrary finite slice.
