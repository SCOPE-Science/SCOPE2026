# Sharp fifth-level saturation for order-eleven power sums modulo prime powers

## Finding
For coprime positive integers \(q,e\), write \(m(q,e)\) for the least positive integer \(t\) such that a sum of \(t\) (not necessarily distinct) nonnegative powers of \(q\) is divisible by \(e\). If a prime \(p\equiv1\pmod{11}\), an integer \(k\ge3\), and a unit \(q\) of exact multiplicative order \(11\) modulo \(p^k\) are given, then

\[
m(q,p^k)=\begin{cases}
9,&p=23\text{ and }k\in\{3,4\},\\
11,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

Consequently, the least common stabilization exponent for every order-eleven subgroup over all primes is exactly \(5\). For every prime other than \(23\) the same conclusion is already true at exponent \(3\); the two exceptional values are genuine. The sequence at \(p=23\) through level \(5\), namely \((3,5,9,9,11)\), is reported in the originating paper; the contribution here is the **uniform assertion for all primes**, the sharp global threshold, and exhaustive completeness of the exceptional cases.

## Assumptions and scope
The prime \(p\) is any prime congruent to \(1\) modulo \(11\); \(k\) ranges over all integers at least \(3\). The base \(q\) may be any integer whose residue has exact order \(11\) in \((\mathbb Z/p^k\mathbb Z)^\times\). Repetition is permitted in a sum of powers, and powers have exponents in \(\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\). No positivity restriction on the integer representative of \(q\) affects the residue problem; if the conventional definition requires \(q>1\), select any positive representative greater than one. The conclusion depends on the subgroup generated by \(q\), not on a particular generator.

## Proof
**Step 1: reduction to eleven coefficients and an upper bound.** The kernel of reduction \((\mathbb Z/p^k\mathbb Z)^\times\to(\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z)^\times\) is a \(p\)-group. Since \(p\ne11\), reduction preserves the exact order \(11\). Thus \(q-1\) is a unit modulo \(p^k\), so \((q-1)(1+q+\cdots+q^{10})=q^{11}-1\equiv0\pmod{p^k}\) gives \(m(q,p^k)\le11\). Every sum of at most ten powers is equivalent, by reducing exponents modulo \(11\), to

\[
g(q)=\sum_{i=0}^{10}a_iq^i\equiv0\pmod{p^k},\qquad a_i\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0},\quad 1\le\sum_i a_i\le10.
\]

The unit group modulo odd prime powers is cyclic; hence it has a unique subgroup of order \(11\). Its generators permute the eleven group elements, so the existence of a short sum is independent of the choice of \(q\).

**Step 2: the infinite-to-finite resultant bound.** Let \(\Phi_{11}(X)=1+X+\cdots+X^{10}\) and let \(\zeta\) be a primitive complex eleventh root of unity. For any polynomial \(g(X)=\sum a_iX^i\) in Step 1 with \(t=\sum a_i\le10\), one has \(g(\zeta)\ne0\). Indeed, irreducibility of \(\Phi_{11}\) and \(\deg g\le10\) would otherwise imply \(g=c\Phi_{11}\), which with nonnegative integer coefficients forces \(t=11c\), impossible for \(1\le t\le10\). Hence the integral resultant

\[
R=\operatorname{Res}(\Phi_{11},g)=\prod_{j=1}^{10}g(\zeta^j)
\]

is nonzero and satisfies \(1\le|R|\le t^{10}\le10^{10}\) by the triangle inequality. The integer-polynomial Bézout identity for a resultant writes \(R=A(X)\Phi_{11}(X)+B(X)g(X)\) for polynomials \(A,B\in\mathbb Z[X]\). Since \(\Phi_{11}(q)\equiv g(q)\equiv0\pmod{p^k}\), it follows that \(p^k\mid R\). Thus **no** sum of length at most ten can vanish when \(p^k>10^{10}\). In particular, if \(p\ge2155\) and \(k\ge3\), then \(m(q,p^k)=11\). This norm technique is already present as a general prior method; it is used here solely to justify an exhaustive finite remainder.

**Step 3: finite, reproducible exhaustive classification.** The primes \(p<2155\) satisfying \(p\equiv1\pmod{11}\) are exactly

\[
\begin{gathered}
23,67,89,199,331,353,397,419,463,617,661,683,727,859,881,947,\\
991,1013,1123,1277,1321,1409,1453,1607,1783,1871,2003,2069,2113.
\end{gathered}
\]

For each of these \(29\) primes, the attached executable `verify_order11.py` constructs one exact-order-eleven element modulo \(p^3\) and checks every coefficient vector in Step 1. There are precisely \(\binom{21}{11}-1=352715\) such vectors, by stars and bars. The result: only \(p=23\) has a vanishing sum shorter than eleven, and its shortest length is \(9\). The same exhaustive procedure at \(p=23\) and levels \(4\) and \(5\) gives respectively \(9\) and \(11\). This totals \(31\) full modulus scans and \(10,934,165\) coefficient vectors, using exact integer modular arithmetic. The code asserts these counts, the exact list of primes, all individual conclusions, and explicit exceptional witnesses. No probabilistic primality oracle, floating-point tolerance or cutoff beyond the proved resultant reduction is used.

An illustrative exceptional witness at level four is \(q=60793\pmod{23^4}\), of order eleven, with

\[
q^2+2q^4+4q^7+q^8+q^{10}\equiv0\pmod{23^4}.
\]

The same exponent-multiplicity pattern works at level three for \(q=12125\pmod{23^3}\). The checker directly verifies both witnesses.

**Step 4: lifting to every higher prime-power level.** If a sum of length at most ten vanished modulo \(p^k\) with \(k\ge3\), its reduction to \(p^3\) would also vanish. This rules out all primes other than \(23\), by Step 3. At \(p=23\), reduction to \(23^5\) rules out every such sum when \(k\ge5\). Step 1 supplies the universal eleven-term sum at every level. The two exact nine-term exceptional minima were verified exhaustively, completing the classification. Since \(m(q,23^4)=9\), the uniform threshold cannot be smaller than \(5\).

```

WITNESS p=23 k=3 length=9 q=12125 counts=0,0,1,0,2,0,0,4,1,0,1
WITNESS p=23 k=4 length=9 q=60793 counts=0,0,1,0,2,0,0,4,1,0,1
```

The full integer search is a certificate for the finite remainder of the proof. It is not by itself a proof for infinitely many primes or exponents: the resultant bound and reduction to lower prime-power levels provide those steps. A separate short-multiset enumeration in the script cross-checks a subset of cases.

## Relationship to prior work
Jacob and Külshammer, *Congruences for sums of powers of an integer* (arXiv:2204.09403, first posted 20 April 2022; published 2023), define \(m(q,e)\), study prime-power stabilization, and in their order-eleven examples already report \(m(q,23^k)=3,5,9,9,11\) for the first five levels, as well as selected data for primes \(67\) and \(89\). Those numerical cases and the general problem are prior work, not new claims here. Breuer, Héthelyi, Horváth and Külshammer (arXiv:1912.03065, Section 2) develop related invariants and finite prime-order examples but do not present this uniform order-eleven prime-cube classification. A previously accessible mathematical record also gives a general cyclotomic-resultant large-prime cutoff; the present proof uses that method and adds an exact, sharp finite exception analysis. Another earlier record classifies the three- and four-term order-eleven zero sums over prime fields, not this prime-power saturation. The new assertion concerns **all** primes and all prime powers from level three onward, not the previously tabulated small-prime samples or the already established generic large-modulus criterion.

## Limitations
This classification is restricted to cyclic subgroups of order exactly \(11\) modulo powers of primes congruent to \(1\) modulo \(11\). It does not determine \(m(q,p^k)\) at all first or second levels for all primes, nor does it treat other subgroup orders or composite moduli. Completeness of the modular verification is inspectable in the included source; no formal proof-assistant or independent human review has been performed. Bibliographic searches and accessible source inspections do not eliminate the possibility of an unindexed equivalent theorem.
