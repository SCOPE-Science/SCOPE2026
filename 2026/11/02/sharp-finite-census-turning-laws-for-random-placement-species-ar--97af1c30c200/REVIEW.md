# Scientific review

## Correctness — PASS

For every positive integer community abundance vector, the binomial absence probability gives expected richness as a finite polynomial. The terminal first and second derivatives depend exactly on singleton and doubleton abundances, yielding the stated one-sided elasticity derivative with no extrapolation from numeric plots. For the \((1,n)\) family, a direct quotient differentiation gives the printed polynomial \(P_n\). Its coefficients have exactly one sign change and its endpoint values have opposite signs, giving one and only one positive root in \((0,1)\). The direction of the sign is correctly reversed by \(q=1-a\). Explicit enumeration of integer partitions up to three individuals proves sharp minimality. Independent exact integer coefficient checks and finite numerical tests passed; they are not the infinite proof.

## Originality — PASS, qualified by priority risks

He and Legendre (2002) explicitly gave the same random-placement expected-richness equation and even the most-uneven abundance case, so neither expression is original. Tjørve et al. (2008) already explained qualitative S-shaped and inverted-S-shaped log–log species–area curves from abundance and occupancy heterogeneity, so such curvature changes are not claimed to be discovered here. The new mathematical scope is the full finite-count terminal singleton/doubleton criterion, a Descartes-based all-integer uniqueness classification for \((1,n)\), and exact minimal four-individual onset. These implications were not found in the materially inspected He–Legendre and Tjørve sections, nor in the distinct closest published mathematical research-record summaries. One previously recorded two-species Poisson result exhibits two positive elasticity turnings, but a fixed finite census has different terminal behavior and the Poisson statement does not imply the one-minimum finite theorem. The complete Coleman 1981 text was inaccessible; a broader classic theorem could subsume the specialized derivative analysis. Absence of a matched search hit is not a priority proof.

## Mathematical value — PASS

An instantaneous log–log exponent is a natural scale-dependent statistic for species–area curves. Whether its tail rises determines whether applying one fitted slope to most of the sampled area obscures a rare-species effect. The terminal sign law is a complete all-community local diagnostic requiring only the two lowest abundance-frequency counts. The two-species theorem gives a natural exact global shape classification, with a sharp four-individual onset, not an arbitrary numerical slice. This is a self-contained motivated structural fact; no empirical or downstream theorem is asserted.

## Closest literature and limitations

He and Legendre (2002), Eq. (1) and Table 1, provide the exact baseline distribution and most-uneven formula but study abundance effects on expected richness. Tjørve et al. (2008) cover qualitative nonmonotonic log–log curves for related grid occupancy and must be credited for that phenomenon. Coleman (1981) is the classical antecedent and remains insufficiently inspected. The theorem assumes independent uniform individual locations and concerns expected rather than realized richness. The terminal equality boundary and arbitrary-community counts of interior extrema are not resolved.
