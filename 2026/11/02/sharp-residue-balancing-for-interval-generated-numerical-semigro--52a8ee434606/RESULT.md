# Sharp residue balancing for interval-generated numerical semigroups

## Finding

Fix integers \(n\ge 3\), \(2\le k<n\), and a divisor \(m\ge2\) of \(k\). Let \(S=\langle n,n+1,\ldots,n+k\rangle\), let \(H=\mathbb N_0\setminus S\), and write \(N_r=\#\{h\in H:h\equiv r\pmod m\}\) for \(0\le r<m\). Set \(L=\lceil(n-1)/k\rceil\) and
\[
C=\frac{k}{m}\binom{L}{2}.
\]
Then the **complete residue histogram** is
\[
\boxed{N_r=\#\{1\le x<Ln:x\equiv r\pmod m\}-\#\{1\le t<L:tn\equiv r\pmod m\}-C.}
\]
In particular, the gaps are equidistributed modulo \(m\) if and only if
\[
\boxed{\gcd(n,m)=1\quad\text{and}\quad\bigl(n\equiv1\pmod m\ \text{or}\ L\equiv0\pmod m\bigr).}
\]
When \(\gcd(n,m)=1\), the difference between the largest and smallest residue counts is **at most two**, independently of \(n,k,m\). This sharp universal constant is attained for every \(m\ge4\) by \(n=2m-1,k=m\). For \(m=2\) the difference is always zero; for \(m=3\) it is at most one, attained by \(n=5,k=3\). In the opposite regime \(m\mid n\), the difference is exactly \(L\), which can grow without bound.

## Assumptions and scope

The semigroup is generated by every integer in the interval from \(n\) to \(n+k\); it is not an arbitrary arithmetic-progression semigroup. The theorem applies only when the tested modulus divides the interval width \(k\). No primality assumption on \(m\) is made. A residue histogram records *distinct gaps*, not factorizations with multiplicity. All assertions hold for arbitrarily large parameters.

## Proof

**1. Exact gap intervals.** A sum of precisely \(t\) generators can have any value in the integer interval \([tn,t(n+k)]\): its offset from \(tn\) is the sum of \(t\) numbers in \([0,k]\), which covers every integer in \([0,tk]\). Hence the positive elements of \(S\) form the union of these intervals for \(t\ge1\). The nonempty intervening gaps are exactly
\[
I_t=[t(n+k)+1,(t+1)n-1],\qquad 0\le t<L,
\]
where \(L=\lceil(n-1)/k\rceil\) because \(tk\le n-2\) precisely for these \(t\). After these intervals, the generated intervals touch or overlap, so no further gaps occur.

**2. Histogram identity.** For each \(t<L\), compare \(I_t\) to \(J_t=[tn+1,(t+1)n-1]\). Both begin in the same residue modulo \(m\) because \(m\mid k\). The length of \(I_t\) is \(n-1-tk\), which is shorter than the length \(n-1\) of \(J_t\) by exactly \(tk\), an integer multiple of \(m\). Consequently every residue appears precisely \(tk/m\) fewer times in \(I_t\) than in \(J_t\). The intervals \(J_t\) partition \([1,Ln-1]\) except for the points \(n,2n,\ldots,(L-1)n\). Summing the \(tk/m\) corrections gives \((k/m)\binom{L}{2}\), proving the boxed histogram identity. As a consistency check, the genus is \(L(n-1)-k\binom{L}{2}\).

**3. Uniform bound and sharpness.** If \(\gcd(n,m)=1\), the first term in the histogram formula counts a consecutive interval of integers and differs by at most one as the residue changes. The second term also differs by at most one: multiplication by \(n\) permutes the residues modulo \(m\), so \(tn\) for \(1\le t<L\) visits each residue either \(\lfloor(L-1)/m\rfloor\) or \(\lceil(L-1)/m\rceil\) times. Their difference, minus the constant \(C\), therefore has spread at most two.

For sharpness, take any \(m\ge4\), \(k=m\), \(n=2m-1\). Here \(L=2\), and the gap intervals are \([1,2m-2]\) and \([3m,4m-3]\). In residue order \(0,1,\ldots,m-1\) the histogram is
\[
(2,\underbrace{3,\ldots,3}_{m-3\text{ times}},2,1),
\]
which has spread two. If \(m=2\), coprimality means \(n\equiv1\pmod2\), so the exact criterion in step 4 shows equality. If \(m=3\), coprimality gives \(n\equiv1\) or \(2\pmod3\). In the latter case, writing \(L\) modulo three in the histogram formula shows that the incomplete block in each of the two counts contributes at most one discrepancy overall: if \(L\equiv0\), both are uniform; if \(L\equiv1\), only the first count has a one-element remainder; if \(L\equiv2\), only the second has one. This yields spread at most one; \((n,k,m)=(5,3,3)\) gives counts \((2,2,1)\).

If \(m\mid n\), the first term of the histogram formula has count \(Ln/m-1\) in residue zero and \(Ln/m\) in each other residue. The second term puts all \(L-1\) points in residue zero. The other residue counts therefore exceed the zero count by exactly \(L\).

**4. Exact equidistribution criterion.** Let \(P(X)=\sum_{h\in H}X^h\). From the interval formula,
\[
(1-X)P(X)=\sum_{t=0}^{L-1}\bigl(X^{t(n+k)+1}-X^{(t+1)n}\bigr).
\]
Since \(m\mid k\), modulo \(X^m-1\) this becomes
\[
(1-X)P(X)\equiv(X-X^n)\sum_{t=0}^{L-1}X^{tn}.
\]
The residue counts \(N_r\) are all equal precisely when \((1-X)P(X)\equiv0\pmod{X^m-1}\): multiplication by \(1-X\) takes the cyclic first difference of the coefficient vector, whose kernel consists of constant vectors.

Thus equidistribution is equivalent to equality (with multiplicities) of the residue multisets \(\{1+tn:0\le t<L\}\) and \(\{n+tn:0\le t<L\}\) modulo \(m\). If \(g=\gcd(n,m)>1\), the first multiset lives entirely in residue \(1\pmod g\), the second in residue \(0\pmod g\), so equality is impossible. If \(g=1\), multiplying residues by \(n^{-1}\pmod m\) converts them to the consecutive cyclic blocks \(\{n^{-1}+t:0\le t<L\}\) and \(\{1+t:0\le t<L\}\). If \(m\mid L\), both cover every residue equally often. Otherwise write \(L=qm+s\), \(0<s<m\); after removing \(q\) full cycles, each is a nonempty proper consecutive arc of \(s\) residues. Such an arc has no nonidentity translational symmetry on the cycle, since it has exactly one cyclic transition from occupied to unoccupied. Therefore the arcs agree exactly when \(n^{-1}\equiv1\pmod m\), or equivalently \(n\equiv1\pmod m\). This proves the criterion without assuming it from a finite computation.

## Verification

The standalone \(\texttt{verify.py}\) checks semigroup membership by an independent dynamic-programming enumeration rather than using the gap-interval formula as its membership oracle. It compares the enumerated gaps to the theorem's intervals, independently checks the boxed residue-count identity and equidistribution condition for every divisor modulus of the widths, tests the sharp two-discrepancy examples, and separately checks the resonant case \(m\mid n\). It passed 2,844 semigroups, 7,921 divisor-modulus profiles, and 26 sharp examples. Finite checks supplement, but do not establish, the all-parameter theorem.

## Relationship to prior work

Caleb M. Shor's 2021/2022 paper supplies a general Apéry-multiset criterion for equidistribution of numerical-semigroup gaps (Proposition 3.9) and specialized classifications for semigroups whose nonzero Apéry elements form an arithmetic progression (Theorem 4.5, Corollaries 5.6 and 5.10). That general criterion can, in principle, be specialized to the family here, and maximal-embedding-dimension and two-/three-generator boundary cases are already accounted for by existing results. The contribution stated here is the explicit, simultaneous arithmetic criterion **for every interval width and every modulus dividing it**, together with the exact histogram formula and sharp two-discrepancy phenomenon. For \(2\le k<n-1\), the usual Apéry elements \(r+n\lceil r/k\rceil\) do not, in general, form one arithmetic progression. The 2023 work of Robles-Pérez and Rosales concerns monotone Apéry structure and related semigroup invariants, not this complete modular-residue classification. These comparisons do not prove that no unindexed source contains an implicit equivalent.

## Limitations

The modulus must divide \(k\). The bound of two requires \(\gcd(n,m)=1\); it fails dramatically when \(m\mid n\), as quantified above. The theorem concerns the distribution of gaps, not their factorization lengths, random sampling, or moduli outside the stated range. No statement of independent review or literature exhaustiveness is made.
