# Sharp three-point exchange connectivity and geodesic multiplicities of the hyperovals of \(\operatorname{PG}(2,4)\)

## Finding
Let \(\mathcal H\) denote every six-point hyperoval in the classical projective plane \(\operatorname{PG}(2,4)\). For integer \(j\ge1\), define the (at-most-\(j\))-exchange graph \(\mathcal R_j\) on \(\mathcal H\), where distinct \(A,B\) are adjacent precisely when \(|A\setminus B|=|B\setminus A|\le j\). Then the **exact minimum exchange batch size for connectivity is three**. Specifically, \(\mathcal R_1\) and \(\mathcal R_2\) have no edges; \(\mathcal R_3\) has 168 vertices, 3360 edges, is 40-regular, and has radius and diameter two.

For any two distinct hyperovals with \(s=|A\cap B|\), the number of their common neighbors in \(\mathcal R_3\) is
\[
|N(A)\cap N(B)|=\begin{cases}4&s=0,\\5&s=1,\\16&s=2,\\11&s=3.\end{cases}
\]
The unordered-pair intersection census for \(s=0,1,2,3\) is respectively \(840,6048,3780,3360\). In particular, every pair of nonadjacent configurations has at least four distinct shortest paths of length two. The distance-one pair count is \(3360\), and the distance-two pair count is \(10668\).

## Assumptions and scope
Use \(\mathbb F_4=\mathbb F_2[\alpha]/(\alpha^2+\alpha+1)\) and let the points of \(\operatorname{PG}(2,4)\) be one-dimensional subspaces of \(\mathbb F_4^3\). Each projective point is represented uniquely by the normalized nonzero vector whose first nonzero coordinate is one. The 21 projective lines are the kernels of the 21 normalized nonzero linear forms, each incident with five points. A **hyperoval** here means a subset of exactly six projective points with at most two points on every projective line. All sets are unlabelled subsets of fixed, labelled projective points. Only the Desarguesian plane of order four is considered. The exchange rule is a batch replacement of up to \(j\) points, not a succession of illegal intermediate one-point steps.

## Proof
The theorem admits a finite complete enumeration proof, rather than an asymptotic or probabilistic argument. The stand-alone `direct_incidence.py` constructs \(\mathbb F_4\) as four 2-bit polynomials, checks all 21 normalized projective points and all 21 line kernels, and iterates through every one of the \(\binom{21}{6}=54264\) possible six-element point sets. For each candidate it verifies the six-point hyperoval condition against *every* projective line. Exactly 168 pass. This tests the defining condition for all possible candidates, so no classification or sampling assumption enters.

The same checker enumerates every four-arc (four-point set containing no collinear triple). There are exactly 2520, and each occurs in exactly one hyperoval: the 168 hyperovals each have 15 four-element subsets, and the multiplicities are individually checked to be one. Therefore distinct hyperovals intersect in at most three points, establishing that no batch of one or two replacements can connect distinct states.

For each unordered pair of the 168 certified hyperovals, the checker counts its intersection and constructs the exchange relation \(|A\cap B|=3\). These \(\binom{168}{2}=14028\) exact pairwise comparisons yield intersection counts \((840,6048,3780,3360)\) at intersections \((0,1,2,3)\). They produce 40 neighbors for each hyperoval and 3360 edges. For every unordered pair, the program separately constructs both adjacency sets and computes their intersection cardinality, obtaining respectively \((4,5,16,11)\) for the four possible intersection sizes, with no exceptions.

If \(|A\cap B|=3\), the two states are adjacent. Otherwise their common-neighbor number is positive (at least four); hence they are connected by a two-edge path. Since some pairs are nonadjacent, the diameter and all vertex eccentricities equal two. The full graph therefore has radius two as well. The counts of unordered pairs at distances one and two are 3360 and \(14028-3360=10668\). For nonadjacent vertices, each distinct common neighbor gives a different length-two geodesic.

A second independently constructed checker, `projective_orbit.py`, begins from the regular hyperoval \(\{[(t^2,t,1)]:t\in\mathbb F_4\}\cup\{[(1,0,0)],[(0,1,0)]\}\), generates its orbit by explicit invertible projective transformations, and obtains 168 distinct images. It separately rebuilds their pairwise intersection and common-neighbor tables and runs 168 breadth-first shortest-path searches. This confirms every reported exchange invariant without relying on the first program's six-subset enumeration. The complete direct enumeration is the completeness proof; the orbit checker provides independent corroboration, not an unproved assumption that one orbit contains all hyperovals.

## Verification
Both programs are included, fully executable under Python 3 using only the standard library. Run `python3 direct_incidence.py` and `python3 projective_orbit.py` in any order. The expected terminal markers are `DIRECT_FINITE_INCIDENCE_AND_EXCHANGE_PASS` and `INDEPENDENT_PGL_ORBIT_PASS`. The first checker certifies the exhaustive search domain and the unique four-arc completion statement; the second recomputes the 168-state graph from projective group generators. All finite assertions above are checked by integer arithmetic and set operations. There is no step extrapolating finite computations to an infinite family.

## Relationship to prior work
The existence and uniqueness up to projective equivalence of the six-point hyperoval of \(\operatorname{PG}(2,4)\) is classical. Betten describes the regular hyperoval, unique completion of a fundamental quadrangle and general hyperoval-search techniques. Bamberg, Glasby, Popiel and Praeger treat regular hyperovals and transitive pseudo-hyperovals in a different classification problem. Bruen and McQuillan study intersection equivalence relations between hyperconics; Miyamoto, Shinoha and Hagita study pairwise intersection sizes. Those static results are not claimed as new. The claim here is the **sharp exchange threshold and the full table of common-neighbor/geodesic multiplicities** for a precisely defined 168-configuration graph; pairwise intersection information alone does not specify the number of common neighbors unless additional triple-relation information is supplied. The available primary material and targeted searches yielded no explicit equivalent graph-metric theorem; this is not a proof of global novelty.

## Limitations
This is a finite, computer-assisted theorem about only \(\operatorname{PG}(2,4)\). An independent human reproduction is not yet provided. Possible equivalence with a previously tabulated association scheme or a historical hyperoval intersection graph remains a genuine priority risk; the full 2001 Bruen–McQuillan article was not accessible, and its abstract alone cannot exclude stronger coverage. The available primary literature and claims were compared as described in the accompanying review; exhaustive searches across all nomenclature are not claimed. The checkers are complete for the fixed finite plane but are not a proof of anything about larger orders or non-Desarguesian planes.
