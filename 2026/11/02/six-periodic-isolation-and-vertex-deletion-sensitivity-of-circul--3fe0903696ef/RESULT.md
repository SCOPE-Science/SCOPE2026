# Six-periodic isolation and vertex-deletion sensitivity of circular ladder graphs

## Finding
For every integer \(n\ge3\), let \(P_n=C_n\square K_2\) be the circular ladder (prism graph), and let \(v\) be any vertex. The isolation number \(\iota(G)\), equivalently the vertex-edge domination number \(\gamma_{ve}(G)\), is the smallest cardinality of \(S\subseteq V(G)\) for which \(G-N_G[S]\) is edgeless. Then
\[
\iota(P_n)=\left\lceil\frac n3\right\rceil+\mathbf{1}_{n\equiv3\pmod6},\qquad
\iota(P_n-v)=\left\lceil\frac n3\right\rceil-\mathbf{1}_{n\equiv4\pmod6}.
\]
Consequently every vertex is deletion-decreasing exactly when \(n\equiv3,4\pmod6\); for the other four residue classes vertex deletion preserves the invariant. No vertex deletion increases the invariant. For even \(n\), the first equality is a special case of an established general theorem for bipartite graph prisms [2], and is not claimed as new. The odd-prism exact values and the complete deletion-sensitive classification are the substantive claims.

## Assumptions and scope
All graphs are finite, simple, undirected. Vertices of \(P_n\) are \((i,r)\) with \(i\in\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z\) and \(r\in\{0,1\}\); its edges join \((i,r)\) to \((i+1,r)\) and join \((i,0)\) to \((i,1)\). Write the two layers as top \(r=0\) and bottom \(r=1\). An edge is isolated (vertex-edge dominated) by \(S\) if at least one of its endpoints lies in \(N[S]\). A single vertex deletion removes all incident edges. The graph is vertex-transitive, so for the deleted case it suffices to remove \(v=(0,0)\).

## Proof
**Local gap lemma.** Suppose a set \(S\) contains one selected vertex in each of certain cyclic columns; write successive selected columns as \(c_j\), and write their positive cyclic gaps as \(g_j\). If all gaps belong to \(\{1,2,3\}\) and the two selected vertices at the ends of every gap of size three lie in opposite layers, then \(S\) isolates \(P_n\). Indeed, every column is at distance at most one from a selected column, and therefore every rung has an endpoint in \(N[S]\). Within a gap of size one, both columns contain a selected vertex, so both layers are dominated there. Within a gap of size two, each endpoint column contains a selected vertex, so both layers of those columns are dominated, covering both intervening horizontal edges. In a gap of size three, the first internal column is dominated in the selected layer of the left endpoint and the second internal column in the selected layer of the right endpoint; the two horizontal edges adjacent to selected columns are covered, while the unique middle horizontal edge is covered in both layers if the endpoint layers are opposite.

**Lower bound for the intact graph.** Each of the \(n\) rung edges can be isolated only by a vertex selected from its own column or one of its two neighboring columns. One selected vertex serves at most three distinct rung edges, hence \(|S|\ge\lceil n/3\rceil\). If \(n=3k\) and \(|S|=k\), the three-rung neighborhoods of these \(k\) sites must partition all columns. The selected columns therefore occur at every third position, with all cyclic gaps equal to three. In any such gap, if endpoint selections had the same layer, the horizontal edge in the other layer between the two internal columns would have neither endpoint in \(N[S]\). Consequently the selected layers must alternate at each gap. This is impossible on an odd cycle of \(k\) sites. Thus \(n\equiv3\pmod6\) requires at least \(\lceil n/3\rceil+1\) selections. The case \(n=3\) is included: a single selected vertex leaves an edge in the opposite layer undominated.

**Upper bound for the intact graph.** If \(n\not\equiv0,3\pmod6\), choose columns \(0,3,6,\ldots,3\lfloor n/3\rfloor\), alternating the selected layer from one site to the next. These columns have gaps of three except for one closing gap of one or two; the gap lemma applies, and the number selected is \(\lceil n/3\rceil\). If \(n\equiv0\pmod6\), use columns \(0,3,\ldots,n-3\) and alternate layers; there are an even number of sites, so alternation is consistent across the closing gap. If \(n\equiv3\pmod6\), use columns \(0,1,3,6,\ldots,n-3\). Give column zero the top layer, column one any layer, and columns \(3,6,\ldots,n-3\) alternating, starting and ending with the layer opposite to that at column zero where demanded by the length-three closing gap. These selections create two short gaps of lengths one and two and an even-length chain of length-three gaps, and their number is \(n/3+1\). The gap lemma proves isolation in every case.

**Deleted rung lower bound.** Delete the top vertex in column zero. Rungs in columns \(1,2,\ldots,n-1\) survive, and every selected site can isolate at most three of these \(n-1\) rungs. Hence \(\iota(P_n-v)\ge\lceil(n-1)/3\rceil\). For \(n\not\equiv1,4\pmod6\), this already equals \(\lceil n/3\rceil\). For \(n=6k+1\), with \(k\ge1\), assume that \(2k\) vertices isolate the deleted graph. Their three-rung intervals must partition the \(6k\) surviving rungs exactly, and so the selected columns are forced to be \(2,5,8,\ldots,n-2\). Every interior gap of three forces opposite selected layers, just as above: its middle edge on the layer opposite to the endpoint choices would otherwise be undominated. The two surviving bottom-layer edges meeting column zero require the first and last selected vertices (columns two and \(n-2\)) both to lie in the bottom layer, since no other sites cover their bottom endpoints. However there are \(2k\) selected sites and \(2k-1\) interior gaps of three, an odd number, so their layers at the ends must be opposite. Contradiction. Thus the deletion number is at least \(\lceil n/3\rceil\) for \(n\equiv1\pmod6\).

**Deleted upper bounds.** For \(n\equiv4\pmod6\), choose bottom, top, bottom, top, ... along columns \(2,5,\ldots,n-2\), with first and last bottom. There are \((n-1)/3=\lceil n/3\rceil-1\) selections. These cover all surviving rungs, all horizontal edges between columns one and \(n-1\), and the two surviving bottom edges incident to column zero; all horizontal edges incident to the deleted top vertex are absent. For \(n\equiv3\pmod6\), use the alternating sequence bottom, top, ..., bottom in columns \(1,4,\ldots,n-2\), of size \(n/3=\lceil n/3\rceil\). Exactly the same edge check applies, including the case \(n=3\). In all other residue classes, take an intact-graph isolating set of size \(\lceil n/3\rceil\) from the previous construction. If it includes the deleted top vertex, exchange top and bottom layers everywhere. Restricting the resulting set to the deleted graph preserves isolation. This proves both exact formulas.

## Verification
The standalone file `verify.py` builds the actual labelled graph, checks the definition directly edge by edge, exhaustively determines both minimum cardinalities (and numbers of minima) from all vertex subsets for every \(3\le n\le10\), and tests explicit extremal witnesses for each integer \(3\le n\le600\) in both graph families. Run `python3 verify.py` with Python 3 and only its standard library. Output ends with `VERIFY_OK`. Finite tests corroborate but do not replace the general proof.

## Relationship to prior work
Li, Zhang, and Dong [1] established general deletion-change bounds, structural results and sharp inequalities for vertex-edge domination; their inspected original 17-page manuscript does not calculate the circular-ladder family. Brešar et al. [2, Theorem 3.7] prove \(\iota(G\square K_2)=\gamma(G)\) for every bipartite graph \(G\), so the intact-graph formula for even \(n\) is already implied by that stronger theorem and ordinary cycle domination. Their general inequality \(\gamma(G)\le\iota(G\square K_2)\) supplies the common lower bound, but their theorem does not settle odd cycles or deletion of a vertex from the prism. Caro and Hansberg [3] treat general isolation bounds and two-dimensional grid products; their inspected Cartesian-product work does not furnish the present six-periodic deletion classification. These implication comparisons matter more than matching titles; historical priority remains provisional against inaccessible or unindexed results.

## Limitations
This theorem concerns simple circular ladders and the deletion of one vertex. It does not classify all minimum isolating sets, multiple-vertex deletions, or arbitrary Cartesian prisms. The intact even-cycle value is a known corollary, not an independent novelty claim. The proof is a self-contained mathematical argument accompanied by finite reproducibility checks; no independent mathematical audit or universal machine formalization was performed. No assertion of exhaustive historical novelty is made.
