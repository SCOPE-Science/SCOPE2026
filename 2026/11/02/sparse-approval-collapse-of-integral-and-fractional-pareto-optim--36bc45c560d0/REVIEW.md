# Scientific review

## Correctness — PASS

The threshold decomposition is valid for every real fractional committee, not merely grid points. Every single-approval or two-approved-selected ballot forces zero loss; every crossing doubleton imposes the matching threshold implication. The exchange lemma treats both cases for an approved, unselected candidate and produces a strictly better size-\(k\) committee whenever threshold losses outnumber gains. Integrating threshold counts and the weak Pareto improvements then yields an exact-size strict integral improvement, contradicting PO. The sharpness example is verified by all ten two-seat committees. The independent, small-domain exact rational checks exercise the definitions, but the universal conclusion is supported by proof, not by extrapolating finite experiments.

## Originality — PASS, residual priority risk

Becker et al. [1] establish general fPO theory and an equivalence theorem on cloning-closed single-dominance-only domains. Their paper's restricted-domain arguments do not state the two-approval boundary. A concrete two-approval five-ballot example fails single dominance, so their criterion does not automatically subsume the present theorem. Schünke [2] studies interval domains and single-dominance structure; Aziz et al. [3] study integral optimality under preference extensions. Targeted searches of scholarly literature and mathematical records, alongside accessible prior claim comparison, did not identify the present general implication. These searches are not a proof of priority, and an inaccessible or unindexed equivalent result remains possible. The three-approval separation itself is not claimed as new.

## Value — PASS

The theorem exactly identifies an unrestricted sparse-ballot class where the stronger, fractionally robust efficiency requirement is automatic at every integral Pareto optimum. Unlike interval restrictions, the class is described directly by ballot length and can fail the single-swap property previously used to prove equivalence. The sharp ballot-size boundary explains the first possible failure and gives a reusable structural exchange argument. This is an all-instance statement with an explicit tight boundary, not a numerical microcase.

## Scientific scope and remaining risks

The result is restricted to additive zero–one approvals and fixed committee cardinality. Source coverage relies on the accessible primary texts and targeted record searches; no independent audit or universal computer certification is asserted.
