# The exact square-window GCD of cobalancing numbers

## Finding
Define the cobalancing numbers by \(b_0=b_1=0\) and \(b_{n+2}=6b_{n+1}-b_n+2\). Define the balancing and Lucas-balancing sequences by \(B_0=0,B_1=1\) and \(C_0=1,C_1=3\), with homogeneous recurrence \(X_{n+2}=6X_{n+1}-X_n\). For every integer \(k\ge1\), the positive greatest common divisor of all square-window sums is
\[
D_b(k):=\gcd_{n\ge0}\sum_{i=1}^k b_{n+i}^2
=\begin{cases}
4\gcd(5k,B_k,C_k-1),&k\text{ odd},\\
2\gcd(5k,B_k,C_k-1),&k\text{ even}.
\end{cases}
\]
The formula gives a finite closed expression for every window length, including every exceptional prime. It completes the cobalancing-square instance of Question 30 of Mbirika, Schrader and Spilker (2023).

## Assumptions and scope
All indices are nonnegative integers. The GCD is the positive generator of the ideal generated by the displayed integers; zeros in the window-sum family are allowed. There is no restriction on the prime factors or parity of \(k\), beyond \(k\ge1\). The three recurrences above fix the normalization; notably \(b_0=b_1=0\), not a shifted variant.

## Proof
**1. Finite reduction and an exact coefficient identity.** Set \(W_k(n)=\sum_{i=1}^k b_{n+i}^2\). Write \(\lambda=3+2\sqrt2\), so that \(\lambda^{-1}=3-2\sqrt2\). The standard Binet identities are
\[
B_n=\frac{\lambda^n-\lambda^{-n}}{4\sqrt2},\qquad
C_n=\frac{\lambda^n+\lambda^{-n}}2,\qquad
C_n^2-8B_n^2=1.
\]
If \(P_0=0,P_1=1,P_{m+2}=2P_{m+1}+P_m\), then \(b_i=(P_{2i-1}-1)/2\) for \(i\ge1\). Direct Binet summation gives
\[
32W_k(n)=B_{2n+2k}-B_{2n}-16(B_{n+k}-B_n)+10k.\tag{1}
\]
The addition identities \(B_{u+v}=B_uC_v+C_uB_v\), \(C_{2k}-1=16B_k^2\), and \(B_{2k}=2B_kC_k\) turn (1) into
\[
32W_k(n)=2(5k)+2C_{2n}(B_kC_k)+16B_{2n}B_k^2
-16B_n(C_k-1)-16C_nB_k.\tag{2}
\]
In particular,
\[
16W_k(0)=5k+B_k(C_k-8).\tag{3}
\]
The sequence \(b_n^2\) is a linear combination of the five exponential modes \(1,\lambda^n,\lambda^{-n},\lambda^{2n},\lambda^{-2n}\), so \(W_k(n)\) satisfies the reversible integer recurrence whose characteristic polynomial is
\[
(T-1)(T^2-6T+1)(T^2-34T+1)
=T^5-41T^4+246T^3-246T^2+41T-1.
\]
Its constant coefficient is \(-1\); hence the GCD of all \(W_k(n)\) equals that of the first five \(W_k(0),\ldots,W_k(4)\). This uses an actual recurrence, not a finite-sampling assumption.

For \(n=0,1,2,3,4\), the coefficients in (2) form the fixed integer matrix
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}
2&2&0&0&-16\\
2&34&96&-16&-48\\
2&1154&3264&-96&-272\\
2&39202&110880&-560&-1584\\
2&1331714&3766656&-3264&-9232
\end{pmatrix},\quad
\det A=-2^{26}\cdot3\cdot7^2,
\]
acting on the column \((5k,B_kC_k,B_k^2,C_k-1,B_k)^\mathsf{T}\). Thus for every odd prime \(p\notin\{3,7\}\), \(A\) and \(32\) are invertible over \(\mathbb Z_p\), so
\[
\nu_p(D_b(k))=\min\{\nu_p(5k),\nu_p(B_k),\nu_p(C_k-1)\}.\tag{4}
\]
Here the minimum over \(B_kC_k,B_k^2,B_k\) is simply \(\nu_p(B_k)\). The cases \(p=3,7,2\) require separate arguments rather than discarding the determinant factors.

**2. The primes three and seven.** The residue sequences \(b_n^2\) have the respective least periods
\[
(b_n^2\bmod3)_{n\ge0}=(0,0,1,1)^\infty,
\qquad
(b_n^2\bmod7)_{n\ge0}=(0,0,4)^\infty.
\]
If every length-\(k\) window vanished modulo a prime, the difference of consecutive windows would force this residue sequence to have period \(k\). Consequently, \(3\mid D_b(k)\) can hold only if \(4\mid k\), and \(7\mid D_b(k)\) only if \(3\mid k\). For a whole number of these base periods the windows have values \(2(k/4)\bmod3\) and \(4(k/3)\bmod7\), respectively. Therefore
\[
3\mid D_b(k)\iff12\mid k,\qquad
7\mid D_b(k)\iff21\mid k.\tag{5}
\]
We now determine their full valuations. A standard Lucas lifting fact, justified below, states that if \(p\) is odd, \(p\mid B_t\), and \(C_t\equiv1\pmod p\), then
\[
\nu_p(B_{tm})=\nu_p(B_t)+\nu_p(m)\quad(m\ge1).\tag{6}
\]
Indeed put \(U=C_t+2\sqrt2B_t\) in the unramified quadratic \(p\)-adic algebra \(\mathbb Z_p[\sqrt2]\); it is a unit with inverse \(C_t-2\sqrt2B_t\). Since \(U^2\equiv1\pmod p\), the odd-prime binomial lifting identity applied to \((U^{2m}-1)/(U^2-1)\) yields valuation \(\nu_p(m)\); multiplying by the unit \(U^{1-m}\) gives \(B_{tm}/B_t\). This argument also applies when the quadratic algebra splits.

Since \(B_4=204\), \(C_4=577\), \(B_3=35\), and \(C_3=99\), identity (6) gives
\[
\nu_3(B_k)=1+\nu_3(k)\quad(4\mid k),\qquad
\nu_7(B_k)=1+\nu_7(k)\quad(3\mid k).\tag{7}
\]
In these cases \(C_k\equiv1\pmod p\); the norm identity \((C_k-1)(C_k+1)=8B_k^2\) therefore gives \(\nu_p(C_k-1)=2\nu_p(B_k)\). For \(12\mid k\) or \(21\mid k\) respectively, all five coordinates in (2) have \(p\)-adic valuation at least \(\nu_p(k)\). Hence every window is divisible by \(p^{\nu_p(k)}\) (because \(p\) is odd), while (3) gives \(\nu_p(W_k(0))=\nu_p(k)\): its \(5k\) term has exactly this valuation and the other term has strictly larger valuation. Combined with (5), this proves
\[
\nu_3(D_b(k))=\begin{cases}\nu_3(k),&12\mid k,\\0,&12\nmid k,\end{cases}
\quad
\nu_7(D_b(k))=\begin{cases}\nu_7(k),&21\mid k,\\0,&21\nmid k.\end{cases}\tag{8}
\]
These equal the local valuations of \(\gcd(5k,B_k,C_k-1)\).

**3. The prime two.** Every \(b_n\) is even. For odd \(k\), the sequence \(b_n^2/4\bmod2\) has least period four, with pattern \((0,0,1,1)\). If every length-\(k\) window of this sequence vanished, subtracting adjacent windows would force a period \(k\), impossible for odd \(k\). Hence
\[
\nu_2(D_b(k))=2\qquad(k\text{ odd}).\tag{9}
\]
Now let \(k\) be even and put \(r=\nu_2(k)\ge1\). The affine recurrence for \(b_n\) has shift matrix
\[
M=\begin{pmatrix}6&-1&2\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad M^2\equiv I\pmod2.
\]
Repeated squaring shows \(M^{2^r}\equiv I\pmod{2^r}\), so \(2^r\mid b_{n+k}-b_n\) for every \(n\). Because \(b_n+b_{n+k}\) is even, \(2^{r+1}\) divides the difference of their squares. Thus all length-\(k\) windows are congruent to \(W_k(0)\pmod{2^{r+1}}\).

To calculate that first window exactly, binomial expansion of \(3\pm2\sqrt2\) gives, for every even \(k\ge2\), congruences in the \(2\)-adic integers
\[
\frac{B_k}{k}\equiv 3^{k-1}+\frac83\binom{k-1}{2}3^{k-3}\pmod{64},\qquad
C_k\equiv3^k+8\binom{k}{2}3^{k-2}\pmod{64}.\tag{10}
\]
All omitted terms contain \(8^j/(2j+1)\) with \(j\ge2\), so are multiples of \(64\). Both right-hand sides in (10) depend only on even \(k\bmod16\), because \(3^{16}\equiv1\pmod{64}\) and the relevant binomial coefficients have period \(16\bmod8\). Evaluating the eight even residue classes shows
\[
\left(\frac{B_k}{k},C_k\right)\equiv
\begin{cases}(3,17)\pmod{64},&k\equiv2\pmod4,\\(51,1)\pmod{64},&4\mid k.\end{cases}
\]
In either case \(5+(B_k/k)(C_k-8)\equiv32\pmod{64}\). Equation (3) now implies \(\nu_2(W_k(0))=r+1\). The congruence of all windows then proves
\[
\nu_2(D_b(k))=\nu_2(k)+1\qquad(k\text{ even}).\tag{11}
\]
Finally, for even \(k\), \(\nu_2(B_k)=\nu_2(k)\) (also follows from the addition formulas or (10)), and \(\nu_2(C_k-1)\ge\nu_2(k)\); hence \(\nu_2\gcd(5k,B_k,C_k-1)=\nu_2(k)\). For odd \(k\) that GCD is odd. Combining (4), (8), (9), and (11) establishes the theorem. \(\square\)

## Verification
The included standard-library script `verify.py` recomputes the five-row coefficient matrix and its exact determinant, checks the window identities and reversible recurrence, checks the local residue patterns, verifies the finite modulo-\(64\) reductions, and compares both sides of the theorem for 250 window lengths using independently accumulated square sums. Run `python3 verify.py`. Its successful execution yields `VERIFY_OK lengths=250 formula_positions=1750 matrix_det=-2^26*3*7^2 recurrence_terms=299 local_cycles=3,7,2 normalized_even_lengths=200`. These checks do not replace the proof for infinitely many lengths.

## Relationship to prior work
Mbirika, Schrader and Spilker (2023), Section 6.1, Question 30, ask for closed expressions for four square-window GCD families. For cobalancing numbers they report the observation that the GCD is divisible by four, not a complete formula. The present theorem gives the exact GCD at every length and recovers that divisibility. A related partial local-prime computation for primes at least five, excluding seven, does not determine the powers of two, three, or seven; the result here resolves all three and unifies them with the other primes. The Pell sum-of-consecutive-terms work of Anand et al. (2024, arXiv version of July 13) treats fixed-multiple identities for sums of first powers, not square-window GCDs.

## Limitations
The theorem concerns the specified cobalancing initial conditions and squares only, not arbitrary affine Pell-type recurrences or higher powers. The prime-adic lifting step requires odd primes and is not used at two. Bibliographic priority beyond the inspected materials is not certified. The finite verification is an error-detection aid, not independent validation of the infinite theorem.
