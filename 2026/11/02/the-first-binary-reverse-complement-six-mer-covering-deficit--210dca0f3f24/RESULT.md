# The first binary reverse-complement six-mer covering deficit

## Finding

Let \(\Sigma=\{0,1\}\), with complementation \(0\leftrightarrow1\), and let \(\rho_k\) reverse and complement a word of length \(k\). A finite *linear* word is \(k\)-RC-complete if, for every \(u\in\Sigma^k\), at least one of \(u\) and \(\rho_k(u)\) occurs as a contiguous factor, without wrapping around its ends. Let \(N_k\) be the smallest length of such a word.

**Theorem.** For even orders \(k\in\{2,4,6\}\), the exact minimum lengths are
\[
(N_2,N_4,N_6)=(4,13,44).
\]
In particular, the naive orbit-count lower bound is attained at orders \(2\) and \(4\) but **first fails at even order \(6\)**: the \(36\) reverse-complement classes of six-bit words require at least \(39\) successive six-bit windows. An explicit word of length \(44\) attains that bound.

## Assumptions and scope

Words are linear, not circular; all factors are consecutive and counted with their actual multiplicities. The complement is the fixed-point-free involution on the two-letter alphabet, not the usual four-letter DNA alphabet. There are no constraints on repeated factors, word balance, or starting/ending bits. For a word of length \(n\ge k\), the number of length-\(k\) windows (including repetitions) is exactly \(n-k+1\).

The reverse-complement involution has \(2^{k/2}\) fixed words when \(k\) is even. Thus, by elementary orbit counting, there are
\[
M_k=\frac{2^k+2^{k/2}}2
\]
classes for such \(k\), and every \(k\)-RC-complete word has at least \(M_k+k-1\) symbols. At orders \(2,4,6\), these orbit counts are respectively \(3,10,36\).

## Proof

**The two smaller orders.** The words `0010` and `0010100001100` cover all reverse-complement classes of two-bit and four-bit factors, respectively. They have respectively \(3\) and \(10\) windows, equal to the orbit counts. Hence \(N_2=4\) and \(N_4=13\).

**A six-mer potential.** Consider the following sets of five-bit states:

\[
\begin{aligned}
A&=\{00011,00101,10011,11010,11100\},\\
B&=\{00110,00111,01011,10100,11000\}=\rho_5(A).
\end{aligned}
\]

They are disjoint. Set \(\phi(v)=2\) on \(A\), \(\phi(v)=0\) on \(B\), and \(\phi(v)=1\) at all other five-bit states. In particular, \(\phi(\rho_5(v))=2-\phi(v)\).

For each six-bit word \(e=e_1\cdots e_6\), put
\[
c(e)=1+\phi(e_1\cdots e_5)-\phi(e_2\cdots e_6).
\]
Every \(c(e)\) is nonnegative: the only way the difference could be less than \(-1\) would be a shift from a state in \(B\) to one in \(A\), and none of the ten possible one-symbol shifts out of \(B\) ends in \(A\). Indeed, the successor pairs of the five states in \(B\), in the order displayed, are

| State | Two possible successors |
|---|---|
| `00110` | `01100`, `01101` |
| `00111` | `01110`, `01111` |
| `01011` | `10110`, `10111` |
| `10100` | `01000`, `01001` |
| `11000` | `10000`, `10001` |

Because the reverse complement exchanges the prefix and suffix under \(\rho_5\), the displayed antisymmetry of \(\phi\) gives \(c(\rho_6(e))=c(e)\). Therefore \(c\) is a well-defined nonnegative weight of each reverse-complement class.

Across all \(64\) six-bit words, the weight sum is \(64\), since each five-bit state occurs exactly twice as a prefix and exactly twice as a suffix. There are exactly eight reverse-complement-fixed six-bit words, namely

`000111`, `001011`, `010101`, `011001`, `100110`, `101010`, `110100`, `111000`.

For five of them the five-bit prefix lies in \(A\), so its weight is \(3\); the other three have weight \(1\). Hence the sum of the eight fixed-word weights is \(5\cdot3+3\cdot1=18\). The other \(56\) words occur in \(28\) two-element reverse-complement classes, with equal weights within each class. Thus the total weight, counting **each of the \(36\) classes once**, is
\[
\sum_{C\in\Sigma^6/\rho_6}c(C)=\frac{64+18}{2}=41.
\]

Now consider any six-RC-complete word \(w\), with \(L\) consecutive six-bit windows \(e_1,\ldots,e_L\). Each of the \(36\) classes appears at least once, so nonnegativity implies
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{L}c(e_i)\ge41.
\]
But consecutive five-bit states telescope, giving
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{L}c(e_i)
=L+\phi(\operatorname{pref}_5(w))
-\phi(\operatorname{suff}_5(w))\le L+2.
\]
Consequently \(L\ge39\), or equivalently \(|w|\ge44\).

**A matching upper bound.** The following \(44\)-bit linear word meets every six-bit reverse-complement class:

`00101111110111000110010011000011101010110100`

It has \(39\) six-bit windows and hits all \(36\) classes: \(33\) classes occur once, while `001100`, `001110`, and `011100` occur twice (canonical lexicographically smaller representatives of their classes). These three repeated classes all have weight zero. The first five bits have potential \(2\) and the last five potential \(0\), so the upper bound saturates every inequality above. The complete class count is independently regenerated by `verify.py`. This proves \(N_6=44\) and the theorem.

## Verification

Run `python3 verify.py` from the same directory as this file; it uses only the Python standard library. It checks the complement involution, the complete set of \(36\) classes, the five-bit potential, all \(64\) six-mer costs, the eight fixed classes and their total weight, the witness's \(39\) windows, and independent string and integer implementations of the reverse-complement orbit map. It also enumerates the \(2\)- and \(4\)-mer small-order equality cases directly. A successful run prints `VERIFY_OK`.

The lower bound is an argument for *all* binary linear words, not an inference from finite enumeration or a solver log. The verification program checks the small finite certificate used in that argument.

## Relationship to prior work

Orenstein and Shamir (2013) introduced the reverse-complement-complete sequence problem in the context of double-stranded DNA probes and provided general optimal algorithms, including a matching-based method for even orders. Their reported numerical table concerns the **four-letter DNA alphabet** and does not report the binary six-mer minimum or a two-valued-exception potential certificate. Their general algorithm is stronger in algorithmic scope; it is not claimed as new here. The exact binary threshold and its elementary, fully displayed dual certificate are the claims established here.

Medvedev and collaborators (2007), as well as Medvedev and Brudno (2009), developed bidirected graph methods for minimum double-stranded sequence assembly. These more general optimization frameworks do not furnish the specific stated binary value, short certificate or first-even-order boundary in the material examined. Existing general algorithms can in principle compute special cases; this does not establish that a particular numerical instance or its compact proof has historical priority.

## Limitations

The exact minimum and first-failure statement are confined to the **binary** reverse-complement problem at even orders through six. They do not assert a formula for all even orders, a result for four-letter DNA sequences, circular superstrings, multiple probes, or gapped factors. The proof has a complete finite certificate for the order-six bound, but independent specialist validation and exhaustive historical-priority clearance have not been performed. General algorithmic treatment of the problem already existed before this result.
