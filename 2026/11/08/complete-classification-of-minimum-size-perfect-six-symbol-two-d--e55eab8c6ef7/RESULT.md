# Complete classification of minimum-size perfect six-symbol two-deletion codes

## Finding
Let \(\Sigma=\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}\). For \(w\in\Sigma^4\), let \(D_2(w)\) be the **set** of distinct length-two subsequences of \(w\), in their original order, obtained by deleting exactly two positions. A code \(C\subseteq\Sigma^4\) is perfect if the sets \(D_2(w)\), \(w\in C\), partition \(\Sigma^2\). Such a code has at least six words, and six-word perfect codes are known to exist [2].

**Classification theorem.** There are exactly \(240\) distinct labeled perfect six-word codes. Under simultaneous permutation of the six alphabet symbols, they form exactly **two orbits of size \(120\)**. Reversing all codewords interchanges the two orbits.

Every such code is obtained uniquely by the following recipe. Choose a perfect matching of the six symbols into three unordered pairs; orient a three-cycle on these three pairs; for each source pair \(\{a,a'\}\) in that cycle, let \(\{b,c\}\) be its successor pair and include the two words \((a,b,c,a)\) and \((a',c,b,a')\), with an independently chosen orientation of \(\{b,c\}\). Thus the count is \(15\cdot2\cdot2^3=240\). The two orbits are distinguished by the parity of the three orientation bits, defined relative to any ordered presentation of the matched pairs along their directed cycle.

For example, one perfect code is

```text
0230 1321 2452 3543 4014 5105
```

The novelty asserted here is the complete structural and orbit classification, **not** the existence or minimum size of these codes, which are established in earlier literature.

## Assumptions and scope
All six symbols are labeled, all four word coordinates retain their order, and deletion is subsequence deletion rather than removal of a contiguous block. Two codes are identified under alphabet relabeling only when a single permutation of \(\Sigma\) is applied to every coordinate. Codeword order within a code is irrelevant. Word reversal is discussed as a separate transformation, not included in the alphabet-relabeling group.

## Proof
**1. Size and word shape.** For any \(w\in\Sigma^4\), there are six position pairs, so \(|D_2(w)|\le6\). As \(|\Sigma^2|=36\), every perfect code has at least six words. If a perfect code has six words, every ball must contain six distinct pairs. Each diagonal pair \((a,a)\) occurs in exactly one of its six balls. A word of length four with a repeated symbol and six distinct ordered two-subsequences must be \((a,b,c,a)\), with \(a,b,c\) pairwise distinct: if the repeated positions differ from \(\{1,4\}\), at least two of the six position pairs produce the same ordered pair; additional repetitions also force collisions. Such a word contains exactly the diagonal \((a,a)\). Hence the six words are uniquely indexed by their first and last symbol \(a\in\Sigma\).

**2. Undirected-edge decomposition.** The off-diagonal outputs of \((a,b,c,a)\) are the five ordered pairs \((a,b),(b,a),(a,c),(c,a),(b,c)\). Call \(\{a,b\}\) and \(\{a,c\}\) *spoke edges* and \(\{b,c\}\) its *single edge*. Each spoke edge supplies both orientations, whereas a single edge supplies only the orientation \(b\to c\). Spoke edges cannot repeat, and no single edge can also be a spoke edge, since either would repeat an ordered output. There are exactly twelve distinct spoke edges among the fifteen edges of \(K_6\). Consequently the remaining three edges form a simple graph \(M\) whose edges are each single edges in exactly two words, in opposite orientations. In particular, every word at center \(a\) chooses an edge \(\{b,c\}\) of \(M\) disjoint from \(a\), and neither \(\{a,b\}\) nor \(\{a,c\}\) may belong to \(M\).

**3. The three missing edges form a matching.** The possible graph types of a simple three-edge graph are \(3K_2\), \(P_3\sqcup K_2\), \(P_4\), \(K_{1,3}\), and \(K_3\), with any necessary isolated vertices. In \(P_4\), \(K_{1,3}\), or \(K_3\), choose respectively an internal vertex, the star center, or any triangle vertex. No edge of \(M\) lies outside that vertex and all its neighbors in \(M\), so this vertex cannot select any valid single edge, a contradiction. In \(P_3\sqcup K_2\), label the two adjacent edges \(\{u,v\}\), \(\{v,w\}\). Each must be chosen as the single edge by two centers. A valid center for either one cannot be \(u,v,w\): the center cannot belong to the edge and cannot have a missing spoke to one of its endpoints. Only the two endpoints of the disjoint \(K_2\) and the sole isolated vertex remain, providing only three centers for the required four distinct choices. Hence \(M\cong3K_2\).

**4. Forced directed cycle.** Contract the three edges of \(M\) to three groups of size two. A center in one group must select one of the other two whole groups as the endpoints of its single edge. Between two distinct groups, the four cross-edges must each be assigned as a spoke to exactly one endpoint. A center selecting the other group assigns two of these cross-edges. Thus precisely two centers across the two groups must make this selection. They cannot consist of one center from each group: the two stars then overlap on one cross-edge and omit another. Therefore the two centers in one group both select the other group, and neither center in the other group selects back. These choices orient every pair of groups as a tournament, with outdegree one at each group. The tournament is necessarily a directed three-cycle. For each source group, its two centers must choose opposite orders of the target pair to cover both orientations of the corresponding missing edge. This proves the recipe, including its converse: the recipe covers each diagonal once, each matching-pair orientation once, and each cross-pair orientation once.

**5. Enumeration and inequivalent orbits.** There are \(15\) perfect matchings on six labeled vertices, two orientations of a three-cycle on the three matched groups, and two choices for each of the three opposing internal orders. The matching, cycle, and orders are all reconstructible from the code, so this counts \(15\cdot2\cdot2^3=240\) distinct codes.

To analyze the action of alphabet permutations, fix an ordered cyclic presentation \(G_i=\{a_i^0,a_i^1\}\) for \(i\in\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z\) with arrows \(G_i\to G_{i+1}\). Let \(\varepsilon_i\in\{0,1\}\) encode whether the word centered at \(a_i^0\) uses \((a_{i+1}^0,a_{i+1}^1)\) or its reverse internally. Switching the names \(a_i^0,a_i^1\) simultaneously flips \(\varepsilon_i\) and \(\varepsilon_{i-1}\), so the parity \(\varepsilon_0+\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2\pmod2\) is invariant. Cyclically rotating the three groups also preserves parity. Conversely, swaps of names within groups generate all even-parity changes, hence the eight choices of orientation bits split into exactly two orbits of four for a fixed matching and directed cycle. Since alphabet relabeling acts transitively on the \(15\cdot2=30\) matching-cycle pairs, there are exactly two global orbits, each with \(30\cdot4=120\) codes. Reversal of every word exchanges \((a,b,c,a)\) with \((a,c,b,a)\), toggles all three bits, and therefore swaps the orbits.

## Verification
Run the standalone `verify.py` with Python 3. The program uses all six ordered position pairs directly to form each descendant set, enumerates the \(20\) feasible maximal-ball words for each diagonal center, and performs exact-cover backtracking without assuming the matching-cycle classification. It finds exactly \(240\) six-word perfect codes. It then applies all \(720\) alphabet relabelings to an explicit example, finding an orbit of \(120\) codes, and verifies that reversal gives a disjoint orbit of another \(120\) codes. These finite checks corroborate the independent structural proof, which establishes the theorem without relying on code execution.

## Relationship to prior work
Chee, Ge, and Ling [2, §7.2, Table 7.1] explicitly give a perfect six-word \((4,2)_6\) deletion-correcting code and determine the size spectrum, including the already-known minimum six; Wei and Ge [3, Theorem 1.4] confirm the same size spectrum. Those results establish **existence and possible cardinalities**, whereas the statement above classifies *every* size-six realization, counts them, and determines the two alphabet-relabeling classes. Xie, Sun, and Ge [1, §V] study deletion covering through asymptotic hypergraph methods and provide recent motivation for understanding exact small-alphabet partitions; their general covering results neither require disjoint balls nor imply this finite orbit structure.

## Limitations
This classification is only for six alphabet symbols, four source coordinates, two arbitrary subsequence deletions, and perfect codes of the already-known minimum size six. It makes no claim for nonperfect covers, other alphabet sizes, other deletion radii, or independently verified historical priority. The size-six existence and example are prior results; these are not presented as new. Independent mathematical audit has not been performed.
