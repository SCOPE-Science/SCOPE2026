# Every nonconstant independent stable timestep destabilizes velocity Verlet almost surely

## Finding

Apply velocity Verlet to the *unit harmonic oscillator* \(\ddot q=-q\). Choose the successive timesteps independently from **any common probability distribution** \(\mu\) supported in \((0,2)\). For \(N\ge1\), let \(P_N=M(H_N)\cdots M(H_1)\), with the phase-space step map defined below. Then a deterministic number \(\lambda(\mu)\) exists such that

\[
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\|P_N\|_2=\lambda(\mu)>0\quad\text{almost surely (non-point-mass law)}.
\]

For non-point-mass laws, writing \(T_N=\sum_{j=1}^N H_j\), the same trajectory has the positive physical-time growth exponent

\[
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{T_N}\log\|P_N\|_2
=\frac{\lambda(\mu)}{\mathbb E[H_1]}>0\quad\text{almost surely}.
\]

Conversely, if \(\mu\) is a point mass at any admissible fixed timestep, both exponents vanish. Thus *every nonconstant independent timestep distribution supported inside the fixed-step stability interval has a strictly positive top Lyapunov exponent*, without exceptions, no matter how small its fluctuations are. In particular the result holds for every distinct two-point support with arbitrary positive selection probabilities.

More strongly, deterministic periodic alternation need not be unstable: the pair \(a=1\), \(b=6/5\) has \(\operatorname{tr}(M(a)M(b))=-347/250\), so this fixed two-step product is elliptic with bounded powers, although independent nondegenerate random selection from the same pair has the positive exponent above.

## Assumptions and scope

The oscillator is a *deterministic* second-order ordinary differential equation, not an oscillator with randomly varying physical stiffness or external forcing. The initial phase-space state is \((q,v)\in\mathbb R^2\). A step of velocity Verlet with timestep \(h\) updates the momentum by a half kick, position by a drift, then momentum by the remaining half kick:

\[
 v_{1/2}=v-\frac h2q,\qquad q^+=q+h v_{1/2},\qquad v^+=v_{1/2}-\frac h2q^+.
\]

Consequently

\[
\begin{pmatrix}q^+\\v^+\end{pmatrix}=
M(h)\begin{pmatrix}q\\v\end{pmatrix},\qquad
M(h)=\begin{pmatrix}
1-h^2/2&h\\ -h(1-h^2/4)&1-h^2/2
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

The independent identically distributed timesteps are exogenous (not state-dependent), with **any Borel probability law carried by** \((0,2)\). Such laws automatically have finite matrix logarithmic moments because \(M(h)\) and its inverse are uniformly bounded on \(0<h<2\). The norm is the Euclidean operator norm. The theorem concerns the random product's top norm exponent, not a deterministic upper bound for every realization or a quantitative lower bound for the exponent. A point-mass law is the only zero-exponent case.

## Proof

**1. Every fixed admissible step is elliptic.** Direct calculation gives \(\det M(h)=1\) and \(\operatorname{tr}M(h)=2-h^2\), whose absolute value is strictly below \(2\) for \(0<h<2\). Moreover, defining \(G_h=\operatorname{diag}(1-h^2/4,1)\) gives the exact identity \(M(h)^\top G_hM(h)=G_h\). Since \(G_h\) is positive definite, powers of each individual \(M(h)\) are uniformly bounded. This proves the zero-exponent cases when the law is supported at one value.

**2. Noncompactness for every nonconstant law.** A non-point-mass distribution \(\mu\) supported in \((0,2)\) has two distinct values \(a,b\) in its topological support. Set \(A=M(a)\) and \(B=M(b)\). Expanding these displayed two-by-two matrices yields the *exact* identity

\[
\operatorname{tr}(ABA^{-1}B^{-1})
=2+\frac{a^2b^2(a-b)^2(a+b)^2}{16}.
\]

For distinct \(a,b>0\), the right side is strictly greater than \(2\). Hence the commutator is a hyperbolic element of \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb R)\) with an eigenvalue of modulus greater than one. Its powers are unbounded. The group generated by \(A,B\) is therefore noncompact. Since that group is a subgroup of the one generated by the support of the complete law, the latter is noncompact as well.

**3. No finite invariant set of directions.** Suppose the projective action of the **two-point subgroup** generated by \(A,B\) preserved a nonempty finite set \(F\) of real lines. If \(|F|=1\), a common real eigenline would exist, impossible since both \(A,B\) have traces strictly between \(-2\) and \(2\). If \(|F|=2\), each of \(A,B\) must interchange its two lines (neither matrix has a real eigenline). Thus both squares fix the two lines. Because the squares of our elliptic matrices cannot have two real eigenlines unless scalar, Cayley–Hamilton forces \(\operatorname{tr}A=\operatorname{tr}B=0\), i.e. \(a=b=\sqrt2\), contradicting distinctness. If \(|F|\ge3\), the action on \(F\) embeds the generated projective group into the finite permutation group on \(F\): a nonidentity projective linear transformation cannot fix three different lines. This makes every projective group element finite order, contradicting the hyperbolic commutator of Step 2. Therefore the two-point subgroup, and hence the group generated by the entire support of \(\mu\), is *totally irreducible* (no invariant finite projective subset).

**4. Invoke the positive-exponent random-product theorem.** The distribution of \(M(H_1)\) has finite logarithmic moment: the entries of \(M(h)\) and \(M(h)^{-1}\) remain uniformly bounded for \(0<h<2\). By Steps 2 and 3 its support generates a noncompact, totally irreducible subgroup. Furstenberg's positive Lyapunov-exponent theorem for independent \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb R)\) products therefore yields the existence and strict positivity of the almost-sure limit \(\lambda(\mu)\). This is a standard infinite-product theorem, not a conclusion inferred from finite matrix tests. The strong law gives \(T_N/N\to\mathbb E[H_1]>0\) almost surely, proving the physical-time version. The point-mass case was treated in Step 1.

**5. A stable periodic contrast.** At \(a=1\) and \(b=6/5\), the product \(M(a)M(b)\) has unit determinant and trace \(-347/250\in(-2,2)\). This makes the product elliptic with bounded powers; adding one partial-period factor preserves boundedness of the alternating trajectory. It does not contradict Steps 2–4: that conclusion concerns independent timestep draws, which almost surely sample many more words than one deterministic alternation.

## Relationship to prior work

The general Furstenberg positivity criterion is classical and is not claimed as a new theorem. Its noncompactness and strong irreducibility hypotheses, however, require proof for this exact velocity-Verlet family for *every* pair of distinct stable timesteps, and thereby for every nonconstant supported law; here the explicit hyperbolic commutator and finite-projective-orbit exclusion supply that proof without exceptional parameter cases.

Piché studies parametric instability for Runge–Kutta–Nyström methods, including the Störmer/Verlet context, through *periodic* timestep modulation and stability charts. Richardson and Finn analyze prescribed periodic and state-dependent variable timesteps and methods for avoiding resonance; their relevant results do not establish the almost-sure result under every non-point-mass independent step law. Sharma and collaborators study adaptive energy-preserving variational integrators, a different construction. Published analyses of symplectic Euler with random steps and of random-shear products concern different step matrices or hypotheses; mean-square amplification is not the same as a positive almost-sure exponent for this second-order method. Random physical-frequency harmonic oscillators are also distinct from discretizing a fixed-frequency oscillator with random timesteps.

## Limitations

No closed-form value, uniform quantitative lower bound, or small-jitter expansion for \(\lambda(\mu)\) is provided. The theorem is not asserted for correlated, state-adaptive, or deterministically selected steps, for laws assigning mass to steps at or outside \((0,2)\), or for multidimensional nonlinear mechanics. The result is an operator-norm almost-sure instability statement; it does not assert deterministic exponential growth along *every* initial vector on every sample path. Standard random-matrix positivity is used as a stated external theorem; its general proof is not reproduced here. An earlier equivalent specialist result on random-step Verlet remains a residual literature risk.
