# Scientific review

## Correctness — PASS

The polyhedron is defined by exact integer vertices and its 14 supporting inequalities. An exhaustive 36-edge crossing count gives the section type in all five connected normal regions because the only possible interior signed-vertex zero sets are the three identified straight lines. The finite witness classifications therefore cover infinitely many orientations without treating sampling as proof. Three direct spherical-triangle area evaluations, after the explicit factor of 48 for signed permutations, prove the normalized Haar law; exceptional planes have zero measure. The exact-rational tests and independent quadratures corroborate critical computations. The theorem makes no claim about random offsets.

## Originality — PASS, with residual historical risk

The closest geometric source actually read was Socha's 1988 analysis of **angle** distributions of random plane sections, including a truncated-octahedron-type structure. Its analysis and sample-space conventions do not yield the present exact central-section vertex-count frequencies. The full 2011 Ferraro–Zaninetti article was inspected and computes Poisson–Voronoi radius/area distributions under a sphere approximation, not the three polygon-type probabilities. Paul's 1981 numerical **area** histograms for truncated octahedra are plausibly relevant but its full article could not be inspected; it is explicitly retained as a material priority risk rather than treated as disproved coverage. The 1995 Ohser–Mücklich abstract concerns stereological inverse size/shape problems for random polyhedra. Related exact central-section laws of other bodies do not specialize to the present polyhedron. No equivalent or dominating theorem was identified in the inspected accessible sources. Search failure does not prove historical uniqueness; independent priority assessment is still needed.

## Mathematical value — PASS

The Kelvin truncated octahedron is a natural space-filling Voronoi polyhedron with a nontrivial mixed distribution of three generic central-section types. The result gives the **complete exact law**, not merely a simulation or an isolated event, and shows how section complexity is encoded by a small normal-fan subdivision. It is a motivated exact finite classification in geometric probability, useful as a reference case and a check for stereological computations. Generalization, inference under noisy orientation samples, and noncentral sections are not established.
