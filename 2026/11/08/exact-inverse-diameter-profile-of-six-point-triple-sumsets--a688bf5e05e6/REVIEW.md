# Scientific review

## Correctness — PASS
The precise quantifiers are all six-element integer sets and all forty attainable triple-sumset cardinalities. Translation to minimum zero is bijective on translation classes, and the search covers exactly \(\binom{45}{5}=1,221,759\) normalized sets of diameter at most \(45\). Two independently implemented exact algorithms enumerate all these sets, count every allowed repeated-summand triple, and agree on every minimum and every extremizer. Rajagopal's published Theorem 1.7, not finite experimentation, excludes cardinality \(17\) globally. The notation \(N(3,6)=46\) explicitly uses the [0,N−1] interval-length convention of the compression paper. The finite-search computation is decisive for the exact small-parameter claim but is not a general infinite-domain theorem for other parameters.

## Originality — PASS (qualified)
Nathanson's *Compression and complexity for sumset sizes in additive number theory* defines the least inclusive-interval length and proves a broad exponential bound but does not give the forty sharp minima at six points. Rajagopal's *Possible Sizes of Sumsets* gives the globally possible cardinalities but not the least diameter at each size. Direct claim-level comparison, semantic research record searches using inverse-spectrum and \(B_3\) aliases, exact-data searches, and accessible earlier result claims found no result implying the entire profile. An earlier optimal \(B_3\) table could independently contain the extreme \(56\)-sum examples; it would not by itself settle all forty minimum diameters. Unindexed equivalent tables remain a residual historical-priority risk.

## Mathematical value — PASS
The classification computes a natural exact inverse problem of Nathanson's compression programme rather than arbitrarily choosing a numerical slice: it determines the optimal finite interval length \(N(3,6)\), all forty sharp thresholds for each attainable size, all minimum-diameter full-\(56\)-sum extremizers, and the non-monotone sharp thresholds \(m(40)>m(41)\). The complete spectrum requires roughly 1.22 million normalized configurations and is more informative than reproducing an isolated known \(B_3\) example or giving a nonsharp upper bound.
