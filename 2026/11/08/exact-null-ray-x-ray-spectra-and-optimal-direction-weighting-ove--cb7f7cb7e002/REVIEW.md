# Scientific review of the null-direction finite-field X-ray theorem

## Correctness
**PASS (bounded self-review).** The normal operator is diagonal under additive Fourier characters, with eigenvalue \(q\) times the number of projective null directions perpendicular to the frequency. The proof explicitly accounts for anisotropic versus isotropic frequency hyperplanes, removes a hyperbolic plane in the latter case, and uses quadratic Gauss sums for all finite fields of odd order. Resulting formulas include correct zero-frequency normalization and positive multiplicities summing to the full dimension. Explicit exhaustive checks across \(\mathbb F_3\), \(\mathbb F_5\), and \(\mathbb F_9\) matched the predicted counts and two directly formed normal-matrix spectra; the optimized-weighting inequality also follows from orthogonal-group averaging. Finite checks are corroboration, not proof for all parameters.

## Originality
**PASS (bounded comparison, not historical priority certification).** Koh's full 2012 paper addresses unrestricted finite-field X-ray/Radon mapping estimates, not the null-direction Fourier singular spectrum. Feldman–Grinberg's projective X-ray admissibility result studies a different point/line incidence map. The closest accessible public result on sharp scalar-view weighting addresses projective cyclic square grids, not quadratic null directions of affine finite-field vector spaces; the available description does not imply the present Witt-dependent spectrum. Public searches for null-line tomography and exact singular values did not locate the same claim. The uninspected general finite Radon literature could contain an equivalent special case; this residual risk prevents unconditional historical priority assertions.

## Mathematical value
**PASS.** Quadratically restricted viewing directions are a natural finite analogue of light-ray tomography. The result gives the exact nullspace, full singular values and multiplicities, a dimension-three injectivity obstruction, and a dimension-four split/nonsplit stability separation of orders \(\sqrt q\) versus \(q\). It additionally settles the natural question whether changing scalar exposure by direction repairs the bad conditioning: it cannot. These are structural inverse-problem facts for an infinite family of fields and dimensions, not a small-field census.
