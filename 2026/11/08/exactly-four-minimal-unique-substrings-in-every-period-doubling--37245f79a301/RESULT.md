# Exactly four minimal unique substrings in every period-doubling word

## Finding
Let \(D_0=0\), and for integers \(k\geq0\) let \(D_{k+1}=\varphi(D_k)\), with \(\varphi(0)=01\) and \(\varphi(1)=00\). For **every** integer \(k\geq3\), put \(m=2^{k-2}\). Using zero-based starting indices, the **complete list of minimal unique substring occurrences** in \(D_k\) is
\[
\left\{(m-1,m+1),\;\left(\frac{3m}{2}-1,m+1\right),\;(2m-1,m+1),\;(3m-1,m+1)\right\},
\]
where each ordered pair specifies (start, length). Thus there are exactly four minimal unique substrings, and all four have the same length \(m+1\). This is a classification of occurrences, not merely distinct factor values.

## Assumptions and scope
A *factor occurrence* of a finite word \(W\) is a contiguous interval of positions in \(W\). A factor is *unique* if it occurs at exactly one starting position. A *minimal unique substring* (MUS) is a unique factor every proper factor of which occurs at least twice. It suffices to check its longest proper prefix and suffix. All factors are ordinary noncyclic factors, and the empty word is not included. Indices are zero-based throughout. The theorem concerns the finite iteration \(D_k\), not an arbitrary-length prefix of the infinite fixed point.

## Proof
**Step 1 (four-block decomposition).** Write \(A=D_{k-2}\), \(C=D_{k-3}\), and \(m=|A|\), so \(|C|=m/2\). Denote by \(X^*\) the word obtained by flipping only the last bit of the nonempty word \(X\). The substitution recursion gives \(D_{h+1}=D_h D_{h-1}D_{h-1}\) for \(h\geq1\): indeed \(\varphi^h(1)=\varphi^{h-1}(00)=D_{h-1}D_{h-1}\). Since \(D_h=D_{h-1}D_{h-2}D_{h-2}\) for \(h\geq2\), comparison shows \(D_h\) and \(D_{h-1}D_{h-1}\) agree at all positions but the last, a fact also proved directly by induction beginning at \(D_1=01\) and \(D_0D_0=00\). Consequently \(A=C C^*\) and \(D_k=A B A A\), where \(B=C C\). In particular, \(A\) and \(B\) agree everywhere except at their last bit.

**Step 2 (distinct cyclic shifts).** Every \(D_h\) for \(h\geq1\) contains an odd number of ones. This holds at \(h=1\), and the recurrence for the number of ones is \(o_{h+1}=2^h-o_h\), preserving oddness. A word of power-of-two length with an odd number of ones cannot be a nontrivial integral power, so \(A\) is primitive. Likewise \(C\) is primitive (for \(k=3\), \(|C|=1\)); hence the \(m/2\) rotations of \(C\) are distinct. Moreover, \(A\) has one more or one fewer one than \(B=C C\), so no rotation of \(A\) equals a rotation of \(B\).

**Step 3 (all factors of length \(m\) repeat).** Every start \(i\) of a length-\(m\) factor belongs to one of the following classes. Starts \(i=r\) and \(i=2m+r\), for \(0\leq r<m\), produce the same length-\(m\) rotation of \(A\); the start \(i=3m\) also produces \(A\). This holds because the prefix of \(B\) of length less than \(m\) agrees with that of \(A\). Starts \(i=m+r\), for \(0\leq r<m\), produce rotations of \(B\). These are equal in exactly the pairs \(r\) and \(r+m/2\) with \(0\leq r<m/2\), because \(B=C C\) with \(C\) primitive. The two classes are disjoint by their differing numbers of ones. Consequently every length-\(m\) factor has at least two occurrences, and therefore so does every shorter factor.

**Step 4 (the four unique factors of length \(m+1\)).** In the \(A\)-rotation class, compare the two starts \(r\) and \(2m+r\). Their next letters beyond length \(m\) are respectively \(B[r]\) and \(A[r]\), equal for \(0\leq r\leq m-2\), unequal only for \(r=m-1\). Thus precisely the starts \(m-1\) and \(3m-1\) give unique length-\(m+1\) factors of this class. In the \(B\)-rotation class, compare starts \(m+r\) and \(3m/2+r\) for \(0\leq r<m/2\). Their next letters are \(A[r]\) and \(A[m/2+r]\). Since \(A=C C^*\), these are equal except at \(r=m/2-1\), where they differ. This gives precisely the starts \(3m/2-1\) and \(2m-1\). No cross-class coincidence is possible already at length \(m\). Thus there are exactly four unique factors of length \(m+1\); because all their length-\(m\) prefixes and suffixes repeat, all are MUSs.

**Step 5 (exhaustion at all larger lengths).** For each repeated length-\(m+1\) factor there is a companion occurrence given in Step 4. The longest common prefix of the words starting at the two \(A\)-class positions \(r\) and \(2m+r\), when \(0\leq r\leq m-2\), has exactly \(2m-r-1\) letters. Indeed, the two starts read the common cyclic pattern up to the last letter of \(B\) versus the last letter of \(A\); these letters differ. At that first disagreement, the extended factor starting at \(r\) contains the unique length-\(m+1\) factor starting at \(m-1\); the extended factor starting at \(2m+r\) contains the one starting at \(3m-1\). Both companions have room for their first disagreement, so finite-end truncation cannot create a further MUS.

For the two \(B\)-class starts \(m+r\) and \(3m/2+r\), with \(0\leq r\leq m/2-2\), their longest common prefix has exactly \(3m/2-r-1\) letters: the first disagreement occurs when the first occurrence reaches the last letter of the middle \(C\) block and the other reaches the corresponding last letter of \(C^*\). The first differing extension contains the length-\(m+1\) MUS starting at \(3m/2-1\), or respectively at \(2m-1\). Again, this difference is reached before the later companion ends. Therefore a factor longer than \(m+1\) which does not contain one of the four listed MUS occurrences has a matching companion and is not unique. Every unique longer factor contains one of the four listed MUSs as a proper subfactor, so cannot itself be minimal. This proves completeness for all \(k\geq3\). \(\square\)

## Verification
The standalone `verify_period_doubling_mus.py` regenerates the substitution words for \(3\leq k\leq10\), constructs all suffix-pair longest-common-prefix values, independently determines every minimal unique occurrence, and compares them with the theorem. It also checks the four-block decomposition and both exact companion-prefix lengths. All eight levels passed. These finite experiments are corroboration; Steps 1–5 prove the result for all orders.

## Relationship to prior work
Banbara, Bannai, Guo, Köppl, Mieno, and Okamoto (arXiv:2602.16152; first public 18 February 2026) define these period-doubling iterates and classify their *smallest string attractors*. Their section on period-doubling words proves two attractor pairs but does not give the MUS inventory. Their separate Fibonacci section classifies Fibonacci MUSs, a different word family. Kishi, Guo, Urbina, and Bannai (arXiv:2605.18034; first public 18 May 2026) study occurrence-preserving morphisms and MUSs of other morphic words; their relevant applications do not imply this four-occurrence period-doubling classification. The theorem here concerns the complete minimal-unique-factor language, not attractor optimality. Priority against unindexed literature remains subject to independent review.

## Limitations
The exact four-MUS theorem applies only to complete iterates \(D_k\) with \(k\geq3\). No classification is asserted for arbitrary non-dyadic prefixes, larger alphabets, or other substitutions. Finite verifier outputs by themselves do not prove an all-order theorem. The argument above supplies an infinite proof.
