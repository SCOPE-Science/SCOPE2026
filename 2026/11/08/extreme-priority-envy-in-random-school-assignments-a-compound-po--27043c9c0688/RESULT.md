# Extreme-priority envy in random school assignments: a compound-Poisson cluster law

## Finding

Consider a balanced one-to-one matching market of \(n\) students and \(n\) schools. Fix **any** bijective assignment independently of all preferences and priorities. Each student's strict ranking of all schools, and each school's strict priority ranking of all students, are mutually independent uniform random permutations. Larger priority rank means lower priority.

For a fixed integer \(h\ge 2\) and \(n\ge h\), let \(X_{n,h}\) count ordered student-school pairs \((i,j)\) for which student \(i\) strictly prefers school \(j\) to her assigned school **and** has a priority advantage of more than \(n-h\) positions over the incumbent of school \(j\). This is the count of *very justified envy* pairs at the extreme threshold \(k=n-h\), for the fixed assignment.

The exact expectation for every \(n\ge h\) is
\[
\mathbb E X_{n,h}=\frac{h(h-1)}4.
\]
As \(n\to\infty\) with \(h\) fixed, \(X_{n,h}\) converges **in total variation** to
\[
X_h=\sum_{t=1}^{h-1}\sum_{a=1}^{N_t}B_{t,a},
\]
where the \(N_t\) are independent \(\operatorname{Poisson}(1)\) variables and, independently, the \(B_{t,a}\) are \(\operatorname{Binomial}(t,1/2)\). In particular,
\[
\mathbb E z^{X_h}=\exp\left[\sum_{t=1}^{h-1}\left(\left(\frac{1+z}{2}\right)^t-1\right)\right]
\quad(0\le z\le1),
\]
and therefore
\[
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr(X_{n,h}=0)=\exp\left(-h+2-2^{-(h-1)}\right).}
\]
The limiting variance is \(\operatorname{Var}(X_h)=h(h-1)(h+1)/12\). For \(h=2\), \(X_h\) is exactly Poisson with mean \(1/2\), but for every \(h\ge3\) it is a genuine compound-Poisson law with clustering and variance strictly above its mean. This gives a quantitative distinction between counting envy pairs and counting schools with at least one severe priority violation.

Equivalently, let \(M_n\) denote the largest positive priority-rank advantage over an incumbent among all student-school pairs that exhibit envy, with \(M_n=0\) if no such pair exists. For each fixed integer \(h\ge2\),
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr(n-M_n\ge h)=\exp\left(-h+2-2^{-(h-1)}\right).
\]
In particular, the maximum such violation typically lies within a finite number of ranks of the theoretical maximum, even though the underlying assignment is fixed independently of priorities and preferences.

## Assumptions and scope

Number students and schools \(1,\ldots,n\). Relabeling schools lets the predetermined perfect matching be \(i\mapsto i\). Write \(\rho_j(i)\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\) for student \(i\)'s priority rank at school \(j\), with rank \(1\) best. Write \(j\succ_i i\) if student \(i\) prefers school \(j\) to her assigned school \(i\). Define
\[
X_{n,h}=\sum_{j=1}^n\sum_{i\ne j}
\mathbf1\bigl\{j\succ_i i,\ \rho_j(j)-\rho_j(i)>n-h\bigr\}.
\]
All \(2n\) permutations are mutually independent and uniform. The integer \(h\) is fixed before taking \(n\to\infty\). The allocation is not generated by deferred acceptance or any preference-responsive allocation mechanism; the theorem is a **mechanism-independent fixed-matching null benchmark**, not a claim about the outcome of those algorithms. The joint-distribution claim requires independent school-specific priorities rather than a common priority ranking.

## Proof

**1. Exact mean.** For distinct students \(i,j\), an independent uniform preference ranking yields \(\Pr(j\succ_i i)=1/2\). A uniform priority permutation gives
\[
\Pr\bigl(\rho_j(j)-\rho_j(i)>n-h\bigr)
=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{d=n-h+1}^{n-1}(n-d)
=\frac{h(h-1)}{2n(n-1)}.
\]
Independence and summing over \(n(n-1)\) ordered pairs give the asserted exact expectation.

**2. Critical schools and cluster sizes.** Fix school \(j\). Its incumbent \(j\) must have rank \(n-q\) for some \(q\in\{0,\ldots,h-2\}\), or no student can enjoy a sufficient priority advantage. At rank \(n-q\), only the top \(t=h-q-1\) students on the priority list can cause an event. Because the incumbent's rank is uniform, each \(t=1,\ldots,h-1\) occurs with probability exactly \(1/n\), independently across schools. Conditional on such a rank, these \(t\) candidates form a uniform subset of \(n-1\) students other than the incumbent. For any one candidate the comparison between school \(j\) and the candidate's own school is a fair coin.

**3. Decoupling dependent preferences.** Different schools can select the same candidate in their short top lists, causing dependence between two pairwise comparisons drawn from the same student's full ranking. Define a *collision* as a student appearing in the short top lists of two distinct critical schools. For a given pair of schools, both are critical with probability \(((h-1)/n)^2\). Conditional on their criticality, each list has size at most \(h-1\) and their expected intersection is at most \((h-1)^2/(n-1)\). A union bound over school pairs therefore gives
\[
\Pr(\text{collision})\le\binom n2\frac{(h-1)^4}{n^2(n-1)}=\frac{(h-1)^4}{2n}.
\]
Outside the collision event, every active pairwise preference comparison is associated with a different student. Conditional on the priority lists, these comparisons are thus mutually independent fair Bernoulli variables. Coupling them to independent fair coins gives a random variable \(\widetilde X_{n,h}\), with
\[
d_{\rm TV}\bigl(\mathcal L(X_{n,h}),\mathcal L(\widetilde X_{n,h})\bigr)
\le\frac{(h-1)^4}{2n}.
\]
The surrogate is a sum of \(n\) independent per-school variables: for each \(t\in\{1,\ldots,h-1\}\), a school contributes an independent \(\operatorname{Binomial}(t,1/2)\) variable with probability \(1/n\), and otherwise contributes zero. Accordingly its probability generating function is
\[
\mathbb E z^{\widetilde X_{n,h}}=
\left[1+\frac1n\sum_{t=1}^{h-1}\left(\left(\frac{1+z}{2}\right)^t-1\right)\right]^n.
\]
Taking the limit yields the displayed compound-Poisson generating function. The coupling proves convergence in total variation, rather than only weak convergence: the counts of school types form a multinomial array with cell probabilities \(1/n\), converging in total variation to independent \(\operatorname{Poisson}(1)\) counts by the elementary law of small numbers (for fixed \(h\)); adding independent binomial marks cannot increase total variation.

**4. Zero events, moments, and maximal severity.** At \(z=0\), the exponent is
\[
\sum_{t=1}^{h-1}(2^{-t}-1)
=(1-2^{-(h-1)})-(h-1)=-h+2-2^{-(h-1)}.
\]
Taking the first two derivatives of the limiting compound-Poisson cumulant generating function gives mean \(\sum_{t=1}^{h-1}t/2=h(h-1)/4\) and variance \(\sum_{t=1}^{h-1}(t^2+t)/4=h(h-1)(h+1)/12\). The prelimit second moments also converge: \(X_{n,h}\le (h-1)K_n\), where \(K_n\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,(h-1)/n)\) counts critical schools, and \(K_n\) has uniformly bounded exponential moments for fixed \(h\). Finally, \(X_{n,h}=0\) is exactly the event \(M_n\le n-h\), proving the maximal-severity tail formula.

## Verification

The standalone dependency-free program `check.py` computes violations directly from full preference and priority permutations and separately through the critical-school reduction. It exhausts all \(16\) profiles at \(n=2\), and all \(46{,}656\) independent ranking profiles at \(n=3\), for every admissible \(h\). It compares both implementations and checks the exact first-moment formula using rational arithmetic; additional seeded finite randomized comparisons cover \(n=4,9,28\). Run `python3 check.py` from the directory containing this file.

Recorded checks: \(93{,}328\) exhaustive profile-threshold comparisons and \(36{,}000\) seeded comparisons passed. These are finite consistency tests; the rigorous infinite-size conclusion comes from the explicit coupling and rare-marked-school limit above.

## Relationship to prior work

Hamdan's *Very Justified Envy* (arXiv:2609.07682, first posted September 7, 2026) introduces thresholded priority violations and develops mechanisms and simulations. The present theorem studies the **distribution of extreme violations for a fixed, exogenous perfect matching** under independently randomized school priorities and student preferences, obtaining an explicit compound-Poisson law and the maximal-gap tail. This is not a statement about the incentive, efficiency, or stability properties of the mechanisms in Hamdan's paper. The available abstract does not state this distributional theorem; the full paper could not be inspected, so complete noncoverage is not certified.

Shen et al. (AAMAS 2023) study stochastic- and likelihood-envy fairness for uncertain priorities under a shared ranking, with algorithmic allocation choices; their formal fairness definitions and model do not imply the school-specific extreme-gap law above. Ortega et al. (2026) study envy distributions for outcomes of deferred acceptance and random serial dictatorship, rather than this fixed-matching independent-priority null model. Standard Poisson approximation supplies a classical tool here, but does not independently furnish the school-specific binomial cluster law without the critical-school reduction and collision coupling.

## Limitations

The theorem requires strict independent uniform student preferences, independent uniform priority lists **at each school**, and a matching fixed before these lists are sampled. It does not claim these assumptions describe actual administrative assignments; it does not quantify severe envy after deferred acceptance or other endogenous algorithms. The window keeps \(h\) fixed as \(n\to\infty\); growing \(h\) is not addressed. The novelty assessment is conditional on accessible source material; the full text of the most directly related 2026 article was not obtainable and may contain additional related computations.
