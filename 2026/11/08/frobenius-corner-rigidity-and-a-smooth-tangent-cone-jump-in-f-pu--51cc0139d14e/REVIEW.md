# Self-review

## Correctness

**PASS.** The corner congruence is checked for all exponents, including \(e<r\); exact nonvanishing and vanishing at the next exponent both follow from the single surviving monomial. The diagonal Fermat calculation uses nonzero binomial coefficients modulo \(p\) via Lucas's theorem and bounds exponents strictly below \(p^e\). The strict order inequality \(n(q-1)>q+1\) (for \(n\ge2,q\ge4\)) proves a common smooth tangent cone, and the initial Jacobian regular sequence proves common finite Milnor number \(q^n\). The embedded independent-of-CAS exact finite checker replay is corroboration, not an infinite proof.

## Originality

**PASS, subject to historical risk.** The closest motivating source, Svoray (arXiv:2606.04014, 2026), gives a particular plane-branch threshold jump and related bounds, but not the full Frobenius-corner formula for arbitrary \(p,r,n\) nor the precise same-smooth-tangent-cone/same-Milnor-number dichotomy. The homogeneous families in Smith (2023) do not imply the nonhomogeneous jump. Local constancy of F-pure thresholds for each fixed singularity (Hernández–Núñez-Betancourt–Witt, 2018) is not constancy across this finite-order perturbation. The polynomial technique resembles the motivating example and is not independently claimed. Further historical comparison could reveal an equivalent consequence of general deformation literature.

## Mathematical value

**PASS.** The natural question left by higher-order-perturbation examples is whether the smooth leading projective hypersurface and the isolated Milnor number prevent a threshold change. A uniform counterexample for every prime, every suitable Frobenius power and every ambient hypersurface dimension gives a structural boundary to this expectation; the exact complete sequence of Frobenius exponents explains the jump. The general corner-rigidity lemma identifies the mechanism and an infinite-dimensional stable ideal class rather than just a toy numerical example.

## Limits

The proof concerns local F-pure thresholds, not arbitrary ordinary multiple points or other invariants. The all-degree result is mathematical, while finite verification is a cross-check. Historical priority and general external validation remain unsettled.
