# Hexagon-controlled scale-two Rips persistence of line graphs

## Finding

**Theorem.** Let \(G=(V,E)\) be a finite connected simple graph of girth at least six, with at least one six-cycle, and suppose **every two distinct simple six-cycles are edge-disjoint**. Set \(b=|E|-|V|+1\), and let \(q\) be the number of unoriented simple six-cycles in \(G\). On the vertex set of the line graph \(L(G)\), use shortest-path distance and inclusive Vietoris--Rips complexes \(X_r=\operatorname{VR}_{\le r}(V(L(G)))\). Then \(q\le b\) and

\[
 X_1\simeq\bigvee^b S^1,\qquad
 X_2\simeq\left(\bigvee^{b-q}S^1\right)\vee\left(\bigvee^q S^2\right).
\]

The map \(\widetilde H_1(X_1;\mathbb Z)\to\widetilde H_1(X_2;\mathbb Z)\) induced by inclusion is surjective. Its kernel is the **primitive direct summand** freely generated by the \(q\) original six-cycle classes, viewed through the canonical equivalence \(X_1\simeq G\). Moreover \(\widetilde H_2(X_2;\mathbb Z)\cong\mathbb Z^q\), and all reduced integral homology in other degrees vanishes except the rank-\(b-q\) group in degree one. These statements hold with arbitrary vertex degrees, bridges, and noncactus configurations such as theta graphs with paths of lengths three, three, and seven.

The new ingredient is the exact description of the *Rips--Čech difference*: when \(G\) meets the hypotheses, the centered closed-neighborhood complex inside \(X_2\) misses **exactly two maximal 2-simplices per six-cycle**, and no higher simplices.

## Assumptions and scope

An edge of \(G\) is a vertex of \(L(G)\). Two such vertices are adjacent exactly when their underlying edges have an endpoint in common. The distance in \(L(G)\) is the **unweighted graph shortest-path distance**, not an ambient Euclidean or learned edge-feature distance. A Rips simplex at integral scale \(r\) is an edge subset with all line-graph pairwise distances \(\le r\). Six-cycles count unoriented simple cycles, not walks or only facial cycles. Girth \(\ge6\) excludes triangles, squares and pentagons; all six-cycles, not just a chosen basis, must be pairwise edge-disjoint. The theorem asserts the scales one and two only. A wedge with zero copies of a sphere is a point.

For each vertex \(v\in V\), write \(S_v=\{e\in E:v\in e\}\). For each edge \(e=uv\), its closed line-graph neighborhood is \(N_{L(G)}[e]=S_u\cup S_v\). Let \(\Delta(A)\) be the full simplex on \(A\). Define

\[
Y=\operatorname{Cl}(L(G))=\bigcup_{v\in V}\Delta(S_v),\qquad
 B=\bigcup_{e\in E}\Delta(N_{L(G)}[e]).
\]

Then \(Y=X_1\subseteq B\subseteq X_2\). Here \(B\) is also the intrinsic graph Čech complex of \(L(G)\) at scale two, with the radius-one convention.

## Proof

**1. Unique joining edge.** Let \(a,b\) be disjoint edges of \(G\) satisfying \(d_{L(G)}(a,b)=2\). There is an edge \(c\) joining one endpoint of \(a\) to one endpoint of \(b\), and it is unique. Indeed, two such joining edges either meet at an endpoint of \(a\) or \(b\) and produce a triangle, or meet different endpoints and produce a quadrilateral. Both are ruled out by girth \(\ge6\).

**2. An explicit simplicial collapse \(B\searrow Y\).** Since \(G\) is triangle-free, every pairwise intersecting collection of graph edges has a common endpoint. This gives the displayed description of \(Y\). Every face \(\sigma\in B\setminus Y\) has two disjoint edges \(a,b\). Its generating closed neighborhood must be centered at the unique joining edge \(c=c(\sigma)\) from Step 1: otherwise no other closed neighborhood could contain both \(a,b\). Consequently \(\sigma\subseteq N_{L(G)}[c]\), and the unique center stays the same upon adding or deleting \(c\). Pair each lower face \(\sigma\in B\setminus Y\) with \(c\notin\sigma\) to \(\sigma\cup\{c\}\). Every face outside \(Y\) belongs to precisely one pair. Remove pairs in descending order of the lower face's cardinality. At its turn, a lower face has no remaining coface other than its matched upper face: any other coface is still outside \(Y\), has the same unique center and belongs to a strictly larger, previously removed pair. Thus each removal is an elementary free-face collapse, giving \(B\searrow Y\). In particular each Rips edge \(\{a,b\}\) of distance two contained in \(B\) is homotopic rel endpoints to the two-edge path \(a,c,b\) in \(Y\), via the triangle \(\{a,c,b\}\).

**3. All exceptional triples are alternating hexagon edges.** Suppose a Rips simplex \(\sigma\) is outside \(B\). There are disjoint \(a,b\in\sigma\) with line-graph distance two; let their unique joining edge be \(c\). Since \(\sigma\) is not contained in any closed neighborhood, some third edge \(d\in\sigma\setminus N_{L(G)}[c]\) is within line-graph distance two of both \(a,b\). Label \(a=ux\), \(c=uv\), and \(b=vy\). The edge \(d\) cannot meet \(a\) or \(b\): for example, if it meets \(a\) at \(x\) rather than \(u\), the required path of at most two line-graph edges from \(d\) to \(b\), combined with \(a,c,b\), would create a cycle of length at most five in \(G\); meeting \(a\) at \(u\) would place \(d\) in \(N_{L(G)}[c]\). The argument at \(b\) is symmetric. Thus \(a,b,d\) are pairwise disjoint and their pairs have unique joining edges. The joining edge between \(a,d\) must meet \(a\) at \(x\), not \(u\), and that between \(b,d\) must meet \(b\) at \(y\), not \(v\); otherwise a forbidden cycle of length at most five occurs. Writing \(d=zw\), the joining edges are \(xz\) and \(wy\). We obtain the simple hexagon

\[
 u-x-z-w-y-v-u.
\]

Conversely, in every such hexagon the three alternate edges \(ux,zw,vy\) form a Rips 2-simplex, and the other three alternate edges \(xz,wy,vu\) form another. Neither belongs to \(B\): their three pairwise joining edges are distinct, and uniqueness in Step 1 excludes any common closed-neighborhood center. Thus there are exactly two exceptional triples for every hexagon. Distinct hexagons cannot give the same exceptional triple because their joining edges are uniquely determined.

**4. No exceptional triple extends to a larger simplex.** Fix exceptional triple \(a,b,d\) with \(a=ux\), \(b=vy\), \(c=uv\), \(d=zw\) and hexagon as above. Any edge \(t\in N_{L(G)}[c]\setminus\{a,b\}\) has \(d_{L(G)}(t,d)>2\): a possible length-two connection between an edge incident at \(u\) or \(v\) (including \(c\)) and \(zw\) would close a triangle, quadrilateral or pentagon along the displayed hexagon. If \(t\notin N_{L(G)}[c]\) and is within distance two of \(a,b\), the argument in Step 3 produces a second simple hexagon through the fixed three-edge path \(a,c,b\). For \(t\ne d\), it is distinct from the first and shares those original graph edges, contrary to the hypothesis on six-cycles. Therefore no fourth vertex extends the exceptional triple. The complete difference \(X_2\setminus B\) is precisely the set of two *maximal triangles* per hexagon, not merely a list of some triangles.

**5. Read the attaching maps in \(G\).** The cover of \(Y\) by the simplices \(\Delta(S_v)\) has contractible nonempty intersections: two meet at the vertex corresponding to edge \(uv\) precisely when \(uv\in E\), and no three meet. The nerve is \(G\), so \(Y\simeq G\) canonically up to homotopy. Equivalently, subdivide original graph edges, send each vertex \(e\in L(G)\) to the midpoint of the original edge \(e\), and send each line-graph edge between \(a,b\) to the two half-edges through their common endpoint. Every exceptional triangle's three boundary Rips edges correspond under \(B\searrow Y\) to the three two-edge paths through their unique joining edges. The resulting six-step loop in \(Y\), and then the corresponding loop in \(G\), traverses its underlying hexagon once. Both alternating exceptional triangles of the same hexagon attach along this same freely homotopic loop, up to orientation.

**6. Cancel independent hexagon loops.** Up to homotopy, \(X_2\) is obtained from the graph \(G\) by attaching *two* 2-cells along each of its \(q\) six-cycle loops. First attach one cell per hexagon. Choose one original graph edge in each hexagon; these choices are distinct, and no other selected hexagon loop traverses that edge. Each chosen edge occurs exactly once in its corresponding attaching loop, so the first attached 2-cell cancels that graph 1-cell as a CW free pair. Deleting these selected edges leaves the graph connected: each deleted edge has a detour along the other five edges of its hexagon, none of which are selected because hexagons are edge-disjoint. The resulting connected graph has first Betti number \(b-q\), proving \(q\le b\). Attach the second cell per hexagon. Its attaching loop is already nullhomotopic after the corresponding first cell, hence contributes one wedge summand \(S^2\). The claimed homotopy equivalence follows over the integers.

**7. The persistence kernel.** At scale one, \(X_1=Y\simeq G\). Under the inclusion \(Y\hookrightarrow X_2\), exactly the \(q\) oriented hexagon classes are killed by the first cells, and no other degree-one classes. Their cellular edge vectors have mutually disjoint supports and each has a \(\pm1\) coefficient at its private chosen edge, so they span a primitive rank-\(q\) direct summand of \(H_1(G;\mathbb Z)\). The second cells contribute precisely \(q\) free degree-two classes. This proves all integral homology and persistence statements. \(\square\)

## Verification

The standalone, dependency-free `verify.py` reconstructs original graphs, every simple cycle of length at most six, line-graph distances, full Rips and centered complexes, and all centered collapse pairs. It checks the uniqueness of each generating center, verifies each relative elementary collapse is free *at the moment removed*, and compares every exceptional simplex with the two alternating triangles of the actual six-cycles. It independently calculates reduced mod-two simplicial homology by exact bit-matrix elimination. The cases include individual six-, seven- and eight-cycles; bouquets of two hexagons and mixed hexagon/octagon; genuine noncactus theta graphs; a hexagon with multiple leaves; and a boundary example with overlapping six-cycles outside the theorem. The recorded output is in `CHECKS.txt`. Run `python3 verify.py` alongside no external files. These finite experiments corroborate the proof; they do not prove its arbitrary-graph quantifiers or replace the integral CW argument.

## Relationship to prior work

Shin, Jeon, Cho, Cho and Gwon [1] motivate line-graph Vietoris--Rips persistence for graph representations. Their studied learned edge filtrations do not state this unweighted shortest-path integral homotopy formula. Adamaszek [2] treats powers of graph metrics, including \(\operatorname{Cl}(C_6^2)\simeq S^2\), the high-girth collapse theorem, and the unsquared line-graph equivalence \(\operatorname{Cl}(L(G))\simeq\operatorname{Cl}(G)^{(2)}\); none calculates the squared *line graph* for arbitrary overlapping graph structure with edge-disjoint hexagons.

The closest published line-graph result [3] proves the radius-one graph Čech complex \(B\) collapses to \(Y\) when \(G\) has no triangles or four-cycles. That result explains the centered portion but does not include the two noncentered Rips triangles per hexagon, their attachments, or the scale-one-to-scale-two Rips kernel. The related hexagon-packing theorem [4] proves a formally similar wedge formula for **vertex** Rips complexes of graphs of girth at least six. It cannot be directly applied to \(L(G)\) when any vertex of \(G\) has degree three: its line graph then has triangles, violating the girth hypothesis. The new content here is a full Rips-versus-Čech combinatorial reduction on line graphs and the resulting integral persistence interpretation. Metric gluing theorems [5] imply some special cactus/wedge cases but do not provide this all-graph conclusion for noncactus theta graphs.

## Limitations

The argument assumes every six-cycle is edge-disjoint from every other and that no cycle has length below six. A theta graph with three internally disjoint paths of length three has three overlapping six-cycles and is outside the theorem; its line-graph scale-two complex has mod-two second Betti number four, illustrating why one cannot simply count overlapping hexagons. No statement is made for higher scales, for all possible Rips metrics on graph edges, or for geometric realizations including edge-interior points. The supporting computation covers only the listed finite examples. Equivalence to unindexed or differently formulated earlier work remains a residual originality risk.
