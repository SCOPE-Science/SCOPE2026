# Minimum maximal matching mobility in the four-dimensional cube

## Finding
Let \(Q_4\) be the graph with vertex set \(\{0,1\}^4\), with edges joining strings of Hamming distance one. Its minimum inclusion-maximal matchings consist of exactly \(6\) edges; there are precisely \(192\) of them. Let \(\mathcal M\) be this set and define \(\mathcal R_t\) on \(\mathcal M\) by joining distinct matchings \(M,N\) exactly when \(|M\setminus N|\leq t\). As both matchings have the same cardinality, exactly \(|M\setminus N|\) selected edges are removed and the same number added. Thus intermediate sets are not required to remain maximal; the two endpoints must be minimum maximal matchings.

The one-exchange graph \(\mathcal R_1\) is edgeless. The two-exchange graph \(\mathcal R_2\) is connected, has \(192\) vertices and \(528\) edges, with degree distribution \(96\) vertices of degree \(5\) and \(96\) of degree \(6\), radius \(5\), and diameter \(6\). Exactly \(96\) vertices have eccentricity \(5\), and \(96\) have eccentricity \(6\).

Under the full hypercube automorphism group \(\mathbb F_2^4\rtimes S_4\), the set \(\mathcal M\) is the union of two orbits of size \(96\). These are distinguished by their degrees in \(\mathcal R_2\), namely \(5\) and \(6\). The induced graph on the first orbit has \(48\) edges, the induced graph on the second has \(96\), and there are \(384\) edges between orbits. Hence the smallest allowed number of simultaneous edge replacements guaranteeing connectivity of the optimal-configuration space is exactly \(2\).

## Assumptions and scope
All graphs are simple and undirected. Vertices of \(Q_4\) are integers \(0,\ldots,15\), interpreted as four-bit strings, and the edge \(\{u,v\}\) exists exactly when \(u\oplus v\) is one of \(1,2,4,8\). There are \(16\) vertices and \(32\) edges. A matching is a set of pairwise vertex-disjoint edges. It is *maximal* when no extra graph edge can be added; equivalently, the vertices it does not cover form an independent set. It is *minimum maximal* if it has the least possible number of edges among maximal matchings, a notion distinct from both minimum matching and maximum matching. A reconfiguration move may replace two disjoint edge members at once, not necessarily through a one-exchange intermediate state.

The claim concerns \(Q_4\) only; no analogous threshold is claimed for cubes of arbitrary dimension. The numerical findings include the complete finite configuration graph, rather than a sampled statistic.

## Proof
**1. Lower bound and exhaustive optimal-set identification.** The graph is connected, bipartite, and \(4\)-regular, with bipartition classes of size \(8\). Any independent set \(I\) has size at most \(8\), because its \(4|I|\) incident edges are all distinct and the entire graph has \(32\) edges. If \(|I|=8\), every edge has exactly one endpoint in \(I\), so its complement is also independent. Connectedness then forces \(I\) to be one of the two bipartition classes. A maximal matching with \(r\) edges leaves \(16-2r\) vertices unmatched, and those vertices must be independent. For \(r\leq3\), this is impossible since more than eight vertices remain unmatched. For \(r=4\), eight vertices remain unmatched, and they include four vertices from each bipartition class because a matching covers four from each. They cannot be a full bipartition class, so \(r=4\) is impossible. For \(r=5\), the independent checker tests all \(\binom{32}{5}=201,376\) distinct five-edge subsets and finds none that is simultaneously a matching and has independent unmatched vertices. It tests all \(\binom{32}{6}=906,192\) distinct six-edge subsets, finding exactly \(192\) valid ones. The vertex-recursive checker independently enumerates every maximal matching, finding counts \(192,1536,272\) at cardinalities \(6,7,8\), respectively. These two algorithms therefore prove the stated minimum and exact optimum count.

**2. Exchange graphs.** Let \(\mathcal M\) be the enumerated list of \(192\) distinct six-edge maximal matchings. For every unordered pair \(\{M,N\}\subset\mathcal M\), compute \(q=|M\setminus N|\), which equals \(|N\setminus M|\). The first implementation checks all \(\binom{192}{2}=18,336\) pairs and counts none with \(q=1\), and exactly \(528\) with \(q=2\). This exhaustively establishes \(\mathcal R_1=192K_1\) and the edge set of \(\mathcal R_2\). Vertex degrees are exactly \(5\) for \(96\) configurations and \(6\) for the other \(96\). The independent second implementation reaches the same edge count using its independently generated edge-subset list.

**3. Connectivity, metric, and orbits.** In each implementation, breadth-first search is performed from each of the \(192\) states through the fully constructed undirected \(\mathcal R_2\). Every search reaches every state, and the eccentricity multiset is exactly \(96\) fives and \(96\) sixes. This proves connectivity, radius \(5\), and diameter \(6\) without drawing any inference from partial enumeration. Independently, the second checker applies all \(16\cdot4!=384\) maps \(v\mapsto P(v)\oplus b\), where \(P\) permutes four bit positions, to the entire set \(\mathcal M\). It obtains precisely two distinct group orbits, each of size \(96\). Edges of \(\mathcal R_2\) split into \(48\), \(96\), and \(384\) across the two internal orbit-pairs and cross-orbit type, respectively. Since the degree sum of the orbit with \(48\) internal edges is \(2\cdot48+384=480=96\cdot5\), while the other has \(2\cdot96+384=576=96\cdot6\), the orbits coincide with the degree classes.

**4. Sharpness.** At most one edge replacement gives no transitions at all, but permitting two yields the connected graph already established. Therefore \(2\) is the exact sharp exchange threshold.

The finite proof rests on the two attached, fully specified exhaustive computer programs. The assertions about the infinite algorithmic search spaces elsewhere in graph theory are not made here: the input graph and every checked candidate set are finite and exhaustively processed.

## Verification
From this package root, run `python3 verification/enumerate_matchings.py` and `python3 verification/independent_edges.py`. The outputs must exactly equal `verification/enumerate_matchings.out` and `verification/independent_edges.out`. The first program recursively partitions vertices into unmatched vertices or matched pairs, tests maximality via unmatched independence, compares all optimal states, and performs all-source breadth-first search. The second tests all five- and six-edge subsets of the \(32\)-edge graph independently, calculates full cube-automorphism orbits, reconstructs exchanges, and conducts independent all-source breadth-first search. They use distinct generation methods, but the verification remains finite and computational rather than a formal proof in an external theorem prover.

## Relationship to prior work
Minimum maximal matching size is also called the edge domination number: the equivalence is standard for simple finite graphs. Foundational general work by Yannakakis–Gavril and Horton–Kilakos concerns edge domination and its computation, not the exact \(192\)-state optimal-matching exchange graph of \(Q_4\). Vandenbussche–West studies extension of hypercube matchings to perfect matchings, with first verified online publication on 2009-10-09; this is a different objective and configuration class. Hibi–Higashitani–Kimura–Tsuchiya treats dominating *induced* matchings and edge-ideal regularity, not all minimum maximal matchings. Baste–Fürst–Henning–Mohr–Rautenbach investigates general upper bounds and approximation for minimum maximal matchings in regular graphs; its statements do not imply the exact exchange graph of this cube. Other related cube work enumerates *perfect* matchings or investigates matching complexes, neither of which determines this optimum-set mobility theorem.

No exact overlap was identified in the searched indexed mathematical results or the accessible prior claim archive. That negative search does not constitute proof of universal historical priority. ## Limitations
The theorem is an exact claim for the fixed sixteen-vertex \(Q_4\), not a claim about all hypercubes. Replacements of two edges are simultaneous endpoint moves: the theorem does not require that either half-move be legal. The exhaustive programs rely on ordinary Python integer and set operations and the correctness of the finite search; both are included to support reproducibility. Historical novelty is assessed against accessible sources, and unindexed earlier enumerations may exist. No independent scholarly audit has been performed.
