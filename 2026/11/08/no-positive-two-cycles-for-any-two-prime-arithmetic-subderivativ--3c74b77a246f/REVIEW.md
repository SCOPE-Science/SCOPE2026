# Scientific assessment

## Correctness — PASS
The claim and proof cover every positive integer and every unordered pair of distinct primes, with no restriction on their size. Any positive two-periodic state has positive first and second iterates, so the logarithmic ratios are defined. The product of the two ratios is one, making both numerator factors divisors of \(p^2q^2\); unique factorization yields nine and only nine exponent splits. The valuation equation is exact, including zero initial valuations. Eight splits are algebraically impossible, and the remaining split gives the identity ratio. Direct integer regression on 45 prime pairs and the range through 100000 agrees with the proof but is not used to establish an infinite assertion.

## Originality — PASS within inspected evidence
The 2019 introduction of subderivatives gives the map and its Leibniz-additive identities, not a two-cycle classification. The 2026 iterated-subderivative study determines one-step fixed points and vanishing, and explicitly calls periodic points a future problem; those results do not rule out \(\(D^2(n)=n\)\) with \(\(D(n)\ne n\)\). An available online theorem excludes all nontrivial cycles for the special set \(\{2,3\}\), but it does not extend its conclusion to arbitrary distinct pairs. Searches of indexed research, equivalent formulations, prior mathematical records and accessible preceding claims found no result implying the all-pairs period-two exclusion. The residual risk of unpublished or poorly indexed equivalent work is explicitly acknowledged.

## Mathematical value — PASS
The arithmetic-subderivative family arises naturally from Leibniz arithmetic differentiation, and periodic points are an identified outstanding direction in its dynamical study. A universal prime-support obstruction is more than a single example: it proves that the first possible nontrivial positive period for **every** two-prime map is at least three, without requiring either prime to be small or permitting a finite parameter cutoff. The nine-case constraint supplies a general structural reason for the obstruction. This does not establish existence or impossibility of longer periods, nor supersede the stronger special-case theorem for \(\{2,3\}\).
