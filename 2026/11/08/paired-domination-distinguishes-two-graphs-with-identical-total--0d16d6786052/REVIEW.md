## Correctness — PASS
The graph models are explicit and fixed. The two independent-permutation arrays are checked against every one of the 256 open-neighborhood incidences; this proves equality of total-domination polynomials. The 80 minimum total-dominating sets are also counted analytically, and the 32 exceptional Shrikhande induced claws are identified through the local six-cycle structure. The full polynomials follow by a complete enumeration of all 65,536 subsets of each graph using an exact finite perfect-matching recurrence. Two independently coded implementations, one bitmask-based and one set-theoretic, agree coefficient by coefficient. This is a finite proof, not an asymptotic inference.

## Originality — PASS with residual historical uncertainty
The founding 1998 paired-domination paper defines the invariant and gives general results, not the present sixteen-vertex census. The 2011/2013 Cartesian-product paper proves general bounds and does not determine this exact pair of polynomials. The known isomorphism of the graphs' open-neighborhood incidence systems explains total-domination equality but does not preserve an induced matching condition. Existing results for the rook polynomial, the Shrikhande graph's induced matchings, and their independence complexes are explicitly excluded from the novelty claim. Searches for equivalent graph names, polynomial identities, minimum paired-set counts and stronger parameter coverage did not identify an exact Shrikhande paired-domination polynomial; an unindexed result or unavailable complete paper remains a possibility.

## Value — PASS
These two nonisomorphic strongly regular graphs are a canonical comparison pair with the same \((16,6,2,2)\) parameters. The exact same total-domination polynomials but different paired-domination polynomials provide a sharp, natural demonstration that open-neighborhood incidence alone does not determine the matchability of optimal selected subsets. The precisely classified 32 induced claws explain the first coefficient difference; the complete positive discrepancy across three even degrees is a reproducible finite benchmark for comparing domination invariants.
