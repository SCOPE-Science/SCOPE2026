# Sharp \(4d\)-point Fourier-grid reconstruction of polynomial intensities on noncircular ellipses

## Finding
Let \(a>b>0\), let \(d\ge1\) be an integer, and parametrize the ellipse by
\[
z(\theta)=a\cos\theta+i b\sin\theta,\qquad\theta\in\mathbb R.
\]
Write \(\mathcal P_d\) for the complex polynomials of degree at most \(d\), and
\(\mathcal I_d\) for the **real linear span** of the functions
\(\theta\mapsto|p(z(\theta))|^2\), with \(p\in\mathcal P_d\).
The following claims hold simultaneously:

1. The intensity-profile space has the **exact** Fourier description
   \[
   \mathcal I_d=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}
   \{1,\cos(k\theta),\sin(k\theta):1\le k<2d;\ \cos(2d\theta)\},
   \qquad\dim_{\mathbb R}\mathcal I_d=4d.
   \]
   Equivalently, on Hermitian coefficient matrices indexed by \(0,\dots,d\), the profile-restriction map has kernel precisely the Hermitian bivariate polynomials divisible by the defining ellipse quadratic. Its real dimension is \((d-1)^2\), interpreted as zero for \(d=1\).
2. Put \(N=4d\), and sample at \(\theta_j=\theta_0+2\pi j/N\), \(0\le j<N\). These \(N\) samples reconstruct **every** profile in \(\mathcal I_d\) linearly if and only if
   \[
   \cos(2d\theta_0)\ne0.
   \]
   In this case they are optimal in count among all linear sampling schemes reconstructing the entire profile space. If the cosine vanishes, the sampling rank is exactly \(4d-1\). In particular the zero-offset equispaced design always works.
3. If \(p,q\in\mathcal P_d\) have equal moduli on any nonempty open ellipse arc, then \(p=\eta q\) for some \(|\eta|=1\). Thus the explicit \(4d\)-sample scheme also recovers every polynomial up to its unavoidable global phase.

The contrast at the circular limit is sharp: when \(a=b>0\), the profile-space dimension is \(2d+1\), while phase uniqueness fails already for degree one (compare \(p(z)=z\) and \(q(z)=a\) on the centered circle). Translation and rotation give the same assertions for every Euclidean noncircular ellipse.

## Assumptions and scope
All measurements use the *squared modulus* and the standard complex Euclidean coordinate; the ellipse is nondegenerate, with finite distinct principal semiaxes \(a>b>0\). The reconstruction claim concerns the real linear span of intensity profiles, not the full \((d+1)^2\)-dimensional Hermitian matrix space for \(d\ge2\), and not a minimal sample count for nonlinear rank-one phase retrieval. The criterion refers to the eccentric-anomaly parameter \(\theta\), not arclength-uniform sampling.

## Proof
**Step 1 (identifying the ideal and its dimension).** Put
\[
A=(a+b)/2,\quad B=(a-b)/2,\quad 0<B<A,
\quad z=Au+B/u,\quad w=Bu+A/u.
\]
For \(|u|=1\), \(w=\overline z\). The inverse linear equations are
\[
u=\frac{Az-Bw}{A^2-B^2},\qquad
u^{-1}=\frac{Aw-Bz}{A^2-B^2}.
\]
Consequently, substituting the ellipse parameter is the quotient homomorphism
\[
\mathbb C[z,w]\longrightarrow\mathbb C[u,u^{-1}],
\]
with kernel the principal ideal generated by
\[
Q(z,w)=(A^2+B^2)zw-AB(z^2+w^2)-(A^2-B^2)^2.
\]
Indeed the linear change of variables makes this the relation \(u u^{-1}=1\). A polynomial vanishing on the parameter circle gives a Laurent polynomial vanishing at infinitely many points, hence identically, and therefore belongs to \((Q)\).

A Hermitian matrix on \(\mathcal P_d\) corresponds bijectively to a polynomial
\(H(z,w)=\sum_{0\le j,k\le d}h_{jk}z^jw^k\) satisfying
\(h_{jk}=\overline{h_{kj}}\), via \(w=\overline z\). The real dimension of this space is \((d+1)^2\). Since \(AB\ne0\), the degree of \(Q\) in each variable is two, with nonzero constant leading coefficient in either variable. Thus if \(H=QL\) has bidegree at most \((d,d)\), then \(L\) has bidegree at most \((d-2,d-2)\); when \(d=1\), it must be zero. Because \(Q\) is fixed by conjugation and swapping \(z,w\), the Hermitian \(H\) correspond precisely to Hermitian \(L\). The kernel dimension is therefore \((d-1)^2\) for \(d\ge2\), yielding image dimension \((d+1)^2-(d-1)^2=4d\) (also \(4\) when \(d=1\)). By polarization, all Hermitian forms are real linear combinations of rank-one squared moduli, so this is exactly \(\mathcal I_d\).

**Step 2 (missing highest sine harmonic).** On \(|u|=1\), \(z=Au+Bu^{-1}\) and \(\overline z=Bu+Au^{-1}\). Every polynomial \(|p(z)|^2\) of degree at most \(d\) is a real Laurent trigonometric polynomial with Fourier orders between \(-2d\) and \(2d\). Only the monomial \(|z|^{2d}\) can contribute to order \(2d\): its coefficient of \(u^{2d}\) equals the *real* number \((AB)^d\), and the coefficient at order \(-2d\) is identical. Hence the \(\sin(2d\theta)\) coefficient always vanishes. The indicated real Fourier subspace has dimension \(4d\), so equality of subspaces follows from Step 1.

**Step 3 (exact equispaced sampling and its singular offsets).** Every \(y\in\mathcal I_d\) has a unique representation
\[
y(\theta)=\sum_{|k|<2d}c_ke^{ik\theta}+\gamma\cos(2d\theta),
\quad c_{-k}=\overline{c_k},\quad\gamma\in\mathbb R.
\]
For \(N=4d\), \(\theta_j=\theta_0+2\pi j/N\), discrete character orthogonality gives the explicit formulas
\[
c_k=\frac1N\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}y(\theta_j)e^{-ik\theta_j}\quad(|k|<2d),
\qquad
\frac1N\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}(-1)^jy(\theta_j)=\gamma\cos(2d\theta_0).
\]
When \(\cos(2d\theta_0)\ne0\), these reconstruct every Fourier coefficient. When it is zero, the nonzero profile \(\cos(2d\theta)\) vanishes at every sampled point, but the lower \(4d-1\) Fourier basis functions remain independent by discrete Fourier orthogonality. Hence the singular sampling rank is exactly \(4d-1\). No smaller number than \(4d\) of real scalar measurements can linearly reconstruct every element of a \(4d\)-dimensional real space.

**Step 4 (polynomial phase uniqueness).** Let \(p,q\in\mathcal P_d\) have equal moduli on an open ellipse arc. The squared-modulus difference is a real trigonometric polynomial and hence vanishes on the entire ellipse. Define the ordinary polynomials \(U(u)=u^d p(Au+B/u)\), \(V(u)=u^d q(Au+B/u)\). By the relation \(Au+B/u=A(B/A)/u+B/(B/A)u\), the same modulus equality holds for \(|u|=1\) and \(|u|=\kappa\), where \(\kappa=B/A\in(0,1)\). If one polynomial is zero, both are zero. Otherwise the rational quotient \(R=U/V\) has modulus one on both circles wherever defined. Reflection across the two circles gives rational identities
\[
R(u)\,\overline{R(1/\overline u)}=1,
\qquad R(u)\,\overline{R(\kappa^2/\overline u)}=1.
\]
Their composition makes \(R\) invariant under the nontrivial dilation \(u\mapsto\kappa^2u\). A nonconstant rational function cannot have this invariance: its finite nonzero poles and zeros would have infinite dilation orbits, and a rational function with no finite nonzero pole or zero is a monomial, which also fails dilation invariance unless constant. Therefore \(U=\eta V\), where \(|\eta|=1\), hence \(p=\eta q\).

**Step 5 (circle).** If \(a=b\), then \(B=0\) and \(z=au\); squared polynomial moduli are trigonometric polynomials of degree at most \(d\). Their real span is the full \((2d+1)\)-dimensional trigonometric space, by the same polarization argument or direct products \(z^j\overline z^{\,k}\). The stated degree-one ambiguity is immediate.

## Verification
The self-contained script `verify_ellipse.py` uses exact rational Gaussian elimination to confirm intensity-profile ranks for degrees one through six on two rational noncircular ellipses and the circle, and independently checks a rational parametrization identity. It also tests discrete Fourier reconstruction and both regular and singular shifted grids for degrees one through six. Execute `python3 verify_ellipse.py`; its expected terminal line is `ELLIPSE_PHASE_INTENSITY_CHECK_PASS exact_cases=49 fourier_cases=66`. These finite tests are corroboration; the proof above establishes all degrees and all real semiaxes.

## Relationship to prior work
Chalendar and Partington (2024), in particular their two-concentric-circle uniqueness theorem, *already imply the polynomial phase-uniqueness portion* after a Joukowski substitution. That implication is supporting context, **not claimed as new on its own**. Their inspected results do not determine the Hermitian restriction kernel or exhibit the sharp \(4d\)-point equispaced ellipse Fourier sampling criterion, including its exact singular offsets. Przybyłek and Siedlecki (2019) give phaseless polynomial algorithms using unrestricted chosen points, with a different sample geometry. Liehr (2023) gives uniqueness criteria for unions of parallel lines and entire-function classes; it does not cover one ellipse's finite lifting rank. The genuine increment claimed here is the complete ellipse intensity subspace and its exactly invertible, sharp-size deterministic Fourier grid.

## Limitations
The \(4d\) minimum is for **linear reconstruction of all complete intensity profiles**, not a proven minimum for nonlinear phase retrieval of polynomial coefficients. Full Hermitian matrix lifting is not injective for \(d\ge2\), despite uniqueness on rank-one polynomial signals. The explicit grid is uniform in ellipse anomaly, not arclength, and near the circular limit coefficient reconstruction from intensity data may become poorly conditioned. The proof assumes exact continuous mathematics; numerical tests do not certify an infinite theorem. A separate 2018 paper on locating polynomial zeros inside ellipses was located by abstract but its full text could not be inspected, leaving a limited historical-priority uncertainty.
