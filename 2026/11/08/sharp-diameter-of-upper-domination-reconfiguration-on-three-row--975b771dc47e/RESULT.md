# Sharp diameter of upper-domination reconfiguration on three-row rook graphs

## Finding
For every integer \(n\ge 4\), let \(G_n=K_3\square K_n\), the three-row, \(n\)-column rook graph. Its upper domination number (the largest cardinality of an inclusion-minimal dominating set) is \(\Gamma(G_n)=n\). All upper dominating sets are in bijection with non-surjective words \(w\in\{0,1,2\}^n\): place one selected vertex in column \(j\), in row \(w_j\). Hence their number is exactly \(3\cdot 2^n-3\).

Let \(\mathcal R_n\) have these upper dominating sets as vertices, with two sets adjacent when one selected vertex is replaced by one unselected vertex while retaining upper domination. Token jumping (unrestricted replacement) and token sliding (replacement along an edge of \(G_n\)) yield the SAME graph. It is connected, has
\[
|V(\mathcal R_n)|=3\cdot 2^n-3,\qquad
|E(\mathcal R_n)|=3n(2^{n-1}+1),\qquad
\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal R_n)=n+\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1.
\]
Thus restricting three-row rook-board configurations to optimal *inclusion-minimal* dominating sets increases worst-case single-column exchange distance from the ambient Hamming distance \(n\) to asymptotically \(3n/2\).

## Assumptions and scope
All graphs are finite, simple and undirected. The vertices of \(G_n\) are \((i,j)\) for \(i\in\{0,1,2\}\) and \(j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\); two distinct vertices are adjacent if their rows or columns coincide. A set \(S\) is dominating when every vertex outside \(S\) is adjacent to a vertex in \(S\). It is inclusion-minimal when removal of any selected vertex destroys domination. Upper dominating means inclusion-minimal and of maximal cardinality among inclusion-minimal dominating sets, rather than merely a maximum-size dominating set. A reconfiguration edge changes exactly one selected vertex, with all intermediate sets again upper dominating. All counts treat rows and columns as labeled. No claim is made at \(n<4\), nor about arbitrary numbers of rows or nonoptimal dominating-set reconfiguration.

## Proof
**1. Classification.** A rook set dominates precisely when it meets every row or meets every column: if some row and some column are empty, their intersection is undominated; conversely a fully hit row-family or column-family dominates all board squares. An inclusion-minimal dominating set can have no more than \(n\) vertices. Indeed, any set with more than \(n\) vertices, if it meets every column, repeats a column and a vertex in that column may be removed while retaining all columns. If it does not meet every column, it must meet every row; since it has more than three vertices, some row repeats and a vertex may be removed while retaining all rows. This proves \(\Gamma(G_n)\le n\).

For \(n\ge4\), any inclusion-minimal dominating \(n\)-set must meet all columns; otherwise it must meet all three rows, but since \(n>3\) it repeats a row and is not minimal. Hitting \(n\) columns with \(n\) vertices means one selected vertex per column. If all three rows are also hit, a row repeats and its vertex can be removed while the remaining set still meets every row, contradicting minimality. Conversely, selecting exactly one vertex per column and leaving at least one row empty is inclusion-minimal: removal makes that column empty while the previously empty row remains empty. This proves the bijection and shows \(\Gamma(G_n)=n\).

**2. Exchange graph and counting.** Each upper set is a word over the three row symbols missing at least one symbol. Any one-vertex replacement between two upper sets keeps the occupied column (there is exactly one vertex per column at both endpoints), so token jumping automatically is a rook-edge slide, and the word changes at exactly one position. For each of the three omitted-symbol choices, \(2^n\) words use the other two symbols. Each constant word is counted twice, yielding \(3\cdot2^n-3\) distinct vertices. Every one-symbol word has \(2n\) neighbors; each nonconstant admissible word has \(n\) neighbors by changing a coordinate within its two used symbols. It has one additional neighbor precisely when exactly one coordinate carries a singleton-occurring symbol: changing that singleton to the third symbol remains admissible. For \(n\ge4\), the latter words number \(6n\), and there are three constant words. The degree sum is \(3n2^n+6n\), giving \(|E(\mathcal R_n)|=3n(2^{n-1}+1)\).

**3. Diameter upper bound.** Put \(k=\lfloor n/2\rfloor\). If two admissible words together use at most two symbols, the differing coordinates can be changed directly, in at most \(n\) steps. Suppose the union uses three. If one word is constant, say \(a^n\), and the other uses only the two other symbols, with respective multiplicities \(s,t\ge1\), convert all but one coordinate destined for the less frequent symbol first to the more frequent symbol, change the last remaining \(a\) to the less frequent symbol, and correct the temporarily changed coordinates. This uses \(n+\min(s,t)-1\le n+k-1\) steps. The same argument works in reverse if the destination is constant.

Otherwise both words use two symbols with exactly one symbol \(c\) in common: write the source alphabet as \(\{a,c\}\) and the target alphabet as \(\{b,c\}\). Partition coordinates by their ordered source-target pair, with counts \(A=\#(c,c)\), \(B=\#(c,b)\), \(C=\#(a,c)\), \(D=\#(a,b)\). Thus \(n=A+B+C+D\) and the Hamming distance is \(h=B+C+D=n-A\). When \(D\ge1\), change every source \(a\) other than one chosen \((a,b)\) coordinate to \(c\), switch the last \(a\) directly to \(b\), then change the remaining required \(c\)'s to \(b\). Every intermediate word uses at most two symbols and the length is \(h+D-1\). When \(D=0\), both \(B\) and \(C\) are positive; by using one \((a,c)\) coordinate as a temporary \(a\)-to-\(b\)-to-\(c\) bridge, a path of length \(h+1\) exists. Since \(k\ge2\), the latter length is at most \(n+k-1\).

For \(D\ge1\), if \(D\le A+k\), the first path has length \(h+D-1\le n+k-1\). If instead \(D\ge A+k+1\), use the all-\(b\) intermediate word. A two-symbol source with \(t=\min(A+B,C+D)\) occurrences of its minority symbol can be converted into the constant word \(b^n\) (where \(b\) was absent) in exactly \(n+t-1\) moves: first replace all but one minority occurrence by the majority symbol, replace the last one by \(b\), then replace all remaining majority occurrences by \(b\). From \(b^n\), obtain the target in \(A+C\) moves. The resulting total is at most \(n+(A+B)-1+(A+C)=n+2A+B+C-1\). The assumed inequality \(D\ge A+k+1\) implies \(2A+B+C\le n-k-1\le k\), so this second path has length at most \(n+k-1\). This proves connectivity and the upper diameter bound.

**4. Matching lower bound.** Take the constant word \(a^n\) and a target word using \(k\) occurrences of \(b\) and \(n-k\) occurrences of \(c\), with all three letters distinct. Consider any legal reconfiguration path, and the first step at which \(a\) disappears entirely. Just before that step there is one remaining \(a\), and all other entries must be one and the same letter (otherwise all three letters would appear). Just after it, at least \(k-1\) positions must carry the other target letter at some later stage and therefore require a second change of their coordinate. As all \(n\) positions originally carry \(a\) and must each change at least once, at least \(n+k-1\) moves are necessary. The upper construction attains this number, proving the diameter formula.

## Verification
Two standalone programs accompany this result. `verify_rook.py` independently enumerates every vertex subset of the actual \(3\times n\) rook graph in sizes at least \(n\) for \(4\le n\le7\), applies direct domination and irredundancy tests, and compares both swap models and the exact edge count. `verify_words.py` independently constructs the admissible ternary-word graph, counts vertices and edges, and runs a complete breadth-first search from EVERY state for \(4\le n\le9\). Both print a terminal `*_OK` line. The finite checks validate boundaries and implementation but are not substitutes for the all-\(n\) proof above.

## Relationship to prior work
Brešar's 2005 proof establishes a general *lower bound* for upper domination of Cartesian products; it does not classify all upper dominating sets or their exchange graph. Suzuki, Mouawad and Nishimura (2014) study addition/removal reconfiguration of dominating sets of cardinality at most a threshold, which is not the fixed-size, inclusion-minimal exchange model here. AbouEisha and collaborators (2016) investigate upper-domination complexity and hereditary graph classes, not the exact rook optimum-exchange diameter. Burdett, Haythorpe and Newcombe (2023) use rook graphs as upper-domination benchmarking instances and cite previously established numerical upper-domination values; their text does not give the present connected-state classification and diameter. The result adds an exact optimum-space metric for a natural unbounded family; claims of absolute historical priority are not made.

## Limitations
The theorem is restricted to three-row rook graphs with \(n\ge4\); no statement is made about four or more rows, general all-pair shortest paths, or transition mixing times. Complete finite calculations were performed only up to the stated lengths. Searches and inspected texts did not identify a covering theorem, but unindexed or differently named work may exist.
