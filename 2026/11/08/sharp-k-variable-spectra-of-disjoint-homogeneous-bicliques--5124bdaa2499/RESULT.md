# Sharp \(k\)-variable spectra of disjoint homogeneous bicliques
## Finding
Let \(G(n;a,b)\) be the vertex-disjoint union of \(n\) copies of the complete bipartite graph \(K_{a,b}\), for integers \(n\ge1\) and \(a,b\ge2\). All graphs are finite, simple, undirected and viewed in the language of equality and adjacency alone. For any \(G(n;a,b)\) and \(G(n';a',b')\), counting-free first-order logic with no more than \(k\) reusable variables and *arbitrarily large quantifier depth* has the following complete equivalence classification:

* Widths \(k=1,2\): all members of the class are equivalent.
* Every width \(k\ge3\): equivalence holds precisely when
\[
\min(n,k)=\min(n',k),\qquad
\{\!\{\min(a,k),\min(b,k)\}\!\}
=\{\!\{\min(a',k),\min(b',k)\}\!\}.
\]
The doubled braces denote an unordered pair with multiplicity. There are exactly \(k^2(k-1)/2\) theories at every width \(k\ge3\). Each has a unique smallest representative within the family, up to isomorphism, obtained by replacing each parameter by its \(k\)-truncation. The sharp largest such minimum order is \(2k^2\). The least width of a sentence that identifies \(G(n;a,b)\) within this family is \(1+\max\{n,a,b\}\). Thus width one and width two coincide, and every increase from width two onward strictly increases expressive power.

## Assumptions and scope
For each component choose two opposite independent sets of sizes \(a,b\). Their order is unlabelled, so interchanging \(a,b\) gives an isomorphic graph. Distinct components are not named or joined by edges. There are no counting quantifiers, vertex-colour predicates, constants or distinguished sides. The equivalence symbol \(\equiv_{\mathrm{FO}^k}\) refers to agreement on every sentence using at most \(k\) distinct variable names; quantifier rank is unrestricted. The width of identification is relative to the graph family defined above, not relative to every finite graph.

## Proof
**One and two variables.** Every graph is nonempty and loopless. Each vertex has an adjacent vertex (its opposite side is nonempty) and a distinct nonadjacent vertex (its own side has size at least two). In the ordinary two-pebble back-and-forth game, after one pebble is lifted, Duplicator can answer an equal, adjacent, or distinct nonadjacent move relative to the one remaining vertex. These are all possibilities for atomic equality and adjacency on a pair. The strategy lasts arbitrarily long; the one-pebble assertion is immediate.

**Sufficiency for \(k\ge3\).** Suppose the three stated truncated parameters agree, with a fixed matching of the two side labels. Maintain an injection between currently occupied components on the two boards, together with a corresponding orientation of their sides and injective matches of occupied vertices. This gives a partial graph isomorphism. When one paired pebble is lifted, at most \(k-1\) pebbles remain. A move into an already occupied component and side can be answered by an unoccupied vertex in its partner side. If its size is less than \(k\), the two side sizes agree exactly; if both sizes are at least \(k\), at most \(k-1\) vertices in that side are occupied. For a move into a fresh component, use a fresh component in the other graph, possible by exact equality when the component counts are below \(k\), and otherwise by having at least \(k\) components with at most \(k-1\) currently occupied. Reorient the matched sides only when a new component is introduced. These rules are permanent, hence establish equivalence at every quantifier depth.

**Necessity of the component count.** Suppose the counts differ after truncation. Without loss of generality, the smaller graph has \(m<k\) components and the larger has at least \(m+1\). Spoiler places \(m+1\) pebbles on vertices from distinct components of the larger graph. If any two replies are adjacent, Duplicator already loses. Otherwise, since the smaller graph has at most \(m\) components, two of the replies must lie on the same side of one component, although their corresponding vertices lie in different components. With \(k\ge3\), a third pebble position can be freed (or an unused pebble used) while retaining that pair. Spoiler now selects in the larger graph a neighbor of the first retained vertex. It is nonadjacent to the second, because those vertices lie in different components. No vertex in the smaller graph can be adjacent to one of the coalesced pair without also being adjacent to the other, a contradiction. This is a finite winning strategy with at most \(k\) pebbles.

**Necessity of the side sizes.** For each threshold \(h\) with \(3\le h\le k\), let \(c_h\in\{0,1,2\}\) count the two sides of a component having size at least \(h\). The unordered truncated pair is uniquely determined by the \(c_h\), since every side has size at least two. If two graphs have different side invariants, choose \(h\le k\) for which these counts differ. If one has no large side and the other has at least one, Spoiler begins with a vertex on a large side of the latter; Duplicator must answer on a small side. If one has one large side and the other has two, Spoiler begins on the small side of the former; Duplicator must answer on a large side of the latter. In both cases, Spoiler next plays on the large-side board, filling \(h\) pebbles with distinct vertices from the same side of the same component, including the already selected vertex. In the small-side board, Duplicator cannot put all \(h\) replies into that corresponding side. Since all the selected vertices are pairwise nonadjacent, replies cannot lie on opposite sides of one component. Some two replies therefore lie in different components. As \(h\ge3\), lift a third pebble and put it on a vertex of the opposite side in the large-side graph, adjacent to both corresponding retained vertices. Their replies in distinct components have no common neighbor, so Duplicator loses. This again uses at most \(k\) pebbles.

**Enumeration and defining width.** At width \(k\ge3\), the truncated component count ranges over \(1,\ldots,k\), and each of the two side counts ranges over \(2,\ldots,k\), modulo swapping. There are \(k\binom{k}{2}=k^2(k-1)/2\) invariants; every one occurs. The coordinatewise smallest triple representing each invariant gives the unique minimum-order graph. Its maximum possible order is \(k(k+k)=2k^2\). A graph is isolated in its relative \(k\)-variable theory precisely when each of its three parameters is less than \(k\), giving least width \(1+\max\{n,a,b\}\). In particular, the examples \(G(k;2,2)\) and \(G(k+1;2,2)\) witness strictness from width \(k\) to width \(k+1\) for every \(k\ge2\).

## Verification
The standalone standard-library program `verify.py` implements non-counting \(k\)-tuple colour refinement, with **sets**, not multiplicities, of replacement colours. It checks every unordered pair among the specified small parameter cases for widths two, three and four. The recorded deterministic output in `verify_output.txt` reports \(45\), \(136\), and \(55\) comparisons, respectively, with zero discrepancies and nine width-three classes among the tested representative and above-threshold instances. These are finite corroborations, not a replacement for the permanent pebble strategy and Spoiler arguments proving the infinite-parameter theorem. Replay with `python verify.py`.

## Relationship to prior work
Finite-variable logic and ordinary versus counting pebble games are standard, as surveyed by Pikhurko and Verbitsky and studied by Grohe and Otto and by Dawar and Holm. The present classification is specifically about *disconnected homogeneous biclique unions* and does not assert novelty of the general pebble-game method. The single-component slice is a special case of complete multipartite/Ferrers-graph analyses; the \(K_{2,2}\) slice is a union of four-cycles. Neither slice alone supplies the simultaneous sharp three-parameter classification, where component, left-side and right-side counts all saturate at width \(k\), with no loss for recovering invisible components and sides.

## Limitations
The claim does not cover components with a singleton side, arbitrary mixtures of different component shapes, arbitrary bipartite graphs or counting-variable logic. The classification is relative to one common biclique shape repeated across all components. Historical priority of the combined result has not been independently established; a stronger composition theorem might subsume the claim. There has been no independent mathematical audit.
