# Scientific review

## Correctness — PASS
The claim holds for each even \(n\ge4\), any half-height \(h>0\), and Haar-uniform central planes. The proof counts intersected vertical and cap edges, identifies the top two absolute vertex projections by regular-polygon symmetry, integrates independent altitude and azimuth distributions with exact Haar normalization, and proves a unique derivative sign transition. Boundary cases at both height endpoints are limits, not included. The expansion in \(\pi/n\) verifies the asymptotic constant. Direct independent edge-sign enumeration over 2,850,000 section classifications and numerical quadrature across five orders agree with the closed expression; a checker is included. The argument is analytical and does not rely on finite experimentation for the all-order statement.

## Originality — PASS (historical-priority qualification)
The exact probability and the all-even-order unique optimum have not been found in the checked accessible sources or semantic-index results. Padrol–Pfeifle addresses possible polygonal sections and intersection complexity, not probabilistic orientation laws. Kim–Yaskin–Zvavitch studies area distributions; area data do not directly imply a vertex-count event. De Loera–Lopez-Campos–Torres gives counting and combinatorial slice-sequence results without the claimed all-even regular-prism formula. Published results for cubic/rectangular-box sections cover the \(n=4\) case but not \(n\ge6\); results for triangular prisms concern odd \(n=3\). A plausible 1995 stereological regular-prism article was accessible only as an abstract; it emphasizes simulation and noncentral random sections, but a full-text implication comparison is not possible. Thus the precise older-source priority is an explicit residual risk, not an assertion of exhaustive uniqueness.

## Mathematical value — PASS
The maximally complex \((n+2)\)-vertex section is a natural combinatorial event for an \(n\)-gonal prism. The theorem gives an exact probability for every even order, an explicit unique optimizing aspect ratio, and a universal inverse-quadratic decay law. It identifies a structural distinction: the square optimum is uniform (cubic) whereas the six-sided optimum has full height \(\sqrt5\) in circumradius units, not unit edge height. These features are not simple reformulations of earlier expected-size or shadow results.
