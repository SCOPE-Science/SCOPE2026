# Scientific review

## Correctness

PASS. The claim fixes the initial edge, a distinguished initial vertex, the affine attachment kernel with \(\delta>-1\), and the total leaf count. The proof reconstructs exact conditional increments, including the nontrivial event that the chosen parent is simultaneously the root and a leaf. This yields three exact mean recurrences and the covariance recurrence. The sign proof checks the initial boundary \(n=2,3\) separately. The complete gamma-product expression for the root mean and the normalized summability bound for the residual establish the infinite covariance asymptotic. The included finite exhaustive calculations only check small cases, rather than proving asymptotics.

## Originality

PASS on the checked evidence, with residual historical-priority risk. Hartle–Krapivsky (2026, arXiv:2602.01021) treat the global number of leaves and degree–leafdegree population statistics, not the cross-moment between one specified old vertex and total leaves. Krapivsky–Redner (2002, arXiv:cond-mat/0207107), including their Appendix D, treat moments of *population counts* of fixed-degree classes; an initial vertex's changing degree is not a fixed-degree population count. The public result *Equal-share covariance of root degree and leaves in a random recursive tree* has the same two statistic names but selects parents uniformly, so its result cannot directly cover the degree-proportional model and its continuum of affine offsets. A broader, unidentified joint-moment theorem might subsume this statement and is not excluded merely by searches.

## Mathematical value

PASS. The sharp covariance determines a mathematically natural cross-scale dependency between a founder hub and the global fringe, distinguishing growth of a single special vertex from total leaf fluctuations. Strict positivity holds at every nondegenerate finite tree size, and the power exponent and normalized constant vary explicitly with the attachment offset. The result is not presented as a Gaussian limit, a general tree-centrality correlation law, or a theorem about maximum-degree vertices.
