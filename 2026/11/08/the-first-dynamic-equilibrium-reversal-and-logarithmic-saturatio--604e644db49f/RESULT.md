# The first dynamic–equilibrium reversal and logarithmic saturation law for ladder adsorption

## Finding

Consider random sequential adsorption on a two-row square ladder with integer blockade range \(b\ge1\). Excited vertices must have graph distance at least \(b+1\). The *dynamic* infinite-length jamming density, \(\rho_\infty^{(b)}\), is sampled by sequentially accepting uniformly proposed compatible vertices. The *equilibrium* infinite-length density, \(\rho_\star^{(b)}\), is the limiting density under the uniform distribution on maximal admissible configurations, or equivalently the maximizer of the published configurational entropy. Define the normalized densities
\[
J_b=2b\rho_\infty^{(b)},\qquad S_b=2b\rho_\star^{(b)}.
\]

**Theorem (first strict reversal and quantitative saturation).** For every integer \(1\le b\le9\), \(J_b>S_b\); at \(b=10\), \(S_{10}>J_{10}\). Thus the first positive integer blockade range for which the equilibrium packing density exceeds the dynamic packing density is exactly **10**. The narrow boundary case \(b=9\) is certified, not inferred from rounded numerical data.

For \(W\) the principal Lambert function defined by \(W(x)e^{W(x)}=x\), the equilibrium density also satisfies
\[
S_b=\frac{W(2b)}{1+W(2b)}+O\!\left(\frac{\log b}{b}\right)
\quad (b\to\infty),
\]
so its deficit from unit normalized coverage has the quantitative expansion
\[
1-S_b=\frac1{\log(2b)}+
\frac{\log\log(2b)-1}{\log^2(2b)}+
O\!\left(\frac{(\log\log b)^2}{(\log b)^3}\right).
\]
In particular, \((1-S_b)\log b\to1\). The infinite-length dynamic normalized density tends instead to the established Rényi parking constant \(R\approx0.7475979202\), as proved in the source paper.

## Assumptions and scope

The ladder has two rows and an infinite-length thermodynamic limit, the blockade range \(b\) is a positive integer, and the graph-distance compatibility rule is exactly that of Došlić–Puljiz–Šebek–Žubrinić. Dynamic configurations are generated by sequential random adsorption; equilibrium configurations are equally weighted among maximal configurations. The two probability ensembles must not be conflated. The statement is about *densities* and their normalization by \(2b\), not about the distribution of finite-ladder occupation counts.

The source [1], first publicly released 2023-12-05, proves these foundational exact formulas. If \(t_b\in(0,1)\) is the unique root of
\[
t_b^b\frac{(1+t_b)(1-t_b^b)}{1-t_b}=1,
\]
then [1, equations (3.9), (4.4)] gives
\[
\frac1{S_b}=\frac{2t_b}{b(1-t_b^2)}+
\frac{1-2t_b^b}{1-t_b^b}.
\tag{1}
\]
With \(H_b=\sum_{j=1}^b1/j\) and
\[
P_b(u)=-2\sum_{j=1}^b\frac{1-(1-u)^j}{j}
+\frac{1-(1-u)^b}{b},
\]
the same paper [1, equation (4.2)] yields
\[
J_b=\frac b2\exp(1/b-2H_b)+b\int_0^1\exp(P_b(u))\,du.
\tag{2}
\]
Equations (1)–(2) are credited as previously known. New here are the certified *minimal crossing index* and the rate/expansion of equilibrium saturation for this ladder model.

## Proof

**1. Exact algebraic reduction.** Set \(q_b=t_b^b\) and \(w_b=q_b(1-q_b)\). The polynomial root equation in (1) is equivalent to
\[
\frac{1-t_b}{1+t_b}=q_b(1-q_b)=w_b,
\qquad t_b=\frac{1-w_b}{1+w_b}.
\tag{3}
\]
Consequently,
\[
-\log q_b=2b\operatorname{artanh}(w_b),\qquad
\frac1{S_b}=1-\frac{q_b}{1-q_b}+
\frac{1-w_b^2}{2bw_b}.
\tag{4}
\]
All transformations are exact for every integer \(b\ge1\), with \(0<q_b,w_b<1\).

**2. Asymptotics.** The polynomial relation yields \(q_b(1+q_b)\le1\), hence \(0<q_b\le(\sqrt5-1)/2\). Since \(\operatorname{artanh}(w)\ge w\), equation (4) implies
\[
-\log q_b\ge2bq_b(1-q_b).
\]
Putting \(x_b=2bq_b\), and using the uniform upper bound on \(q_b\), gives \(x_b=O(\log b)\), therefore \(q_b=O(\log b/b)\to0\). The Taylor estimate \(\operatorname{artanh}(w)=w+O(w^3)\) in (4) yields
\[
-\log q_b=x_b+O(x_bq_b),\qquad
x_b+\log x_b=\log(2b)+O((\log b)^2/b).
\tag{5}
\]
The function \(x\mapsto x+\log x\) is strictly increasing with derivative at least 1. Its positive inverse at \(\log(2b)\) is \(W(2b)\). Thus
\[
x_b=W(2b)+O((\log b)^2/b).
\tag{6}
\]
Using \(w_b=q_b(1-q_b)\) in the reciprocal formula (4) gives
\[
\frac1{S_b}=1+\frac1{x_b}+O(q_b),
\quad S_b=\frac{x_b}{1+x_b}+O(q_b).
\]
Together with (6), this proves the claimed Lambert function approximation. The standard expansion
\(W(z)=\log z-\log\log z+O(\log\log z/\log z)\), substituted into \(1/(1+W(z))\), proves the displayed two-term deficit expansion. The asymptotic error \(O(\log b/b)\) is smaller than the displayed logarithmic remainder.

**3. Finite, exact rational separation.** We certify all ten integer comparisons via a complete *finite* calculation with explicit analytic error bounds. The polynomial in (1) is strictly increasing on \((0,1)\); it has a root between \(1/2\) and 1. Fifty-one dyadic bisections using exact rational arithmetic give \(a_b<t_b<c_b\). The first term in (1) increases in \(t\), while the second decreases, so substituting opposite endpoints gives rigorous rational lower and upper bounds on \(S_b\).

For the dynamic integral, the function \(f_b(u)=\exp(P_b(u))\) satisfies \(0<f_b\le1\), \(|P_b'|\le2b\), and \(|P_b''|\le2b^2\) on \([0,1]\). Therefore \(|f_b''|\le6b^2\). The composite trapezoid approximation with \(N\) equal intervals gives the rigorous error bound
\[
\left|b\int_0^1 f_b(u)\,du-bT_{b,N}\right|
\le\frac{b^3}{2N^2}.
\tag{7}
\]
At every rational mesh point, \(v=-P_b(u)\ge0\) is rational. Taylor's theorem supplies rational endpoint bounds
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{39}\frac{(-v)^k}{k!}
\le e^{-v}\le
\sum_{k=0}^{40}\frac{(-v)^k}{k!}.
\tag{8}
\]
The same bound encloses \(\exp(1/b-2H_b)\). Thus (1), (2), (7) and (8), combined with the dyadic root enclosures, produce **exact rational bounds without floating-point sign decisions**. The complete standalone checker is `ladder_gap_checker.py`; it recomputes each rational bound rather than trusting a log.

For \(b=1,\ldots,6\) use \(N=256\), for \(b=7\) use 512, for \(b=8\) use 1024, for \(b=9\) use 8192, and for \(b=10\) use 2048. All ten rational interval comparisons are strict. Especially, outward-rounded decimal presentations of the certified enclosures are

The tight \(b=9\) case is therefore distinguished rigorously. Combining all ten signs proves that \(b=10\) is the first strict reversal.

## Relationship to prior work

Reference [1] derives both exact density formulas, tabulates \(b=1,2,3,4,5,10,100\), and proves \(S_b\to1\), \(J_b\to R\). Its table already displays the reversal between \(b=5\) and \(b=10\). The unreported boundary cases \(b=6,7,8,9\), the proof that the *first* reversal is at 10, and the quantitative two-row equilibrium asymptotic above are the additions. No new derivation of the known density formulas is claimed.

Reference [2] develops a Lambert-\(W\) scaling for **one-dimensional** constrained jammed configurations, so the Lambert function mechanism itself is prior art. Reference [3] likewise concerns the one-dimensional Rydberg model. In contrast, (1) is the specific two-row ladder equation with the additional \(1+t_b\) factor, and (2) the specific ladder dynamic integral. Neither one-dimensional result supplies the integer crossing or the displayed ladder coefficient without additional analysis.

## Limitations

The result establishes the **first** integer reversal, not the stronger assertion \(S_b>J_b\) for *every* \(b\ge10\); later recrossings are not excluded by the finite certificate. The limit \(S_b\to1\) and \(J_b\to R\), already proved in [1], imply that equilibrium eventually exceeds dynamics, but that qualitative observation is not claimed here as new. The comparison concerns infinite-length mean densities and a fixed integer blockade range before taking \(b\to\infty\); no finite-length rates, fluctuation laws, physical anti-blockade effects, or independent originality certification are asserted. The argument uses published exact input formulas and is not a new derivation of those formulas.
