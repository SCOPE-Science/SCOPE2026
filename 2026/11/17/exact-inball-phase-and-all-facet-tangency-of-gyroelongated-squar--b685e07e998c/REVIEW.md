# Scientific assessment

## Correctness — PASS
All ten spherical vertices, the height domain, and every facet plane are explicit. The proof verifies sixteen supports and closes the facet list by Euler's bound, then uses the full symmetry group to equate the *unrestricted* inradius with the minimum origin-centered facet distance. A factored sign identity establishes the unique switch of the two branches; the monotonicity of each active branch proves the exact maximum. At the crossing, all sixteen unit normals give a unique incenter. The comparison with the published volume-maximizing height follows by a strict radical inequality. The independent-implementation numerical script verifies 620 finite geometry assertions, but does not replace the analytical proof.

## Originality — PASS, provisional historical priority
The closest primary source, Hoehner (arXiv:2611.10777v1), fixes the volume-maximizing ten-vertex configuration, its combinatorial facets, and its volume; it does not establish the *full varying-height inradius profile* or the all-facet tangency classification. Hardin–Sloane–Smith (1994) already gives the ten-point covering radius numerically to eight decimal places; therefore that numerical optimum and the general proposed configuration are explicitly acknowledged as prior work. Its numerical table alone does not imply the two-branch profile, proof of unique optimal height within the full symmetric family, or differing volume optimum. A related two-latitude-ring covering result omits both polar apices and covers another shape class. Search evidence is not a proof of global novelty; inaccessible older complete texts remain a priority risk.

## Mathematical value — PASS
This is a sharp complete classification of the inscribed-ball radius in the natural ten-vertex symmetric family singled out by a new global volume theorem. The mathematically meaningful distinction is that optimizing geometric roundness makes *all sixteen* facets simultaneously tangential, while optimizing volume selects a different height and strictly smaller inradius. The sharp contact transition and exact radical threshold are not an arbitrary numerical slice or a general theorem's trivial substitution. The numeric covering radius itself is known and is not presented as the contribution.
