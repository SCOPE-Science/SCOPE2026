# Sharp Fourier stability for the Japanese inradius theorem near regular polygons

## Finding

For every integer \(n\ge4\), the \(n\) differences of triangle-inradius sums across the **consecutive quadrilateral flips** in a small radial perturbation of the regular \(n\)-gon detect exactly the non-cocircular perturbations. More precisely, their linearization has a three-dimensional kernel generated by changing the common circle's center and radius, and its *smallest positive singular value* is
\[
\mu_n=\frac{4\sin(\pi/n)\sin(2\pi/n)\sin(3\pi/n)}{\cos(\pi/n)(1+\cos(\pi/n))(1+2\cos(\pi/n))}.
\]
This value is sharp for a local nonlinear error bound, with the least stable deformation carried by the second discrete Fourier harmonic. It obeys \(\mu_n\sim4\pi^3/n^3\) as \(n\to\infty\).

Specifically, if \(F(u)\) is the vector of these \(n\) flip differences, and \(\mathcal C_n\) is the local set of cocircular radial configurations, then \(F(u)=0\) exactly when \(u\in\mathcal C_n\) for all sufficiently small \(u\); moreover,
\[
\lim_{\delta\downarrow0}\inf_{\substack{0<\|u\|_2<\delta\\\operatorname{dist}(u,\mathcal C_n)>0}}
\frac{\|F(u)\|_2}{\operatorname{dist}(u,\mathcal C_n)}=\mu_n.
\]
The limiting infimum is attained asymptotically along the normalized second Fourier mode.

## Assumptions and scope

Let \(n\ge4\) be fixed; all vectors in this theorem are real. Write \(h=2\pi/n\), \(\theta_j=jh\), and \(e_j=(\cos\theta_j,\sin\theta_j)\), with indices interpreted modulo \(n\). For a sufficiently small \(u=(u_0,\ldots,u_{n-1})\in\mathbb R^n\), define the ordered strictly convex vertices
\[
p_j(u)=(1+u_j)e_j.
\]
For three noncollinear planar points, \(r(a,b,c)\) denotes their Euclidean triangle inradius, equivalently
\[
r(a,b,c)=\frac{|\det(b-a,c-a)|}{\|b-a\|+\|b-c\|+\|c-a\|}.
\]
Define each measured flip defect by
\[
F_j(u)=r(p_j,p_{j+1},p_{j+2})+r(p_j,p_{j+2},p_{j+3})
-r(p_j,p_{j+1},p_{j+3})-r(p_{j+1},p_{j+2},p_{j+3}).
\]
The local cocircular locus \(\mathcal C_n\) consists of the radial vectors arising from a circle of center \(q\) and radius \(R\), with \(q\) near the origin and \(R\) near \(1\):
\[
u_j(q,R)=q\cdot e_j+\sqrt{R^2-\|q\|^2+(q\cdot e_j)^2}-1.
\]
The distance in the theorem is ordinary Euclidean distance in \(\mathbb R^n\). In particular, there is no optimally selected scalar normalization hidden in the result. The statement is local in \(u\), not a global condition on arbitrary polygons.

## Proof

**1. Classical identity and the four-point first variation.** The Japanese theorem for cyclic quadrilaterals asserts that the sums of the inradii of the two possible pairs of triangles coincide. Therefore \(F\) is smooth near \(0\) and vanishes identically on \(\mathcal C_n\). The parameterization \(u(q,R)\) has tangent space
\[
K_n=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}\{(1)_j,(\cos\theta_j)_j,(\sin\theta_j)_j\}
\]
at \(0\); hence \(DF(0)\) annihilates \(K_n\).

It remains to calculate just one coefficient of \(DF(0)\). In the initial quadruple \((0,h,2h,3h)\), radially displace its first point and leave the other three fixed. For a triangle with points at angles \((0,bh,ch)\), \(1\le b<c\le3\), set
\[
P_{bc}=2\left[\sin(bh/2)+\sin((c-b)h/2)+\sin(ch/2)\right],
\quad A_{bc}=2\sin(bh/2)\sin((c-b)h/2)\sin(ch/2).
\]
These are respectively its original perimeter and unsigned area, with \(r_{bc}=2A_{bc}/P_{bc}\). Differentiating the determinant and the two changing edge lengths gives the radial derivative
\[
r'_{bc}(0)=\frac{\sin(bh)-\sin(ch)-r_{bc}[\sin(bh/2)+\sin(ch/2)]}{P_{bc}}.
\]
Therefore the first coefficient of \(DF_0(0)\) is
\[
r'_{12}(0)+r'_{23}(0)-r'_{13}(0)=-\kappa_n,
\qquad
\kappa_n=\frac{1}{2c(1+c)(1+2c)},\quad c=\cos(\pi/n).
\]
The last identity follows by substitution \(t=\tan(\pi/(2n))\): the left-hand side simplifies identically to
\[
-\frac{(1+t^2)^3}{4(1-t^2)(3-t^2)}=-\kappa_n.
\]
The four-coordinate row of \(DF_0(0)\) is perpendicular to the restrictions of the vectors \(1,\cos\theta,\sin\theta\) to four distinct circle vertices. These three restricted vectors are independent; their perpendicular complement has dimension one. Thus the computed first entry uniquely determines the row. Writing \(a=1+2\cos h\), one obtains
\[
(DF(0)u)_j=\kappa_n[-u_j+a u_{j+1}-a u_{j+2}+u_{j+3}].\tag{1}
\]
Rotational symmetry gives this formula for all \(j\).

**2. Exact Fourier singular spectrum.** Let \((Su)_j=u_{j+1}\). Formula (1) factors as
\[
DF(0)=\kappa_n(S-I)(S-e^{ih}I)(S-e^{-ih}I).
\]
The unitary discrete Fourier basis diagonalizes the cyclic shift. For \(k=0,\ldots,n-1\), its singular value is
\[
\sigma_k=8\kappa_n\left|\sin(k\pi/n)\sin((k-1)\pi/n)\sin((k+1)\pi/n)\right|.
\]
Exactly the modes \(k=0,1,n-1\) vanish. If \(2\le k\le\lfloor n/2\rfloor\), set \(x=\pi/n\) and \(s=\sin(kx)\). The product of the three nonnegative sine factors becomes \(s(s^2-\sin^2x)\). It strictly increases with \(k\) on this range. Conjugate symmetry covers the remaining frequencies. Hence the minimum positive singular value is attained exactly at \(k=2,n-2\), has the displayed value \(\mu_n\), and \(DF(0)\) has rank \(n-3\). The stated \(n^{-3}\) asymptotics follow by expanding the three sines and \(\kappa_n\to1/12\).

**3. Local nonlinear rigidity.** The formula for \(u(q,R)\) is a smooth embedded three-dimensional manifold near \(0\), because the three tangent vectors spanning \(K_n\) are independent. The differential of \(F\) has rank \(n-3\) at \(0\). Choose \(n-3\) output coordinates with independent differentials; the implicit-function theorem makes their common local zero set a three-dimensional manifold. This zero set contains \(\mathcal C_n\), which has the same dimension and tangent space. By a local-coordinate uniqueness argument, these two submanifolds agree near \(0\); therefore \(F(u)=0\) if and only if \(u\in\mathcal C_n\) sufficiently close to \(0\).

For any nearby \(u\), let \(v\in\mathcal C_n\) be a nearest point. In a fixed tubular neighborhood, \(u-v\) is normal to \(T_v\mathcal C_n\), and \(F(v)=0\). Smoothness gives
\[
F(u)=DF(v)(u-v)+O(\|u-v\|_2^2).
\]
As \(v\to0\), the least singular value of the restriction of \(DF(v)\) to the normal space of \(\mathcal C_n\) tends to \(\mu_n\). Consequently, the ratio \(\|F(u)\|_2/\operatorname{dist}(u,\mathcal C_n)\) has lower limit at least \(\mu_n\). Conversely, take \(u=\varepsilon w_2\), with \(w_2\) any real unit vector in the second Fourier eigenspace. Then \(w_2\perp K_n\), so \(\operatorname{dist}(u,\mathcal C_n)=\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^2)\), while \(\|F(u)\|_2=\mu_n\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^2)\). This yields the exact sharp limit.

It independently computes finite-difference Jacobian entries, Fourier singular values, and second-harmonic asymptotic ratios for \(n=4,5,6,7,11,30\). ## Relationship to prior work

Reyes (2002) proves the cyclic-quadrilateral Japanese identity by Thébault's theorem, and Minculete–Barbu–Szöllősy (2012) give additional geometric and weighted inradius identities. Lambert (1994) proves a global Delaunay inradius-sum extremal theorem. A separate published classification of separable inradius objectives over Delaunay triangulations establishes affine rigidity of the **function applied to inradii**, rather than infinitesimal geometric rigidity of near-cyclic polygons. Those statements provide the starting equality but do not specify which finitely many local flips suffice near the regular polygon, their complete Jacobian and Fourier spectrum, or the sharp nonlinear coercivity constant. The result here is a local quantitative converse and condition-number computation, not a new proof of the classical Japanese identity. Prior-art searches are not a definitive priority certificate.

## Limitations

The number of vertices is fixed for the local limit; no neighborhood size uniform in \(n\) is claimed. The perturbations are radial at equally spaced directions and the metric on the parameter vector is the unweighted Euclidean norm. The formula does not claim global rigidity of arbitrary nonconvex or distant polygons, and it does not provide a global error constant for nonregular cyclic polygons. Lambert's full 1994 proceedings paper was not obtained during this comparison. Possible folklore quantitative variants are a residual historical-priority risk. Independent validation has not been performed.
