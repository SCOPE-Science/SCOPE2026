# Self-review

## Correctness — PASS
The squared distance formula is reconstructed from line projections and its discrete Fourier decomposition. For four or more regularly spaced sites, the first harmonic cannot be cancelled by the second. For three sites they alias, and the exact polar curves follow. Substitution gives a positive quadratic in \(\tan(3t)\), so all critical points, boundary cases and equal-radius center counts are accounted for analytically. The independent finite regression program completed 2,796 tests; it is not treated as the infinite proof.

## Originality — PASS (provisional priority)
Everett et al. (2007/2009) already established that a three-line revolution-hyperboloid trisector comprises a cubic and a line, with four real branches. Papadopoulou et al. (2026) established universal upper bounds on local extrema and a degree-12 procedure. Hemmer et al. (2010) give a general exact diagram construction. These results were inspected and are credited. The surviving novelty claim concerns the explicit complete critical-distance law, the sharp 1/2/5/8 all-radius census, and the cyclic \(n=3\) versus \(n\ge4\) classification. Targeted external literature and published-record searches did not identify equivalent coverage. Historical priority remains provisional, particularly for older analytic geometry treatments of hyperboloids of revolution.

## Mathematical value — PASS
The exact count of centers of fixed-radius spheres tangent to multiple skew lines is naturally tied to the critical levels of three-dimensional Voronoi trisectors. The family is geometrically canonical and has a transparent symmetry group. The theorem classifies **all** numbers of cyclic sites and **every** radius, with a nontrivial threshold and a complete, reproducible analytic derivation rather than one isolated numerical example.

## Scientific limitations and closest sources
The known trisector topology is explicitly not claimed as new. The theorem does not address unequal pitch, nonequispaced sites, arbitrary skew-line arrangements, or generic maxima. The closest sources are Everett et al., *The Voronoi Diagram of Three Lines* (2009), https://perso.lip6.fr/Mohab.Safey/Articles/Voronoi.pdf; Papadopoulou et al., *A Collapse Process for Farthest Voronoi Diagrams of Lines in Three Dimensions* (2026), https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.29865; and Hemmer et al. (2010), https://www.cgl.cs.tau.ac.il/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/voronoi_lines.pdf.
