# A descending cumulant-threshold hierarchy for angular active-particle trapping

## Finding

Let \(T_u\) be the first angular escape time of a planar active Brownian heading from a symmetric capture sector, started at the fractional angular offset \(u\in[0,1)\). Compare it to an exponential trapping time \(E_u\) generated by uniform-angle Poisson reorientations, with the reorientation rate selected to give **the same mean trapping time**. For every integer order \(m\ge2\), the ratio of the \(m\)-th cumulants of these two times is
\[
R_m(u)=C_m\frac{1-u^{2m}}{(1-u^2)^m},\qquad
C_m=\sum_{j=0}^\infty\left(\frac{8}{\pi^2(2j+1)^2}\right)^m.
\]
Each ratio increases strictly across one unique threshold \(u_m\in(0,1)\), with \(R_m(u)<1\) below and \(R_m(u)>1\) above. The thresholds form a **strictly decreasing hierarchy**
\[
\frac1{\sqrt5}=u_2>u_3>u_4>\cdots>u_\infty
=\sqrt{1-\frac8{\pi^2}},
\]
and, putting \(q_0=8/\pi^2\) and \(v_\infty=1-q_0\), obey the sharp large-order law
\[
u_m^2-v_\infty\sim\frac{q_0}{m}v_\infty^m\quad(m\to\infty).
\]
The same limiting angle \(u_\infty\) is the sharp changeover in which process has the slower exponential far tail. Thus, for the nonempty window \(u_\infty<u<u_2\), Brownian angular escape has smaller variance than the mean-matched Poisson trapping clock **but a heavier late-time survival tail**. The order-two threshold by itself is not asserted to be new.

## Assumptions and scope

Fix angular diffusivity \(D>0\), symmetric angular half-width \(a\in(0,\pi)\), and initial heading \(\theta_0\in(-a,a)\). The Brownian heading has generator \(D\partial_\theta^2\) and is killed upon first exiting \((-a,a)\), with \(u=|\theta_0|/a\). The competing run-and-tumble heading is redrawn uniformly on \(( -\pi,\pi ]\) at independent Poisson times of rate \(\gamma\); a redraw outside \((-a,a)\) ends trapping, while a redraw inside preserves the bound episode. Successful redraws form a thinned Poisson clock of rate \(\gamma(1-a/\pi)\). For every \(u\), choose
\[
\gamma=\frac{2D}{a^2(1-u^2)(1-a/\pi)},\qquad
\mu(u)=\mathbb ET_u=\mathbb EE_u=\frac{a^2(1-u^2)}{2D}.
\]
This is a comparison at **matched mean**, with \(\gamma\) calibrated separately for each initial orientation. The processes are single uninterrupted angular wall-binding episodes; neither model includes subsequent channel excursions, angular drift, or finite-duration tumbles. All cumulants are ordinary cumulants of strictly positive times (not standardized central moments).

## Proof

**1. Exact transform, independent-series law and cumulants.** The Dirichlet boundary problem \(Df''=sf\), \(f(\pm a)=1\), gives
\[
\mathbb E e^{-sT_u}=\frac{\cosh(ua\sqrt{s/D})}{\cosh(a\sqrt{s/D})}.
\]
Set \(\lambda_j=D\pi^2(2j+1)^2/(4a^2)\). Euler's entire-product formula for \(\cosh\) gives
\[
\mathbb E e^{-sT_u}=\prod_{j\ge0}\frac{\lambda_j+u^2s}{\lambda_j+s}.
\]
Each factor is the Laplace transform of the independent product \(B_jW_j\), where \(B_j\) is Bernoulli with success probability \(1-u^2\), and \(W_j\) is exponential of rate \(\lambda_j\). The nonnegative sum \(\sum_jB_jW_j\) converges almost surely because its expectation is finite; uniqueness of Laplace transforms yields the exact distributional representation. Differentiating its absolutely convergent cumulant-generating expansion near zero, for every integer \(m\ge1\), gives
\[
\kappa_m(T_u)=(m-1)!(1-u^{2m})\sum_{j\ge0}\lambda_j^{-m}.
\]
Writing \(q_j=8/[\pi^2(2j+1)^2]\) and \(\lambda_j^{-1}=(a^2/(2D))q_j\) proves the formula. The matched exponential has cumulants \((m-1)!\mu^m\).

**2. One crossing for every order.** Let \(v=u^2\). Direct differentiation shows
\[
\frac{d}{dv}\log R_m(\sqrt v)=
\frac{m(1-v^{m-1})}{(1-v)(1-v^m)}>0\quad(0\le v<1,\ m\ge2).
\]
At \(v=0\), \(R_m=C_m<1\), while at \(v\uparrow1\), \(R_m\) diverges. Hence exactly one crossing \(v_m=u_m^2\) exists. The classical odd reciprocal-power sums give \(C_1=1\), \(C_2=2/3\), \(C_3=8/15\). Solving the second-order crossing yields \(v_2=1/5\).

**3. Strict ordering and spectral limiting threshold.** Put \(q_0=8/\pi^2\), \(v_0=1-q_0\), \(a_j=(2j+1)^{-2}\) for \(j\ge1\), and \(H_m=\sum_{j\ge1}a_j^m\). Since \(1/9<v_0<1/5\), and
\[
H_2=\frac{\pi^4}{96}-1<\frac45v_0^2,
\]
we have \(H_m\le H_2(1/9)^{m-2}<\tfrac45v_0^m\) for all integers \(m\ge2\). These explicit constant inequalities follow, for example, from \(3.14<\pi<22/7\), with strict rational bounds. For \(v\in[v_0,1/5]\) define \(P_m(v)=(1+H_m)(1-v^m)\). The bounds give \(0<P_m(v)<1\). Moreover,
\[
P_{m+1}(v)-P_m(v)
=(1+H_m)v^m(1-v)-(H_m-H_{m+1})(1-v^{m+1})
>v^m(1-v)-H_m>0.
\]
Consequently \(P_{m+1}(v)^{1/(m+1)}>P_m(v)^{1/m}\): both the base increases and the exponent decreases while the base stays below one. Therefore
\[
R_m(\sqrt v)^{1/m}=\frac{q_0}{1-v}P_m(v)^{1/m}
\]
increases strictly with integer \(m\ge2\) throughout that interval. At \(v=v_0\) every \(R_m<1\); at \(v=1/5\), \(R_2=1\) and all higher ratios exceed one. The uniqueness of crossings now gives \(v_2=1/5>v_3>\cdots>v_0\). For fixed \(v>v_0\), \(R_m(\sqrt v)^{1/m}\to q_0/(1-v)>1\), since \(H_m,v^m\to0\). This proves \(v_m\downarrow v_0\).

**4. Sharp large-order approach.** The root equation reads
\[
1-v_m=q_0\big[(1+H_m)(1-v_m^m)\big]^{1/m}.
\]
Uniformly on \([v_0,1/5]\), \(H_m=O(9^{-m})\) and \(v_m^m=O(5^{-m})\), so taking logarithms first gives \(v_m-v_0=O(5^{-m}/m)\). As \(1/9<v_0\), it follows that \(H_m=o(v_0^m)\), and \(v_m^m\sim v_0^m\). One further logarithmic expansion yields \(v_m-v_0\sim q_0v_0^m/m\).

**5. The far-tail inversion.** The standard Dirichlet spectral expansion for heat survival on \((-a,a)\) has strictly positive principal coefficient for \(u<1\), giving
\[
\Pr(T_u>t)\sim\frac4\pi\cos\left(\frac{\pi u}{2}\right)
\exp\left(-\frac{D\pi^2}{4a^2}t\right).
\]
Meanwhile \(\Pr(E_u>t)=\exp[-2Dt/(a^2(1-u^2))]\). The Brownian principal decay is smaller exactly when \(1-u^2<8/\pi^2\), equivalently \(u>u_\infty\), so Brownian's far survival is then exponentially larger; below this threshold it is exponentially smaller. At exact equality, the leading coefficient \((4/\pi)\cos(\pi u_\infty/2)<1\), making Brownian survival asymptotically smaller by a constant factor. Since \(u_\infty<u_2\), the stated variance–tail inversion window is nonempty.

## Relationship to prior work

Moen, Olsen, Rønning and Angheluta (arXiv:2208.00806v1, 2022-08-01) derive the two competing angular trapping first-passage laws and establish that uniform angular tumble escapes produce exponential survival; their first-passage transforms lead to the formula above. That paper does **not** state the strictly nested all-order cumulant thresholds, sharp spectral accumulation law, or matched-mean variance–tail inversion window. A 2026 published analysis of event-time reversal in an exponentially killed angular Brownian wall episode derives the individual Brownian exit-time variance and explicitly identifies the second-order threshold \(u_2=1/\sqrt5\), so this isolated threshold is **excluded** from the originality claim. Bressloff (arXiv:2302.09105v1) studies a competing angular escape/absorption event and conditional means, rather than the all-cumulant comparison with an independently reorienting Poisson heading. These are comparisons of mathematical implications, not merely titles. Related spectral-exit and exponential-factorization literature may contain an equivalent all-order inequality under different terminology, and independent priority assessment remains necessary.

## Limitations

The theorem is an exact statement for the one-dimensional angular sector and uniform-heading Poisson redrawing at matched mean. It does not include translational motion away from a wall, partial tumbles, biased/redirection laws, nonexponential clocks, or later returns. The strict cumulant hierarchy is limited to integer orders \(m\ge2\); the threshold convergence is in cumulant order, not elapsed time. The exponential far-tail comparison concerns large-time asymptotics; it is not a claim of all-time stochastic ordering. The lower-dimensional analytical transforms and second-order crossing are inherited ingredients, not novel results. Further literature may dominate the hierarchy through a general theorem on sums of thinned exponentials.
