# A discontinuous two-date vaccination optimum under gamma-mixture epidemic pressure

## Finding
Let \(\varepsilon>0\) be arbitrary, and let an initially susceptible individual face the externally prescribed first-infection hazard
\[
\lambda_A(t)=\varepsilon t\bigl(Ae^{-20t}+e^{-t/4}\bigr),\qquad t\ge 0.
\]
A single perfectly effective-at-administration dose is given at a freely selected time \(\tau\ge0\). For \(t\ge\tau\), its instantaneous efficacy is \(e^{-(t-\tau)}\); for \(t<\tau\), efficacy is zero. Thus post-dose susceptibility, conditional on still being uninfected, is \(1-e^{-(t-\tau)}\). There is no vaccine influence on the prescribed external force of infection.

**Theorem.** For every \(A\in[100,500]\), the probability of remaining uninfected for all time, as a function of \(\tau\ge0\), has exactly three positive stationary times, all simple: one each in \((0,1/20)\), \((1/20,1)\), and \((1,4)\). Write these \(\tau_1(A)<\tau_2(A)<\tau_3(A)\). The first and third are strict local maxima; the second is a strict local minimum. There exists exactly one \(A_c\in(100,500)\) for which the two maxima tie globally. For \(100\le A<A_c\) the *unique* globally optimal time is \(\tau_3(A)\); at \(A=A_c\), *exactly* \(\tau_1(A_c)\) and \(\tau_3(A_c)\) are globally optimal; and for \(A_c<A\le500\) the *unique* globally optimal time is \(\tau_1(A)\). In particular the optimal timing jumps by more than \(19/20\) time units when \(A\) crosses \(A_c\), for every \(\varepsilon>0\).

The transition is characterized without numerical optimization by the unique zero on \((100,500)\) of
\[
D(A)=J_A(\tau_1(A))-J_A(\tau_3(A)),
\quad J_A(t)= A e^{-20t}\left(\frac{t}{21}+\frac1{441}\right)
+e^{-t/4}\left(\frac{4t}{5}+\frac{16}{25}\right).
\]
For orientation only, \(A_c\approx350.480179990\), with the earlier optimum near \(0.004472463\) and the later optimum near \(3.2\). These approximations are not used to establish existence, uniqueness, or strict inequalities.

## Assumptions and scope
* One initially susceptible individual and a nonhomogeneous Poisson first-infection mechanism; infection is absorbing. The hazard is a prescribed external input, not a prevalence generated by a coupled epidemic model.
* Time is measured in units such that dose efficacy wanes exponentially with rate one. One dose with immediate efficacy one is given at time \(\tau\), even if the person may already have become infected; infection-free probability automatically accounts for infections before \(\tau\).
* The hazard amplitude \(\varepsilon\) may be any strictly positive real number. The relative fast-wave intensity \(A\) is restricted to \([100,500]\); no classification outside that interval is claimed.
* The result optimizes eventual infection-free probability, not severe disease, the impact of vaccination on community transmission, cost-effectiveness, dose availability, or real vaccination guidelines.

## Proof
**1. Exact reduction.** The integral of baseline infection hazard is
\[
H_0=\int_0^\infty\lambda_A(t)\,dt
=\varepsilon\left(\frac A{400}+16\right).
\]
For a dose at \(\tau\), prevented cumulative hazard equals \(\varepsilon J_A(\tau)\), since
\[
\int_\tau^\infty s e^{-\beta s}e^{-(s-\tau)}\,ds
=e^{-\beta\tau}\left(\frac\tau{\beta+1}+\frac1{(\beta+1)^2}\right)
\quad(\beta>0).
\]
Therefore the exact eventual infection-free probability is
\[
P_A(\tau)=\exp\!\left[-\varepsilon\left(\frac A{400}+16-J_A(\tau)\right)\right].
\]
Because \(\varepsilon>0\), its maxima and stationary points agree exactly with those of \(J_A\).

**2. Exactly three stationary points.** The prescribed infection force truly has two distinct waves throughout the stated parameter range: its derivative divided by \(\varepsilon\) equals \(Ae^{-20t}(1-20t)+e^{-t/4}(1-t/4)\), with signs \(+,-,+,-\) at \(t=0,1/10,1,5\), respectively. Multiplying this derivative by \(e^{20t}\) and applying two Rolle steps, just as below, bounds its zeros by three; therefore it has exactly two local maxima. This verifies that the two-wave terminology describes a property of the model, not merely its two summands. Direct differentiation of the prevented-hazard objective yields
\[
F_A(t):=J_A'(t)=
Ae^{-20t}\left(\frac1{441}-\frac{20t}{21}\right)
+e^{-t/4}\left(\frac{16}{25}-\frac t5\right).
\]
For every \(A\in[100,500]\), one has \(F_A(0)>0\), \(F_A(1/20)<0\), \(F_A(1)>0\), and \(F_A(4)<0\). Explicitly,
\[
F_A(1/20)=-\frac{20A}{441e}+\frac{63}{100}e^{-1/80}<0,
\quad F_A(1)=-\frac{419A}{441}e^{-20}+\frac{11}{25}e^{-1/4}>0.
\]
The first inequality follows using \(A\ge100\) and \(e<3\); the second follows using \(A\le500\), \(e^{20}>2^{20}\), and \(e^{1/4}<2\). At four both parenthesized terms are negative. Hence the intermediate value theorem supplies a stationary point in each of the three stated disjoint intervals.

There cannot be four distinct zeros. Indeed put \(G_A(t)=e^{20t}F_A(t)\), so
\[
G_A(t)=A\left(\frac1{441}-\frac{20t}{21}\right)
+e^{79t/4}\left(\frac{16}{25}-\frac t5\right).
\]
Its second derivative is \(e^{79t/4}\) times a **nonconstant affine function**, and therefore has at most one real zero. Four distinct zeros of \(G_A\) would, by two applications of Rolle's theorem, force at least two distinct zeros of its second derivative. Thus the three exhibited stationary points are exhaustive. They are simple: the same Rolle argument counting multiplicities bounds the total root multiplicity by three; three distinct sign-changing roots already exhaust this bound. The displayed signs force local maximum, minimum, maximum, respectively. Moreover \(J_A(t)\to0\) as \(t\to\infty\), so the only possible global maximizers are the first and third stationary points.

**3. The sharp unique switch.** Define
\[
U(t)=e^{-20t}\left(\frac t{21}+\frac1{441}\right),\qquad
V(t)=e^{-t/4}\left(\frac{4t}5+\frac{16}{25}\right),
\]
so \(J_A=AU+V\). The unique maxima of \(U\) and \(V\) occur at \(1/420\) and \(16/5\), with respective values \(e^{-1/21}/420\) and \((16/5)e^{-4/5}\). As \(J_A\) increases to its first maximum, at \(A=500\) we have
\[
J_{500}(\tau_1)>J_{500}(0)=500/441+16/25>1.77.
\]
For \(t\ge1\), the monotone decrease of \(U\) and global bound for \(V\) give
\[
J_{500}(t)\le \frac{11000}{441}e^{-20}+\frac{16}5e^{-4/5}<1.61.
\]
Thus the earlier maximum is larger at \(A=500\). At \(A=100\), for \(0\le t\le1/20\),
\[
J_{100}(t)\le \frac5{21}e^{-1/21}+\frac{17}{25}e^{-1/80}<0.92,
\]
while \(J_{100}(16/5)\ge(16/5)e^{-4/5}>1\); hence the later maximum is larger at \(A=100\). These bounds use only elementary exponential inequalities and do not depend on floating-point computations.

The three roots are simple and therefore vary continuously and differentiably with \(A\). Set \(D(A)=J_A(\tau_1(A))-J_A(\tau_3(A))\). Stationarity eliminates the derivatives of the root locations, yielding the exact envelope derivative
\[
D'(A)=U(\tau_1(A))-U(\tau_3(A))>0.
\]
Indeed \(\tau_1\in(0,1/20)\), \(\tau_3>1\), and \(U\) has one maximum at \(1/420\) with \(\min_{[0,1/20]}U=\min\{U(0),U(1/20)\}>U(1)\ge U(\tau_3)\). Thus \(D\) is strictly increasing, begins negative, and ends positive. The intermediate value theorem gives a **unique** zero \(A_c\), establishing the claimed global switch and exact tie. Finally \(\tau_3-\tau_1>1-1/20=19/20\), proving the discontinuity bound.

## Verification
The standalone file `VERIFY.py` reproduces the formulas, checks root sign patterns and all three stationary intervals across a deterministic parameter grid, verifies the two endpoint comparisons, and estimates the unique crossing by bisection. Run with `python3 VERIFY.py` using only Python's standard library. Its finite computations are numerical diagnostics, not the proof of universal statements; the analytic Rolle and envelope arguments above supply those proofs. The verification reports the number of successful assertions.

## Relationship to prior work
Xiao and Moghadas (2013) use seasonal influenza models with vaccination, pre-existing immunity, and waning-related effects; their full open-access article optimizes coverage across host classes rather than a single variable-time individual dose against an externally specified two-wave hazard. Chao and Dimitrov (2015 online) study how finite transmission seasons change the efficiency of mass vaccination in an SIR setting, not the number of optimal one-dose dates or a sharp intensity-induced switch. Werker and colleagues (2013 online) study optimal seasonal influenza vaccination timing using a clinical Markov cohort model, but their full article was not accessible during this comparison, so its content beyond the available abstract was not assessed. The underlying hazard integration, elementary Rolle bound and envelope derivative are standard mathematical tools; novelty, if sustained, concerns their exact joint conclusion for the stated two-wave model, not those tools or the general importance of waning protection.

## Limitations
The fast and slow wave shapes and the interval \(A\in[100,500]\) are specified exactly to make the three-stationary-point regime rigorous. No claim is made that real influenza incidence has these parameters, that this model is a realistic individual vaccine response, or that an actual vaccination campaign should delay doses. Vaccination access, immunity build-up lag, risk of severe outcomes, outbreak feedback, previous infections, uncertain parameters, and individual heterogeneity are absent. The numerical crossing is approximate. The priority assessment is provisional, especially regarding general optimal-timing and exponential-polynomial literature and the inaccessible full article by Werker et al.
