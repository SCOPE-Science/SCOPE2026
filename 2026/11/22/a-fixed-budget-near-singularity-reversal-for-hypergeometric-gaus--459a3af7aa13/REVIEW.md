# Review of the fixed-budget near-singularity reversal

**Correctness — PASS.** Both matched changes of variable have exactly constant transformed singular weights. The inverse-square correction converges by dominated convergence to the positive integral \(C_f\), while the inverse-cube correction is \(c\log(1/d)+O(1)\) by Taylor expansion and an explicit antiderivative. For fixed quadrature order, all sinh nodes remain strictly inside the interval; each misses the limiting boundary layer at transformed coordinate zero. These estimates yield the four error limits and both sharp ratios. The numerical verifier passed 208 finite diagnostics with direct cancellation-free comparisons. The analytic argument supplies all infinite-limit quantifiers.

**Originality — PASS, qualified.** Hu–He–Sun's published convergence analysis compares the transforms in an increasing-node-order regime at fixed positive distance; the present claim concerns the opposite fixed-order/small-distance regime, a nonconstant smooth numerator class, and exact order-independent leading error ratios. Their transformation formulae are explicitly credited. The Hu–He–Rungamornrat–Gu comparison studies wider physical geometries and radial transformation families, with no exact coverage of this particular two-exponent limit identified in accessible material. Huybrechs–Cools construct generalized near-singular Gaussian rules rather than the same three maps with an unchanged Gauss–Legendre node set. Related full-text access limitations and potential older equivalent formulations remain priority risks; search nonmatches are not novelty proofs.

**Value — PASS.** A fixed sampling budget is a natural computational constraint. The theorem gives a sharp, nonuniform-in-budget counter-regime to a fixed-distance ellipse preference: two transforms have asymptotically perfect relative accuracy and the other loses all leading singular mass. The leading constants tell numerical practitioners which numerator jets and kernel powers matter, rather than merely exhibiting one bad numerical example. It does not claim one transformation dominates at ordinary distances, after adaptivity, or for general multidimensional domains.
