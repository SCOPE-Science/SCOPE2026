# Scientific review

## Correctness — PASS

The proof starts from the precise already-proved prime-power criterion of Farhi and Kane, separates missing-exponent-one events as unique \(N=mp\) factorizations, and bounds higher exponent events by \(O(X^{2/3}\log X)\). Centering by \(\varphi(q)/q\) produces a bounded-partial-sums periodic weight. Effective prime counting and two applications of partial summation isolate the harmonic constant and give the claimed \(O_q(X/(\log X)^2)\) error. The inclusion-exclusion evaluation of the constant and the q=2,3 signs were checked independently. A standalone exact-integer diagnostic verifies the fixed-window input for k<=8 and 12000 parameter values. Risk: the infinite step depends on the conventional effective prime number theorem and exact prior period theorem, not finite experiments.

## Originality — PASS, qualified

The full Farhi–Kane primary text proves a fixed-window period formula, not a distribution or centered coprimality bias over window parameters. The full Huang–Yang text generalizes that period formula but contains no density or asymptotic analysis in the window parameter. Hong–Qian's relevant abstract concerns generalized progression periods. Relevant broad smooth-number literature gives large-prime-factor leading densities or residue-class equidistribution at different cutoffs, not the displayed signed second-order correction in the inspected material. Targeted research-index queries and recoverable prior findings did not reveal a covering result. Risk: a more general published second-order smooth-number coprimality expansion or an unindexed equivalent may imply this theorem. That risk merits independent specialist checking before claiming publication priority.

## Mathematical value — PASS

The theorem determines the first nonzero arithmetic bias in a naturally parametrized least-period defect family. First-order density \(\log2\) alone cannot distinguish the coprime and noncoprime populations. The explicit logarithmic prime-factor constant detects both a negative parity bias and a positive modulus-three bias, with a quantitative error below the main correction; the new information cannot be read off from an individual period formula alone. It is a focused analytic-number-theoretic refinement, not a claim of a new general period classification.
