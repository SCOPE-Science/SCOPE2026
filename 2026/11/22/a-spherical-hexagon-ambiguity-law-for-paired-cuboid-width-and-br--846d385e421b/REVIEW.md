# Scientific review: paired cuboid width and brightness

## Correctness — PASS

For a cuboid with strictly ordered edges \(0<a<b<c\), its width and planar projection area are the two linear functionals \(v\cdot u\) and \(w\cdot u\) of a positive-octant unit vector \(u\). The row-rank-two projection of the sphere has exactly two candidate lifted vectors, and the spherical surface Jacobian is \(1/\sqrt{1-s}\); multiplying by the octant area density and the determinant of the planar linear map proves the complete joint density. The explicit identity \((b^2-a^2)(c^2-b^2)(a^2+c^2)>0\) guarantees the central sign inequality \(q^2>1/2\). The reflective overlap is a geodesically convex spherical hexagon; its six feasible great-circle intersections and their normal turning angles give the stated arcsine area and hence the ambiguity probability. Limiting box shapes prove the sharp range. The checker independently triangulates the spherical region and tests paired inverse branches in six geometrically different regimes. The tests are not substituted for analytic quantification.

## Originality — PASS, provisional

Direct comparisons distinguish this result from Finch's one-measurement cube-width formulas (arXiv:1110.0671), his planar projection area/perimeter treatment (arXiv:1203.2464), the later rectangular-box width marginal (arXiv:2608.03305), and prior same-normal width/brightness covariance studies. A complete two-dimensional pushforward and the closed octant-reflection overlap probability are not implied by those single-measurement or second-moment statements. The generic spherical-coarea ingredient is classical and is not claimed novel. The abstract of arXiv:2609.26202 describes a broad polyhedral width–brightness framework; the full text was inaccessible, leaving a genuine priority risk. Database searches and comparisons against published scientific claims did not locate an explicit equivalent ambiguity formula, but failed discovery cannot establish uniqueness.

## Mathematical value — PASS

Given two exact observations of a known anisotropic box, determining whether they identify one or two classes of viewing directions is a natural inverse-geometry question. The formula quantifies a nontrivial, shape-dependent ambiguity probability, shows its full sharp range \((0,1)\), identifies a six-sided spherical overlap mechanism, and supplies the entire paired-observation density rather than another marginal or covariance. It exposes an exact geometric transition from near-full ambiguity to near-unique recovery across box shapes; this is a substantive finite-dimensional classification, not a routine numerical sample or parameter substitution.
