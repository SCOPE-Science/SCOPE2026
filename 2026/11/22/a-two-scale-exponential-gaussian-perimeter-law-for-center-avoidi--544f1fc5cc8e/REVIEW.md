# Scientific review: center-avoiding random cyclic polygons

## Correctness: PASS
The center-miss event is exactly one angular gap greater than \(\pi\), with exact probability \(n2^{1-n}\). Conditional on the event, the long-gap excess is a beta variable and the remaining gap proportions are independent uniform Dirichlet. The perimeter and area identities follow from elementary chord lengths and the signed shoelace sum. Exact rational Dirichlet cubic moments and fifth-power Taylor bounds control the expansions, while an iid exponential delta-method CLT gives the variance \(360\) for the third-power spacing sum. The resulting independent Gaussian arc-loss variance is \(5\pi^6/8\). The finite verifier is supplementary, not a substitute for proof.

## Originality: PASS, with historical limitations
Wendel's 1962 center-exclusion probability is acknowledged, and the 2013 Koroleva–Nikitin paper concerns extremal inscribed subpolygons, not this whole conditional polygon. The 2019 unconditioned area/perimeter article and publicly indexed unconditional polygon regression work do not imply a law on an exponentially rare conditioning event. The accessible full text for the earlier center-clearance and acute-vertex results has different observables and, in the latter case, a different event. The Bélisle 2011 paper could be inspected only through its abstract, leaving a historical-priority risk; no absolute uniqueness claim is made.

## Value: PASS
The natural Wendel center-miss regime changes the polygon from a near disk to a near semicircle. The theorem identifies two independent fluctuation mechanisms, one from the macroscopic missing semicircle and another from fine interior vertex discretization, and computes a specific second-order coupling of area and perimeter. The exact scale separation and normal variance provide meaningful geometric information not present in known unconditioned polygon laws.

## Limitations
The theorem concerns uniformly sampled points on a unit circle with \(n\to\infty\) conditional on center exclusion, and not arbitrary smooth curves or moderate deviations. Finite checks do not prove the asymptotic theorem. Full-text inspection of one plausible early paper was unavailable; further historical priority verification may be desirable.
