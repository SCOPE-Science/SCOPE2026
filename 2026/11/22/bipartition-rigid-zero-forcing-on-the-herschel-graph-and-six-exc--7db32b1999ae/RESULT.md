# Bipartition-rigid zero forcing on the Herschel graph and six exceptional exchange detours

## Finding

Let \(H\) be the eleven-vertex Herschel graph with the labeled edge set below. Let \(\mathcal Z(H)\) consist of all **minimum ordinary zero-forcing sets** of \(H\). Define the token-jumping graph \(J\) by exchanging any one chosen vertex for one unchosen vertex while remaining minimum zero forcing; define the token-sliding graph \(T\) by additionally requiring the exchanged vertices to be adjacent in \(H\).

**Exact finite classification.** The zero-forcing number is \(Z(H)=5\), with exactly \(114\) minimum sets. In the unique bipartition \(V(H)=L\sqcup R\), where \(|L|=6\) and \(|R|=5\), every minimum zero-forcing set contains exactly three vertices of \(L\) and two of \(R\). Consequently \(T=114K_1\): **all** minimum sets are frozen under token sliding. In sharp contrast, \(J\) is connected, has \(114\) vertices and \(540\) edges, and is self-centered with radius and diameter both \(5\).

More precisely, for the \(\binom{114}{2}=6441\) unordered pairs \(A,B\) of distinct minima, let \(j(A,B)=|A\triangle B|/2\). Then

| \(d_J(A,B)-j(A,B)\) | Number of unordered pairs |
| ---: | ---: |
| \(0\) | \(6183\) |
| \(1\) | \(252\) |
| \(2\) | \(6\) |

All six pairs with excess two have Johnson lower bound two, actual exchange distance four, and form a **single orbit under the twelve graph automorphisms** of \(H\). No pair requires three or more extra exchanges. The complete exchange-distance distribution, for distances \(1,2,3,4,5\), is \((540,1938,2664,1167,132)\).

## Assumptions and scope

The Herschel graph is the finite simple undirected graph on \(\{0,1,\ldots,10\}\) with exactly the following edges, whose notation also fixes the certificate labeling:

```text
(0,1) (0,3) (0,4) (1,2) (1,5) (1,6)
(2,3) (2,7) (3,8) (3,9) (4,5) (4,9)
(5,10) (6,7) (6,10) (7,8) (8,10) (9,10)
```

Its two bipartition classes are \(L=\{0,2,5,6,8,9\}\) and \(R=\{1,3,4,7,10\}\). The initial set of blue vertices is \(S\). A blue vertex with exactly one white neighbor can color that neighbor blue. A set is zero forcing when repeated legal forces color every vertex. Distances in \(J\) count individual exchanges, not simultaneous or multi-vertex movements. Only **ordinary** zero forcing is addressed; PSD and skew variants are different problems. No statement is made about all planar or all non-Hamiltonian graphs.

## Proof

**1. Exhaustive characterization of minima.** Write a subset of vertices as an integer \(m\in[0,2^{11})\) using bit \(i\) for vertex \(i\). Define \(\Phi(m)\) to adjoin every currently white vertex that is the unique white neighbor of some blue vertex. Since forcing only changes white to blue, the iterates of \(\Phi\) terminate in at most eleven strict growth stages; their fixed point is the closure under all legal sequential forces. Thus a mask is zero forcing precisely when its closure is \(2^{11}-1\). The complete verifier `verify_direct.py` examines **all 2048 masks** (not a sample). It finds no forcing mask of cardinality at most four and exactly 114 forcing five-masks. These counts establish both minimality and completeness of the state set.

Independently, a nonempty set \(F\) is a *fort* when each vertex outside \(F\) has either zero or at least two neighbors in \(F\). A set \(S\) is zero forcing if and only if it meets every fort: a disjoint fort prevents the first forcing into it, whereas the nonempty final white set of any stalled forcing process is itself a fort. The separate `verify_forts.py` enumerates all 2047 nonempty subsets, finds 558 forts and exactly 30 inclusion-minimal forts, and enumerates their minimum transversals without using the closure routine. It reproduces exactly the same 114 five-sets after reconciling the source's one-based labeling. The complete roster is stored in `certificate.json`, with every mask independently regenerated by both programs.

**2. Bipartition rigidity and sliding.** For every one of those 114 sets \(S\), the full-roster check gives \(|S\cap L|=3\) and \(|S\cap R|=2\). Any slide along an edge of the bipartite base graph removes one vertex from one bipartition class and inserts one vertex in the other, changing \(|S\cap L|\) by \(+1\) or \(-1\). It therefore cannot result in another minimum set. All 114 slide vertices are isolated. This argument establishes zero slides once the exhaustive bipartition signature is known; it does not infer rigidity merely from an unsuccessful search.

**3. Unrestricted exchange metric.** As all minima have five vertices, two masks \(a,b\) are adjacent in \(J\) if and only if \(\operatorname{popcount}(a\oplus b)=2\). Testing all \(\binom{114}{2}\) pairs gives precisely 540 edges. Breadth-first search from each of the 114 vertices reaches all 114, with maximum distance five from every vertex. Summing each unordered pair exactly once yields the distance distribution stated above. Each exchange can change at most one misplaced selected vertex, giving the universal Johnson lower bound \(j(A,B)=|A\triangle B|/2\). Subtracting it from the exactly computed distances gives 6183 pairs of excess zero, 252 of excess one, and six of excess two. `verify_forts.py` constructs the same exchange edges from literal set intersections, independently computes all-pairs shortest paths with Floyd–Warshall, and reproduces every value.

**4. The six extremal detours form one symmetry orbit.** The six exceptional unordered pairs are explicitly listed in `certificate.json` as sorted integer masks. The second verifier independently enumerates all automorphisms by degree-class-preserving permutations, verifies that exactly twelve preserve all edges, and maps the first exceptional pair through all twelve automorphisms. Its orbit contains exactly the six recorded pairs. This is an exhaustive finite orbit certificate, not a guess from numerical multiplicities.

These four steps prove the entire finite claim. Source-code assertions use the full certificate; the certificate is redundant evidence rather than an assumption needed to establish the result.

## Relationship to prior work

Geneson, Haas and Hogben (arXiv:2009.00220v1, first publicly posted 2020-09-01) introduced the exchange reconfiguration graph of minimum ordinary zero-forcing sets and general structural results. Bong, Carlson, Curtis, Haas and Hogben (arXiv:2205.09668v1, 2022-05-19) treated token addition/removal reconfiguration rather than the fixed-cardinality graph calculated here. Bong and colleagues (arXiv:2501.03642v1, 2025-01-07) studied especially PSD and skew forcing under token exchange and sliding, as well as general constraints shared by vertex-set parameters. The generic definitions, the fort equivalence, and standard shortest-path algorithms are not claimed as original.

The Herschel graph is a canonical smallest non-Hamiltonian polyhedral graph; its explicit edge list agrees with the Sage graph generator. The proposed contribution is its **combined exact ordinary zero-forcing, bipartition-rigid token-slide, and almost-Johnson exchange-metric classification**, especially the single orbit of six two-move detours. This exact graph-specific result was not found in the inspected primary literature or targeted searches, but literature-search absence is not proof of historical priority.

## Limitations

This is a finite exhaustive proof for one named graph, not a general theorem for arbitrary bipartite graphs. Neither program certifies independent expert validation. Possible earlier, unindexed tabulations under alternate names, and the possibility that a broad previously published machine-readable graph database already contains the same 114-state metric, remain residual priority risks. The six-pair orbit and the bipartition obstruction are rigorous only for the explicitly supplied graph and forcing rule.
