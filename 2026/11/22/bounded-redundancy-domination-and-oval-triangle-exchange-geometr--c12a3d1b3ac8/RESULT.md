# Bounded-redundancy domination and oval–triangle exchange geometry in \(\operatorname{PG}(2,3)\)

## Finding
Let \(G\) be the 26-vertex point–line incidence graph of the Desarguesian projective plane \(\operatorname{PG}(2,3)\). A \([1,2]\)-set is a set \(S\subseteq V(G)\) such that **every vertex outside \(S\)** has between one and two neighbours in \(S\), inclusive. Then its minimum cardinality is
\[
\gamma_{[1,2]}(G)=7.
\]
There are precisely \(468\) minimum sets, divided into two families of \(234\):

* **Triangle type:** choose a noncollinear three-point set \(T\) and the four projective lines disjoint from \(T\); put these seven point and line vertices in \(S\).
* **Oval type:** choose a four-point oval \(O\) (no three collinear) and the three projective lines disjoint from \(O\); put these seven point and line vertices in \(S\).

Define the token-jump graph \(\mathcal X\) on these minimum sets: adjacent configurations differ by removing exactly one selected vertex and adding one previously unselected vertex. Then \(\mathcal X\) is precisely the bipartite inclusion graph of the \(234\) noncollinear point triangles and \(234\) four-point ovals, with a triangle adjacent to every oval containing it. Consequently \(\mathcal X\) is connected, four-regular, and has \(468\) vertices, \(936\) edges, radius \(9\) and diameter \(9\). Its distance profile from **every** configuration, for distances zero through nine, is
\[
(1,4,12,30,60,96,114,96,47,8).
\]
The old enumeration of the \(234\) ovals and the metric of their separate one-point-exchange graph are not presented as new. The new claim is the bounded-redundancy optimizer classification and the resulting two-type optimal-configuration exchange graph, including odd-distance layers.

## Assumptions and scope
The incidence graph has 13 point vertices and 13 line vertices; every point belongs to four lines and every pair of distinct points determines exactly one line. For \([1,2]\)-domination, the neighbourhood count is imposed only on vertices outside the chosen set, not on the chosen vertices. The two bipartition classes are distinguished for counting. A point triangle means three noncollinear points, a four-point oval means four points with no three collinear. An external line to a point set is a line meeting that set in zero points. Every vertex of \(\mathcal X\) represents a distinct *unlabelled seven-element subset* of the 26-vertex graph, not an ordered tuple or a colouring. Results concern this fixed ternary projective plane only.

## Proof
**1. Lower bound by incidence coverage.** Write \(a=|S\cap P|\), \(b=|S\cap L|\). Every line disjoint from the chosen points must be a selected line: otherwise it would be an unselected vertex with no selected neighbour. For \(a=0,1,2\), the chosen points meet at most \(0,4,7\) lines, respectively, so \(b\ge13,9,6\), respectively. Thus \(|S|\le7\) implies \(a\ge3\). By point–line duality it also implies \(b\ge3\), leaving only the possibilities \((a,b)=(3,3),(3,4),(4,3)\).

If \(a=b=3\) and the chosen points are noncollinear, the four external lines must be selected, contradicting \(b=3\). If the chosen points are collinear on \(\ell\), there are three external lines, which must be selected. Moreover \(\ell\) must also be selected, since otherwise its three selected point neighbours violate the upper bound two. Again \(b\ge4\). Consequently \(|S|\ge7\).

**2. Uniqueness of the triangle type.** Suppose \((a,b)=(3,4)\). If the three chosen points are collinear on \(\ell\), the three lines disjoint from them and \(\ell\) itself must be selected. All four selected lines pass through the fourth point \(r\) of \(\ell\), which is outside \(S\); this gives \(r\) four selected neighbours, forbidden by the upper bound. The chosen points therefore form a noncollinear triangle \(T\). Exactly four lines are disjoint from \(T\), so these must be the four selected lines. They have no triple concurrence. One direct check is to take the three coordinate vertices as \(T\): its external lines have nonzero coefficients in every coordinate, hence are represented by the four forms \((1,\varepsilon,\delta)\), for \(\varepsilon,\delta\in\{1,-1\}\subseteq\mathbf F_3\). Any three of these coefficient vectors are linearly independent. Thus the four external lines cover all ten points outside \(T\), with multiplicity one or two: they have 16 total point incidences and six distinct pairwise intersection points, so their union contains \(16-6=10\) points. Every unselected point has one or two selected line neighbours. Every unselected line meets \(T\) in one or two points (not zero by definition of external, not three because \(T\) is noncollinear). Hence the seven vertices really form a \([1,2]\)-set.

**3. The oval type by duality.** Projective duality exchanges points and lines, and takes the triangle-type description to three nonconcurrent selected lines together with the four points external to those lines. Those four points form an oval, as follows. Dualize the four external lines to a noncollinear four-point set in the dual plane; equivalently, in the original plane any line through three of the four external points would give a point incident with three of the four lines from Step 2, which is impossible. Hence every \((a,b)=(4,3)\) minimum set has precisely the asserted oval description, and every oval-type set is valid.

**4. Counts.** There are \(\binom{13}{3}-13\binom43=234\) noncollinear point triangles. Any fixed such triangle has exactly four available fourth points extending it to an oval (for the coordinate triangle they are the four points with all three coordinates nonzero). Each oval contains exactly four triangles. Double-counting triangle–oval incidences therefore yields \(234\) ovals and \(468\) minimum sets.

**5. The token-jump graph.** Two distinct triangle-type configurations cannot differ by only one selected vertex: replacing a point changes the set of three selected points and its entire four-line external set, while retaining all three points fixes that line set. Likewise two distinct oval-type configurations cannot differ by one vertex, by the dual argument. For an oval \(O\), remove one point \(p\in O\): the remaining triangle \(T=O\setminus\{p\}\) has exactly one additional external line beyond the three external to \(O\), namely the tangent to \(O\) at \(p\). Replacing the chosen point \(p\) by this line gives precisely the triangle-type minimum set associated with \(T\). These are the four possible token jumps from \(O\), and conversely every token jump corresponds to a triangle contained in an oval. This gives a bipartite, four-regular, 468-vertex graph, and \(|E(\mathcal X)|=468\cdot4/2=936\).

**6. Exact distances.** The diameter, connectivity, radius and uniform profile follow from the following *exhaustive finite certificate* over all 468 classified sets, implemented in two standalone programs using independent incidence models. One enumerates all 234 triangles, 234 ovals and their inclusion adjacencies from normalized projective triples; the other constructs all 26 vertices and their adjacencies from nine affine points and four points at infinity, enumerates **every** selected subset of sizes 1 through 7 from the full 26-vertex universe, evaluates the original neighbour-count condition, and independently constructs the token-jump graph. Both perform breadth-first search from all 468 configurations, require every state to be reached, compare all distance histograms, and assert the displayed common profile and maximum distance nine. These are exhaustive checks for this finite graph, not statistical experiments or proof for larger orders. The structural Steps 1–5 are independent of the distance computation.

The second program enumerates all \(\sum_{k=1}^{7}\binom{26}{k}\) candidate vertex sets of sizes one to seven, finds no valid set smaller than seven, finds exactly 468 valid sets of size seven in the two specified bipartition profiles, constructs the exchange graph by literal six-element intersection, and checks all 468 breadth-first-search profiles. ## Relationship to prior work
Chellali, Haynes, Hedetniemi and McRae (2013) define \([1,2]\)-sets and establish general structural results and bounds, but their investigated classes do not give this ternary projective-plane optimizer or exchange graph. Bishnu, Dutta, Ghosh and Paul (2014) study generalized \((1,j)\)-sets, algorithms and hardness; this does not supply the present exact finite-geometric classification. A published examination of one-point exchanges of ternary projective ovals already gives the 234-oval one-point-exchange graph and its distance geometry, and a published ternary-plane error-detecting open-locating result determines a *different* parameter based on lower bounds of two selected neighbours **at every vertex** and pairwise identification; neither by itself implies the \([1,2]\)-optimizer classification here. The oval-only exchange graph is a two-step related graph, so its known results are expressly excluded from novelty assertions. A separate study of six-point blocking-set exchanges concerns a different state space. The new bipartite optimizer graph includes mixed triangle–oval paths and odd distances; an equivalent earlier description under the names bounded domination, projective flags or derived incidence graphs remains a historical possibility.

## Limitations
The statements are for the unique Desarguesian plane of order three, not all projective orders, and the shortest-path spectrum is proved by exhaustive finite checking, rather than a general symbolic distance formula. The archived classification and current literature checks cannot exclude every unindexed historical enumeration. No independent external review or certification has been performed. The result does not assert anything about distance-two \((1,2)\)-domination, where the notation has a different meaning.
