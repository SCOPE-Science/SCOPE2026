# Complete classification of prime-representative subgroups modulo powers of two

## Finding

For every integer \(a\ge 1\), identify the elements of \((\mathbb Z/2^a\mathbb Z)^\times\) with their standard representatives in \(\{1,\ldots,2^a-1\}\). A subgroup is *prime-representative* if each of its nonidentity standard representatives is a (positive rational) prime. The following is the complete classification.

For \(a=1\), the only subgroup is \(\{1\}\). For \(a=2\), the subgroups are \(\{1\}\) and \(\{1,3\}\). For \(a=3\), they are \(\{1\}\), the three groups \(\{1,3\},\{1,5\},\{1,7\}\), and \(\{1,3,5,7\}\).

For **every** \(a\ge4\), every prime-representative subgroup is cyclic of order at most four. Besides \(\{1\}\), the complete list consists of:

- An order-two subgroup \(\{1,p\}\) for **each** prime among \(2^a-1\), \(2^{a-1}-1\), and \(2^{a-1}+1\).
- At most one order-four subgroup, namely
  \[
  H_a=\{1,\;2^{a-2}-1,\;2^{a-1}+1,\;3\cdot2^{a-2}-1\}.
  \]
  It exists **if and only if** all three nonidentity integers shown are prime.

Let \({\boldsymbol 1}_{\mathcal P}(m)\) be one for prime \(m\) and zero otherwise. In particular, for all \(a\ge4\) the *total number* of prime-representative subgroups, counting the trivial one, is exactly
\[
1+{\boldsymbol 1}_{\mathcal P}(2^a-1)
+{\boldsymbol 1}_{\mathcal P}(2^{a-1}-1)
+{\boldsymbol 1}_{\mathcal P}(2^{a-1}+1)
+\prod_{u\in\{2^{a-2}-1,\;2^{a-1}+1,\;3\cdot2^{a-2}-1\}}{\boldsymbol 1}_{\mathcal P}(u).
\]
For example, \(H_5=\{1,7,17,23\}\) and \(H_9=\{1,127,257,383\}\) both exist. The theorem does **not** assert that these are the only exponents admitting order-four subgroups.

## Assumptions and scope

The modulus is precisely \(2^a\) for an integer \(a\ge1\). Every occurrence of primality refers to the ordinary integer standard representative, **not** the existence of a prime in the corresponding congruence class. The subgroup operation is multiplication modulo \(2^a\). The trivial group is included. The criterion involving three prime numbers is an exact classification without assuming anything about infinitude of Mersenne or Fermat primes.

## Proof

**Involutions and cyclicity.** For \(a\ge3\), the unit group is \(C_2\times C_{2^{a-2}}\). The three distinct nonidentity solutions to \(x^2\equiv1\pmod{2^a}\) have standard representatives
\[
2^a-1,\qquad 2^{a-1}-1,\qquad 2^{a-1}+1.
\]
For \(a\ge4\), at least one of the latter two is composite. If \(a\) is even, then \(2^{a-1}+1\) is divisible by three and exceeds three. If \(a\) is odd, then \(2^{a-1}-1\) is divisible by three and exceeds three. Each noncyclic subgroup of \(C_2\times C_{2^{a-2}}\) contains the full four-element involution subgroup, so it must include this composite involution and cannot be prime-representative. All qualifying subgroups for \(a\ge4\) are consequently cyclic. Their order is a power of two.

**Order four.** When \(a\ge4\), the elements of exact order four are precisely the four solutions of \(x^2\equiv 2^{a-1}+1\pmod{2^a}\):
\[
2^{a-2}-1,\quad2^{a-2}+1,\quad
3\cdot2^{a-2}-1,\quad3\cdot2^{a-2}+1.
\]
These yield exactly two cyclic order-four subgroups, both having \(2^{a-1}+1\) as their involution:
\[
H_a^-=\{1,2^{a-2}-1,2^{a-1}+1,3\cdot2^{a-2}-1\},
\]
\[
H_a^+=\{1,2^{a-2}+1,2^{a-1}+1,3\cdot2^{a-2}+1\}.
\]
If \(2^{a-1}+1\) is prime, the exponent \(a-1\) must be a power of two: otherwise writing \(a-1=2^t u\) with odd \(u>1\) exhibits \(2^{a-1}+1\) as a nontrivial factor of a sum of odd powers. Since \(a\ge4\), this forces \(a=2^t+1\) for some integer \(t\ge2\), in particular \(a-2\) is odd and congruent to three modulo four. Now \(2^{a-2}+1\) is divisible by three and is larger than three; also \(3\cdot2^{a-2}+1\) is divisible by five (because \(2^{a-2}\equiv3\pmod5\)) and is larger than five. Thus \(H_a^+\) cannot be prime-representative. The remaining group \(H_a^-\) is prime-representative precisely when its three displayed nonidentity elements are prime, establishing the order-four classification.

**No order eight or higher.** Suppose a qualifying cyclic subgroup contains an element \(x\) of order eight. Then \(a\ge5\) and the order-four element \(x^2\), being a square of an odd integer, has representative congruent to one modulo eight. The order-four list above shows that \(x^2\) must be either \(2^{a-2}+1\) or \(3\cdot2^{a-2}+1\). Its square, the involution \(2^{a-1}+1\), also belongs to the subgroup, so this involution must be prime. The preceding paragraph then forces \(a=2^t+1\) with \(t\ge2\), under which both candidates for \(x^2\) are composite. Contradiction. Every cyclic group of order at least eight contains an element of order eight; hence all qualifying groups have order at most four.

**Order two and small moduli.** Every order-two subgroup is generated by one of the three involutions listed above, and it qualifies exactly when the involution's representative is prime. The modulus-two, modulus-four and modulus-eight cases follow by directly listing the units. The count formula adds the disjoint order-zero (trivial), order-two and order-four alternatives. This proves every assertion.

## Verification

The standalone `verify.py` uses a sieve and, independently of the classification, creates every subgroup generated by arbitrary prime unit representatives; it rejects any subgroup containing a composite nonidentity representative. For every \(1\le a\le14\), and again for \(a=17\), its exhaustively generated subgroup set agrees with the displayed formula. There are 33 subgroup instances over these 15 moduli. It checks the specific examples modulo 32 and 512 and the absence of order-eight prime-representative subgroups in those ranges. Run with `python3 verify.py` using only the Python standard library. These finite checks corroborate the algebraic arguments but are not substitutes for the infinite proof.

## Relationship to prior work

Trey Li's *Groups of Prime Units* (arXiv:2611.01793v1, first public version November 1, 2026) introduced the exact prime-representative subgroup framework, classified **complete** prime-unit groups and bounded **compact** ones; its Proposition 2.2 gives a general permitted interval for prime representatives, and Section 4 treats order-three groups for prime moduli. Its explicit observations about modulus eight are included in the small-case classification here. The theorem above instead classifies **all** prime-representative subgroups across the infinite family of moduli \(2^a\), not only complete or compact groups, and gives the exact order-four criterion. The standard structure theorem for the unit group modulo powers of two is used as known input. No priority claim is made beyond accessible literature and mathematical-record searches; an unindexed or implicitly equivalent earlier argument remains possible.

## Limitations

The classification applies only to powers of two and does not classify prime-representative subgroups for arbitrary moduli. Determining which exponents beyond the explicitly exhibited examples satisfy the three simultaneous primality conditions is not solved. The computation verifies only listed finite exponents; it proves neither infinitude nor finiteness of admissible order-four cases. Independent mathematical audit has not been performed.
