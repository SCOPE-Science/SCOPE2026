# Complete ternary digit-sum spectra for every power of eleven

## Finding
For an integer \(N>0\), write \(s_3(N)\) for the sum of its base-three digits. For every integer \(k\ge1\), set
\[
\mathcal D_k=\{s_3(N):N\in\mathbb Z_{>0},\ 11^k\mid N\}.
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{\mathcal D_1=\{t\in\mathbb Z:t\ge3\},\qquad
\mathcal D_k=\{5,7\}\cup\{t\in\mathbb Z:t\ge9\}\quad(k\ge2).}
\]
Thus precisely the six positive weights \(1,2,3,4,6,8\) are forbidden at all square-and-higher levels. This classifies *every* possible weight, not merely the minimum weight (which was already known to be \(3\) at the prime level and \(5\) above it).

## Assumptions and scope
Only positive ordinary integers \(N\) are considered. A sum of \(t\) powers allows repeated exponents before normalization. For a modulus coprime to three, let \(H_k=\langle 3\bmod 11^k\rangle\) be the multiplicative subgroup generated by \(3\), and let \(T_k\) be the set of positive integers \(t\) for which there exist \(u_1,\ldots,u_t\in H_k\) with \(\sum_i u_i\equiv0\pmod{11^k}\).

**Digit-sum equivalence.** The equality \(T_k=\mathcal D_k\) holds. A multiple with ternary digit sum \(t\) is a sum of \(t\) powers of three (split digits \(2\) into two copies). Conversely, a vanishing sum of \(t\) subgroup residues can be represented by \(t\) powers of three. Let \(h=\operatorname{ord}_{11^k}(3)\). Select exponents in the prescribed residue classes modulo \(h\), increasing each by a sufficiently large distinct multiple of \(h\) so that all \(t\) exponents are distinct. Their integer sum is positive, divisible by \(11^k\), and has base-three digit sum exactly \(t\). This equivalence is standard in the sparse-multiple literature and is not claimed as new.

## Proof
**Prime level.** Direct multiplication gives
\[
H_1=\{1,3,9,5,4\}\pmod {11},\qquad |H_1|=5.
\]
There is no one-term vanishing sum; no two-term sum can vanish because \(-1\notin H_1\) (the subgroup has odd order). The following zero sums use only elements of \(H_1\):
\[
1+1+9=11,\quad1+1+4+5=11,\quad1+1+3+3+3=11.
\]
Thus weights \(3,4,5\) occur. Concatenation of these zero sums permits every integer weight at least three, proving the first formula.

**Exact finite obstruction modulo \(121\).** Here \(3^5=243\equiv1\pmod{121}\) and \(3\) has order five modulo eleven, so
\[
H_2=\{1,3,9,27,81\}\pmod {121}.
\]
Any \(t\)-term vanishing sum is represented by exactly one multiplicity vector \((c_0,c_1,c_2,c_3,c_4)\) satisfying
\[
 c_i\ge0,\quad\sum_{i=0}^4 c_i=t,\quad
c_0+3c_1+9c_2+27c_3+81c_4\equiv0\pmod{121}.
\]
For \(t=1,\ldots,8\), exhaustive enumeration gives the respective numbers of solutions
\[
(0,0,0,0,1,0,5,0).
\]
This is a *complete finite certificate*: for each \(t\), enumerate every \(c_0\) from zero to \(t\), then every \(c_1\) from zero to \(t-c_0\), likewise \(c_2,c_3\), and set \(c_4=t-c_0-c_1-c_2-c_3\). There are exactly \(\binom{t+4}{4}\) tested vectors, and the standalone checker below implements these bounds with exact integer arithmetic. In particular the only weights below nine are five and seven.

Here are constructive residue-zero vectors for every one of the five consecutive weights nine through thirteen, together with weights five and seven. The entries are \((c_0,c_1,c_2,c_3,c_4)\), and the displayed weighted sums are multiples of \(121\):

| Weight \(t\) | Multiplicity vector | Weighted sum |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | \((1,1,1,1,1)\) | \(121\) |
| 7 | \((0,1,1,1,4)\) | \(363\) |
| 9 | \((0,1,1,4,3)\) | \(363\) |
| 10 | \((2,2,2,2,2)\) | \(242\) |

| 12 | \((1,2,2,2,5)\) | \(484\) |
| 13 | \((0,1,1,10,1)\) | \(363\) |

A five-term zero sum may be appended to any existing zero sum. Since every integer \(t\ge9\) is one of \(9,10,11,12,13\) plus a nonnegative multiple of five, every such weight occurs. The full second-level classification follows.

**Lifting to every higher power.** The integer identity \(3^5-1=2\cdot11^2\), together with the ordinary lifting-the-exponent formula, gives
\[
\operatorname{ord}_{11^k}(3)=5\cdot11^{\max(k-2,0)}\qquad(k\ge1).
\]
For \(k\ge2\), the reduction \(H_k\to H_2\) is onto and has kernel of size \(11^{k-2}\). This kernel lies in \(1+121\mathbb Z/11^k\mathbb Z\), also of size \(11^{k-2}\), and hence equals it. Thus \(H_k\) is precisely the inverse image of \(H_2\) among units modulo \(11^k\).

Given any \(t\)-term zero sum \(u_1+\cdots+u_t=0\) in \(H_2\), choose arbitrary lifts of the first \(t-1\) terms to \(H_k\). Their negative sum \(v\) reduces modulo \(121\) to the original last summand \(u_t\in H_2\); consequently \(v\in H_k\). This provides a \(t\)-term vanishing sum in \(H_k\). Conversely, reducing a vanishing sum in \(H_k\) yields one in \(H_2\). Therefore \(T_k=T_2\) and hence \(\mathcal D_k=\mathcal D_2\) for every \(k\ge2\).

## Verification
The standalone `verify.py` independently enumerates all unordered five-residue combinations for weights through thirteen, checks the complete finite obstruction and seven exact witnesses, verifies orders and reduction fibers for levels through six, and constructs separated ternary exponents to witness each admissible weight at sampled levels. It uses only Python's standard library. Run `python verify.py`; a correct execution prints `VERIFY_OK`. These computations certify the *finite* enumeration because all bounded cases are covered. The unlimited higher-power assertion follows from the group-theoretic proof, not the finite tests.

## Relationship to prior work
Jacob and Külshammer (2022, notably Proposition 9 and the explicit \(q=9,p=11\) example) already establish the minimum vanishing-sum lengths three and five in this prime-power family and a general lifting mechanism for **minimum** lengths. These are **not** the new claims. The present result adds the *entire* attainable-length spectrum, including the nonconsecutive exceptional weights five and seven, the exact holes at six and eight, and the all-weights threshold nine. Computing the minimum alone cannot determine these gaps, nor does the lifting proposition supply the missing base-level census. Clokie and coauthors (2020) relate digit sums to modular power sums and prove general sparse-multiple methods. Phunphayap and coauthors (2023) study attainable digit sums for general moduli; their general constraints do not classify the unusual modulus \(121\). The comparison remains limited to the inspected primary sources and records; unlocated equivalent statements are possible.

## Limitations
The theorem concerns the **set of attainable weights**, not the frequency or least size of integers realizing each weight. The special modulus \(121\) is genuinely significant because the order of three fails to grow at the square; the same explicit weight set is not asserted for arbitrary primes or bases. No independent mathematical validation has been performed, and a previously unnoticed classification of all attainable short power-sum lengths would take priority.
