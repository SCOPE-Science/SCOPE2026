# Consecutive-value GCDs of every degree-six cyclotomic polynomial

## Finding

Let \(\Phi_m(X)\) be the \(m\)-th cyclotomic polynomial and, for every integer \(n\), put
\[
D_m(n)=\gcd\bigl(\Phi_m(n),\Phi_m(n+1)\bigr).
\]
The complete set of indices satisfying \(\deg\Phi_m=6\) is \(\{7,9,14,18\}\). For every integer \(n\), the four GCDs are given *exactly* by
\[
\begin{aligned}
D_7(n)&=29^{\mathbf1_{\{23,24\}}(n\bmod29)},\\
D_{14}(n)&=29^{\mathbf1_{\{4,5\}}(n\bmod29)},\\
D_9(n)&=19^{\mathbf1_{\{4,5,16\}}(n\bmod19)}
         37^{\mathbf1_{\{33\}}(n\bmod37)},\\
D_{18}(n)&=19^{\mathbf1_{\{2,13,14\}}(n\bmod19)}
         37^{\mathbf1_{\{3\}}(n\bmod37)}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Here \(\mathbf1_E(r)\) equals one for \(r\in E\) and zero otherwise, and \(n\bmod q\) is the least nonnegative residue. In particular, each GCD is squarefree, and the possible values are \(\{1,29\}\) for indices seven and fourteen, and \(\{1,19,37,703\}\) for indices nine and eighteen. Their respective least periods are \(29,703,29,703\).

For the canonical aggregate
\[
H(n)=\prod_{m\in\{7,9,14,18\}}D_m(n),
\]
one obtains
\[
H(n)=29^{\mathbf1_{\{4,5,23,24\}}(n\bmod29)}
19^{\mathbf1_{\{2,4,5,13,14,16\}}(n\bmod19)}
37^{\mathbf1_{\{3,33\}}(n\bmod37)}.
\]
Its least period is exactly \(20387=19\cdot29\cdot37\). Over any complete period, its **entire frequency table** is:

| Value of \(H(n)\) | Exact frequency |
|---:|---:|
| \(1\) | 11375 |
| \(19\) | 5250 |
| \(29\) | 1820 |
| \(37\) | 650 |
| \(551=19\cdot29\) | 840 |
| \(703=19\cdot37\) | 300 |
| \(1073=29\cdot37\) | 104 |
| \(20387=19\cdot29\cdot37\) | 48 |

Thus the sharp upper bound \(H(n)\le20387\) is attained on exactly 48 residue classes per least period, for example at \(n=8839\).

## Assumptions and scope

The result holds for all \(n\in\mathbb Z\), including negative arguments, with the GCD taken nonnegative. It is a complete classification at the naturally defined degree-six cyclotomic layer, not a claim for other cyclotomic degrees or for shifts greater than one. All displayed counts concern complete residue periods and therefore give exact natural densities as well.

## Proof

**1. Exhaustiveness of the indices.** A prime dividing \(m\) when \(\varphi(m)=6\) must have predecessor dividing six, so it is among \(2,3,7\). The factor seven forces \(m=7\) or \(14\). With no factor seven, the totient identity for \(2^a3^b\) gives \(m=9\) or \(18\). Their cyclotomic polynomials are
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Phi_7(X)&=1+X+X^2+X^3+X^4+X^5+X^6,\\
\Phi_9(X)&=1+X^3+X^6,\\
\Phi_{14}(X)&=\Phi_7(-X),\\
\Phi_{18}(X)&=\Phi_9(-X).
\end{aligned}
\]

**2. No other common prime factors.** The exact integer polynomial resultants are
\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Res}_X(\Phi_7(X),\Phi_7(X+1))&=53824=2^6\cdot29^2,\\
\operatorname{Res}_X(\Phi_9(X),\Phi_9(X+1))&=253783=19^3\cdot37.
\end{aligned}
\]
These are computed directly as the determinants of the corresponding explicit \(12\)-by-\(12\) Sylvester matrices. A stand-alone fraction-free integer determinant implementation is supplied in `check_degree_six.py`, and its expected integer outputs are asserted. The integer resultant lies in the ideal generated by the two integer polynomials; therefore every common integer value divisor must divide that resultant. Both cyclotomic polynomials take odd values at every integer (check \(X=0,1\pmod2\)), eliminating the factor two in the seventh-index case.

**3. All roots and the prime-square obstruction.** Write \(P(X)=\Phi_m(X)\) and \(Q(X)=P(X+1)\). Directly evaluating the six-degree polynomials modulo the only surviving primes gives precisely:

| \(m\) | prime \(q\) | common roots \(r\pmod q\) |
|---:|---:|---|
| 7 | 29 | 23, 24 |
| 9 | 19 | 4, 5, 16 |
| 9 | 37 | 33 |

Each listed root has \(P'(r)\) and \(Q'(r)\) nonzero modulo \(q\). The unique possible lifting digits \(t_P,t_Q\in\{0,\ldots,q-1\}\) satisfying \(P(r+qt_P)\equiv0\pmod{q^2}\) and \(Q(r+qt_Q)\equiv0\pmod{q^2}\) are:

| \(m,q,r\) | \(t_P\) | \(t_Q\) |
|---|---:|---:|
| 7, 29, 23 | 19 | 26 |
| 7, 29, 24 | 26 | 20 |
| 9, 19, 4 | 5 | 3 |
| 9, 19, 5 | 3 | 12 |
| 9, 19, 16 | 2 | 12 |
| 9, 37, 33 | 28 | 19 |

Indeed, the digit for \(P\) is the unique solution of
\[
t_P\equiv-\frac{P(r)}q\bigl(P'(r)\bigr)^{-1}\pmod q,
\]
and analogously for \(Q\). Every pair of digits differs. Consequently neither \(q^2\) divides both polynomial values, for any integer argument. Together with the root tables and resultant restrictions, this proves the first two GCD formulas *at all integer arguments*.

**4. The remaining two indices.** The polynomial reflection identities above imply \(D_{14}(n)=D_7(-n-1)\) and \(D_{18}(n)=D_9(-n-1)\). Reflecting the exact root sets yields the remaining formulas. The two sets of roots for the paired indices are disjoint at every relevant prime; hence multiplying produces the displayed formula for \(H\).

**5. Sharp period and exact frequencies.** The three prime indicators in \(H\) independently select \(4\) out of \(29\), \(6\) out of \(19\), and \(2\) out of \(37\) residue classes. The Chinese remainder theorem gives each table entry as the product of the appropriate selected or unselected counts, and proves that all eight values occur. The same theorem shows that \(20387\) is a period. If \(T\) is any period, the indicator of divisibility of \(H(n)\) by each of \(19,29,37\) is invariant under translation by \(T\). A proper, nonempty subset of the additive group \(\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z\), with \(q\) prime, is not invariant under a nonzero translation, so each prime divides \(T\). Thus the displayed period is least, and the maximum and its frequency are sharp.

## Verification

The included dependency-free script `check_degree_six.py` recomputes both integer resultants with a fraction-free Sylvester determinant; exhaustively enumerates the roots at all possible common primes; checks every individual lifting digit and its incompatibility; directly compares all four GCD formulas against integer GCDs for each of the \(20387\) residue classes; and verifies the eight frequency counts. It also samples negative integer arguments. Its direct GCD tests corroborate rather than replace the unbounded proof, which follows from the exact determinant, finite root/lift certificates, and reflection/CRT arguments above.

## Relationship to prior work

The first public version of De Nápoli's 2020 cyclotomic-identity paper (arXiv:2010.02668) establishes general multiplicative-index identities for cyclotomic polynomials; the full arXiv HTML text was inspected. Those identities explain the reflection and degree structure but do not furnish the four simultaneous neighboring-value GCD profiles or their least-period histogram. Cohen and Sonn (2015) study GCDs of cyclotomic values \(\Phi_m(a^k)\) and \(\Phi_m(b^k)\) as the *exponent* grows for *fixed* bases, with conditional growth bounds; their full-text introduction and main theorem statements concern a different quantifier and do not imply the bounded neighboring-base result here. Accessible published comparisons about third-cyclotomic shifted GCDs and eighth-cyclotomic LCM windows also address different polynomials. A generic resultant calculation supplies a finite candidate-prime set but does not by itself establish the specific roots, squarefreeness, sharp period, or frequencies established above.

## Limitations

No assertion is made about other degrees or larger shifts. The certificate is an independently reproducible finite algebraic calculation, not a machine-formal proof. An unindexed published computation yielding equivalent numerical tables is a residual originality risk.
