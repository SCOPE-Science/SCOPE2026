# Exact fifth-index common divisors of repunits and their binomial transforms

## Finding

For each integer \(b\ge 2\), define the fifth repunit and its ordinary binomial-transform counterpart by
\[
R(b)=\sum_{j=0}^{4}b^j=\frac{b^5-1}{b-1},\qquad
T(b)=\sum_{j=0}^{5}\binom{5}{j}\frac{b^j-1}{b-1}
=\frac{(b+1)^5-2^5}{b-1}.
\]
Then the **exact** common divisor is
\[
\boxed{\gcd(R(b),T(b))
=5^{\mathbf 1_{b\equiv1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 5)}}
11^{\mathbf 1_{b\equiv5,9\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 11)}}.}
\]
Consequently the gcd assumes only \(1,5,11,55\). Its least positive period as a function of the integer base \(b\) is \(55\); in each period the four values occur in respectively \(36,9,8,2\) residue classes.

For the higher-order Mersenne sequence of Prasad, Kumari, Mohanta and Mahato, let
\[
M_n^{(k)}=\frac{2^{kn}-1}{2^k-1},\qquad
B_n^{(k)}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom nj M_j^{(k)}\qquad(k\ge1).
\]
The specialized result is
\[
\gcd(M_5^{(k)},B_5^{(k)})
=5^{\mathbf 1_{4\mid k}}
11^{\mathbf 1_{k\equiv4,6\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 10)}}.
\]
Its least exponent period is \(20\); within one period the values \(1,5,11,55\) occur \(13,3,2,2\) times respectively.

## Assumptions and scope

The base \(b\) is any integer at least \(2\), with no primality or parity assumption. The parameter \(k\) in the higher-order Mersenne specialization is any positive integer. The gcd is the positive greatest common divisor of the two integers. The indicators equal one when their subscript condition holds, zero otherwise. The claim concerns the fifth index; no general formula for arbitrary index is asserted.

## Proof

**1. Polynomial reduction.** Set
\[
F(X)=X^4+X^3+X^2+X+1,\qquad
G(X)=X^4+6X^3+16X^2+26X+31.
\]
Expanding the binomial numerator and dividing by \(X-1\) proves \(R(b)=F(b)\) and \(T(b)=G(b)\).

**2. Prime support via fifth-root rigidity.** The value \(F(b)\) is odd for every integer \(b\): for even \(b\) its constant term is odd; for odd \(b\) it is a sum of five odd terms. Let a prime \(\ell\) divide both \(F(b)\) and \(G(b)\). If \(\ell=5\), then
\[
F(X)\equiv G(X)\equiv(X-1)^4\pmod 5,
\]
so this occurs precisely for \(b\equiv1\pmod5\).

Now suppose \(\ell\ne5\). The identity \((b-1)F(b)=b^5-1\) shows that the image \(x\) of \(b\) modulo \(\ell\) has multiplicative order exactly five: it satisfies \(x^5=1\), but \(x=1\) would give \(F(1)=5\not\equiv0\pmod\ell\). Thus \(\ell\equiv1\pmod5\) and \(\ell\) is odd. Since \(G(b)=0\pmod\ell\), also \(y=(x+1)/2\) satisfies \(y^5=1\). The fifth roots form the cyclic group generated by \(x\), so \(y=x^j\) for some \(j\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}\), giving
\[
1+x=2x^j\pmod\ell.
\]
For \(j=0,1\) this forces \(x=1\), impossible. For \(j=2\), factoring \(2x^2-x-1=(x-1)(2x+1)\) gives \(x=-1/2\). Using \(x^5=1\) then yields \(\ell\mid 33\), and \(\ell\equiv1\pmod5\) forces \(\ell=11\); its residue is \(x=5\). For \(j=3\), the nontrivial factor is \(2x^2+2x+1=0\). As two is invertible, this implies \(x^2=-x-1/2\), \(x^4=-1/4\), and \(x^5=-x/4\). With \(x^5=1\) this gives \(x=-4\), whence \(25=2x^2+2x+1=0\pmod\ell\), contradicting \(\ell\ne5\). For \(j=4\), the nontrivial factor is \(2(x^3+x^2+x)+1=0\). Because \(F(x)=0\), it is equivalent to \(2x^4+1=0\), and multiplying by \(x\) gives \(x=-2\). Now \(x^5=1\) again implies \(\ell\mid33\), hence \(\ell=11\) and \(x=9\). Conversely, direct substitution verifies \(F(5)=G(5)=0\pmod{11}\) and \(F(9)=G(9)=0\pmod{11}\). Therefore the common prime divisors are exactly \(5\) under \(b\equiv1\pmod5\) and \(11\) under \(b\equiv5,9\pmod{11}\).

**3. Exact valuations.** When \(b=1+5t\), the binomial expansion gives
\[
F(b)=\sum_{j=0}^{4}(1+5t)^j\equiv5+5t\sum_{j=0}^{4}j\equiv5\pmod{25}.
\]
Thus the common divisor contains at most one factor of five.

For eleven, any common root modulo \(11^2\) would be congruent to \(r\in\{5,9\}\pmod{11}\). Put \(b=r+11t\). Reduction of \(F(b)\) and \(G(b)\) modulo \(121\) yields the following coefficients after division by \(11\):

| \(r\) | \(F(b)/11\pmod{11}\) | \(G(b)/11\pmod{11}\) |
|---|---|---|
| \(5\) | \(5+3t\) | \(3t\) |
| \(9\) | \(10t\) | \(3+2t\) |

In either row the two expressions cannot vanish simultaneously. No common factor \(121\) is possible. Combining with step 2 proves the exact gcd formula.

**4. Periods and multiplicities.** The two prime indicators independently inspect residue classes modulo \(5\) and \(11\). The Chinese remainder theorem gives, among \(55\) base classes, exactly \(1\cdot2=2\) classes with gcd \(55\), \(1\cdot9=9\) with gcd \(5\), \(4\cdot2=8\) with gcd \(11\), and \(4\cdot9=36\) with gcd \(1\). Any period preserves both prime-divisibility indicators. As each indicator is nonconstant on its respective prime modulus, its periods must be multiples of \(5\) or \(11\) respectively. Hence the least base period is \(55\).

Finally, \(2\) has orders \(4\) modulo \(5\) and \(10\) modulo \(11\); \(2^k\equiv5,9\pmod{11}\) exactly when \(k\equiv4,6\pmod{10}\). This gives the asserted formula for \(k\), with least joint period \(\mathrm{lcm}(4,10)=20\). Direct residue counting modulo \(20\) yields \(13,3,2,2\) for the values \(1,5,11,55\).

## Verification

`VERIFY.py` is standalone Python 3 with no nonstandard dependencies. It computes the integer Sylvester determinant \(\operatorname{Res}(F,G)=378125=5^5\cdot11^2\) as a second, independent restriction on prime support; checks common roots modulo \(5,11,25,121\); tests the exact gcd over bases \(-500\) through \(20000\); and reconstructs the fifth binomial transforms directly from their defining sum for exponents \(1\) through \(50\). It checks the least periods and all residue frequencies. These finite checks support, but do not replace, the proof for all integer bases.

## Relationship to prior work

Prasad, Kumari, Mohanta and Mahato introduced the higher-order Mersenne sequence and its ordinary binomial transform in the preprint first publicly posted on 2023-07-16, arXiv:2307.08073. Their Definitions 2.1 and equation (2.11), and Theorem 2.23, supply the sequences and the closed transform formula. Their December 2025 journal version contains the corresponding binomial-transform result as Theorem 2.18. Those statements determine the individual terms but do not give the exact fifth-index gcd, the uniqueness of characteristic eleven for the affine fifth-root coincidence, or the exact base/exponent periods and densities. A 2025 paper on \(k\)-Mersenne and \(p\)-binomial transforms treats a differently parametrized recurrence (characteristic roots depending on \(k\)); its ordinary-transform formulas do not imply this gcd classification. General resultant and cyclotomic techniques provide comparison methods, not an identified previous evaluation of this exact joint invariant.

## Limitations

The theorem is specific to index five; no assertion is made about general prime indices, higher indices, or higher-order common factors of other transforms. The literature comparisons support a best-knowledge originality assessment but do not prove the global absence of prior formulations. The result has not received independent verification.
