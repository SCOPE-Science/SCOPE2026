# Scientific assessment

## Correctness — PASS

The pointwise Jordan-variation decomposition into one-sided step indicators gives an exact norm reduction. The binomial-tail Bernstein image of each step is a beta order-statistic distribution function; its integrated error equals mean absolute deviation from the jump. Convexity on each grid gap and exact reflection reduce the sharp finite norm to a maximum over grid indices; one-sided grid jumps attain the norm. Exact incomplete-beta integration supplies the rational formula. A beta/binomial CLT, a uniform second-moment estimate, and subsequence compactness prove both the sharp \(1/\sqrt{2\pi}\) limit and localization at one-half. The standalone exact-rational checker independently verifies polynomial integrals through degree sixteen. The finite tests do not prove the infinite statements; those follow from the written argument.

## Originality — PASS, qualified

The inspected 2015 primary paper derives pointwise BV estimates, not the sharp integrated norm; the 2025 full-text paper likewise studies local pointwise moduli and bounds. The 2017 variation-diminishing result controls a different seminorm, not the integrated approximation error. Published comparisons of Bernstein refinements, consecutive-degree Lipschitz errors, and Gini smoothing also have different operators, input classes, or output seminorms. The exact beta absolute-deviation reduction plus asymptotic norming-jump localization is not implied by those statements. Two plausible historical sources—Cheng (1983) and Bustamante et al. (2014)—could not be inspected fully; originality is a qualified best-knowledge assessment, not a claim of exhaustive historical priority.

## Mathematical value — PASS

The classical Bernstein scheme is foundational in positive approximation. Its sharp integrated approximation error on data admitting jumps is a natural operator norm distinct from its well-known variation diminution and from local pointwise rates. The exact rational finite-degree norm, single-jump attainment, sharp \(n^{-1/2}\) constant, and necessity of midpoint localization provide usable error calibration and explain which discontinuity locations control worst-case performance. The finite answer does not require a chosen arbitrary parameter slice, and the Gaussian limit applies to the complete degree family.
