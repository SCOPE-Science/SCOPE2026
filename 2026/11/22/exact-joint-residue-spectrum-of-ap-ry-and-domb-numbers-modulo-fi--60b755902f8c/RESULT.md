# Exact joint residue spectrum of Apéry and Domb numbers modulo five

## Finding

For every integer \(n\ge0\), define the classical Apéry and Domb numbers
\[
A_n=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom nj^2\binom{n+j}j^2,
\qquad
D_n=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom nj^2\binom{2j}j\binom{2n-2j}{n-j}.
\]
For every integer \(L\ge0\), and residues \(a,d\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}\), let
\[
J_L(a,d)=\#\{0\le n<5^L:A_n\equiv a\pmod5,\ D_n\equiv d\pmod5\}.
\]
Put \(u_r\equiv3^r\pmod5\) for \(0\le r<4\), choosing \(u_r\in\{1,2,3,4\}\). Then the **complete, exact joint census** is
\[
J_L(u_r,u_r)=\sum_{\substack{0\le j\le L\\j\equiv r\pmod4}}\binom Lj2^{L-j},
\]
\[
J_L(0,u_r)=\frac{5^L-3^L+(-1)^r(3^L-1)}4,
\]
and \(J_L(a,d)=0\) for every other ordered pair. Both formulas include \(L=0\).

In particular, for every \(n\ge0\),
\[
D_n\not\equiv0\pmod5,\qquad A_n(D_n-A_n)\equiv0\pmod5.
\]
Exactly eight of the twenty-five ordered residue pairs occur for \(L\ge3\); they are \((u_r,u_r)\) and \((0,u_r)\), with \(0\le r<4\). Every one of those eight pairs occurs for infinitely many distinct indices. Of the indices below \(5^L\), exactly \(3^L\) have \(A_n\not\equiv0\pmod5\). Within the zero-Apéry indices, the counts for Domb residues \(1\) and \(4\) equal \((5^L-1)/4\), while those for residues \(3\) and \(2\) equal \((5^L-2\cdot3^L+1)/4\). This yields an exact pairwise excess \((3^L-1)/2\).

## Assumptions and scope

All sums are integer sums; no division modulo five is used. The Domb numbers are defined by the unsigned binomial sum above (not by an alternatingly signed normalization). The residue pair is ordered Apéry first, Domb second. An \(L\)-digit base-five expansion includes leading zeroes, and \(0\le n<5^L\). The result asserts the entire distribution for each finite \(L\), including the boundary cases \(L=0,1,2\), and infinite occurrence of each supported pair. It does not assert an analogous distribution modulo \(25\) or for another prime.

## Proof

**1. Independent base-five Lucas decompositions.** Write \(n=\sum_i n_i5^i\), where \(0\le n_i<5\). In the defining Apéry sum, a term can be nonzero modulo five only if \(j_i\le n_i\) for every \(i\) and the base-five addition of \(n+j\) is carry-free. Indeed, the first restriction follows by Lucas's binomial theorem applied to \(\binom nj\), while the second follows by the usual carry criterion for \(\binom{n+j}j\). Under these two restrictions, all binomial coefficients split as products of their single-digit coefficients. Summing independently over the permitted \(j_i\) gives
\[
A_n\equiv\prod_i A_{n_i}\pmod5.
\]
For the Domb sum, use the exact multinomial-square identity
\[
D_n=\sum_{a+b+c+d=n}\binom{n}{a,b,c,d}^2.
\]
To verify it, first group the summands by \(a+b=j\), then apply \(\sum_{a=0}^{j}\binom ja^2=\binom{2j}j\) and the corresponding identity for \(c+d\). Multinomial Lucas implies that the squared multinomial vanishes modulo five unless its four lower arguments sum digitwise to each digit of \(n\); otherwise the summand factors over digits. Hence
\[
D_n\equiv\prod_i D_{n_i}\pmod5.
\]
These are exact congruences at every nonnegative index, not claims inferred from finite data. Classical general Lucas theorems for the underlying sequences were established earlier; the two direct arguments here ensure the proof is self-contained.

**2. Single-digit table and possible pairs.** Exact integer evaluation at digits \(0,1,2,3,4\) gives
\[
(A_d\bmod5)_{d=0}^4=(1,0,3,0,1),\qquad
(D_d\bmod5)_{d=0}^4=(1,4,3,1,1).
\]
Thus every Domb digit factor is a unit. If any Apéry digit factor is zero, the first coordinate is zero and the second is a unit. Otherwise every digit belongs to \(\{0,2,4\}\), and the two digit factors agree at every position, giving the diagonal pair \((u_r,u_r)\). The allowed Domb unit group is generated by \(3\), so the eight families above contain every possible pair.

**3. Exact frequencies by cyclic coefficient extraction.** Work in the integral ring \(R=\mathbb Z[g]/(g^4-1)\), recording a nonzero residue \(3^r\bmod5\) by \(g^r\). The five digits contribute the Domb-residue weight
\[
B(g)=3+g+g^2,
\]
where the three constant contributions come from digits \(0,3,4\). Digits with nonzero Apéry factor, and hence diagonal pairs, contribute
\[
C(g)=2+g.
\]
The coefficient of \(g^r\) in \(C(g)^L\), reduced using \(g^4=1\), counts exactly the diagonal pair \((u_r,u_r)\), and its binomial expansion gives the first formula. The coefficient of \(g^r\) in \(B(g)^L-C(g)^L\) counts precisely \((0,u_r)\).

For any polynomial in \(R\), its four coefficients can be recovered by evaluating at \(g=1,-1,i,-i\) and Fourier inversion. At these values, \(B\) gives \(5,3,2+i,2-i\), whereas \(C\) gives \(3,1,2+i,2-i\). Therefore \(B^L-C^L\) evaluates to \(5^L-3^L\), \(3^L-1\), \(0\), \(0\), respectively. Fourier inversion gives
\[
[g^r](B^L-C^L)=\frac{5^L-3^L+(-1)^r(3^L-1)}4.
\]
This proves the second formula, including integrality, the structural zeros, and the total count \(5^L\).

**4. Attainability and infinite occurrence.** For \(L\ge3\), the four diagonal coefficients are all positive: take \(r\) copies of digit \(2\) and the remaining digits \(0\), for \(0\le r\le3\). For \(L\ge3\), every off-diagonal count is also positive by the displayed closed formula. Appending an additional most significant base-five digit \(4\) to any particular index multiplies the corresponding single-digit Apéry and Domb residues by \(1\) without changing the pair. Repeated appending yields infinitely many distinct integers, so every supported pair appears infinitely often.

## Verification

The standalone file `verify_apery_domb.py` recomputes Apéry and Domb numbers from their original integer sums for \(0\le n<300\), compares their residues to the digit formula, performs independent complete direct-sum censuses for \(0\le n<5^L\) at \(0\le L\le3\), complete digit-based censuses through \(L=4\), and checks the closed formulas through \(L=11\). It also checks total mass, the \(3^L\) nonzero-Apéry count, and the admissible-pair condition. These tests passed; the proof, not the finite tests, establishes the all-index claim.

For illustration, at \(L=3\) the four diagonal pair counts in the order \(u_0,u_1,u_2,u_3\) are \(8,12,6,1\); the four off-diagonal counts in the same order are \(31,18,31,18\). Their sum is \(125\).

## Relationship to prior work

Malik and Straub (2015 preprint; 2016 article), Theorem 3.1, proved Lucas congruences for sporadic Apéry-like sequences, extending Gessel's earlier theorem for Apéry numbers. Straub (first publicly posted 28 January 2023), Theorems 1.2 and 1.3, reviews those prime-modulus results and strengthens them modulo prime squares. These are stronger statements about **individual pointwise sequences**, and are fully credited; the single-sequence Lucas decomposition is not asserted here to be original. The additional output here is the evaluated **joint** Apéry–Domb residue distribution, its seventeen missing ordered pairs, and its exact finite-scale conditional bias. Once the existing Lucas theorem and the five digit values are known, this new enumerative conclusion can indeed be recovered by the explicit cyclic-ring calculation above; there is no claim of logical independence from those inputs.

Related work on Galois groups of the separate Apéry-like generating series modulo primes concerns their generating-function structure, rather than simultaneous indexwise frequency matrices. The literature searches and source inspections recorded in the accompanying scientific review did not identify an explicit equivalent joint enumeration. The priority risk that the same group-ring consequence is already implicit or noted elsewhere remains.

## Limitations

This theorem is an elementary explicit joint corollary of general Lucas-type machinery, not a new Lucas theorem or a prime-square congruence. No claim of first discovery, priority, independent validation, or full literature exhaustiveness is made. The joint distribution is specific to the canonical unsigned Domb sequence and modulus five; signed normalizations alter its labels. The checker verifies finitely many cases only and is not substituted for the mathematical proof.
