# Exact state complexity of modular distinct-subsequence counting without adjacent ones

## Finding
For every integer \(m\ge 2\), let \(P(w)\) be the number of distinct (not embedding-counted) scattered subsequences of a binary word \(w\), **including the empty word exactly once**. Define
\[
\mathcal{L}_m=\{w\in\{0,1\}^{*}:\text{the contiguous factor }11\text{ does not occur in }w,\quad P(w)\equiv0\pmod m\}.
\]
The unique minimal **complete** deterministic finite automaton for \(\mathcal L_m\) has
\[
\operatorname{sc}(\mathcal L_m)=2J_2(m)+1=2m^2\prod_{p\mid m}(1-p^{-2})+1
\]
states, where \(J_2\) is the second Jordan totient function. Every one of the two copies of the primitive residue-pair space is reachable and pairwise distinguishable, and the final state is a rejecting sink. Thus forbidding adjacent ones produces exactly twice the unrestricted primitive-state count plus one sink.

## Assumptions and scope
The alphabet is the labeled set \(\{0,1\}\); runs and subsequences have their ordinary linear, noncyclic meanings. A subsequence is a **distinct word**, independent of the number of embeddings. The congruence is computed modulo an arbitrary composite or prime integer \(m\ge2\). The state count applies to complete deterministic one-way finite automata and not to NFAs, transducers, or the number of accepted words at any fixed length.

Write \(R=\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z\). Call a column \(v=(x,y)^\mathsf T\in R^2\) primitive when \(\gcd(x,y,m)=1\), using arbitrary integer representatives. Let \(G=\operatorname{SL}_2(R)\) and let \(\ell=(1,1)\).

## Proof
**Step 1: exact counting coordinates.** For a word \(w\), let \(a(w)\) and \(b(w)\) be the numbers of distinct nonempty subsequences ending in \(0\) and \(1\), respectively. Set \(v(w)=(1+a(w),1+b(w))^\mathsf T\). Then \(v(\varepsilon)=(1,1)^\mathsf T\), and appending the symbols acts by
\[
T_0=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
T_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
v(wc)=T_cv(w).
\]
Indeed, appending \(0\) makes every subsequence ending in \(0\) consist uniquely of the earlier subsequences, including empty, followed by \(0\); the analogous statement holds for \(1\). It follows that \(P(w)=\ell v(w)-1\) over the integers and hence over \(R\). Both matrices have determinant one, so the reduced vector is always primitive.

**Step 2: a complete recognizing DFA.** Use the states \((v,b)\) for every primitive \(v\in R^2\) and \(b\in\{0,1\}\), plus a sink \(\bot\). The bit \(b\) records whether the most recently read symbol was \(1\). Initial state: \(((1,1)^\mathsf T,0)\). A letter \(0\) maps \((v,b)\) to \((T_0v,0)\). A letter \(1\) maps \((v,0)\) to \((T_1v,1)\) and \((v,1)\) to \(\bot\). The sink loops on both symbols. The accepting states are exactly those live states satisfying \(\ell v=1\) in \(R\). This recognizes precisely \(\mathcal L_m\).

**Step 3: the run restriction does not restrict the modular orbit.** Every word in the regular expression \((0\mid10)^*\) is legal, ends in zero if nonempty, and its action is generated by \(T_0\) and \(T_0T_1\) (composition of operators is read right-to-left). These two matrices generate the same subgroup as \(T_0,T_1\), because \(T_1=T_0^{-1}(T_0T_1)\). This subgroup equals \(G\). For completeness, the elementary unipotent matrices \(T_0,T_1\) and their inverses generate \(\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z)\): reduce modulo each prime power, use a unit coordinate of a primitive column to eliminate the other coordinate, and obtain a unit diagonal matrix as a product of elementary unipotents; combine the local operations with Chinese-remainder idempotents. Since \(G\) is finite, every inverse is a positive power, so the **positive** semigroup of the legal blocks also realizes every element of \(G\), including the identity using a nonempty word (for example, \(0^m\)).

The group \(G\) is transitive on primitive columns. To see this, given primitive \(v=(x,y)^\mathsf T\), choose \(a,b\in R\) with \(ax+by=1\). Then \(\begin{psmallmatrix}x&-b\\y&a\end{psmallmatrix}\in G\) sends the first standard basis vector to \(v\); and an elementary shear sends \((1,1)^\mathsf T\) to that basis vector. Therefore all primitive columns occur with last-bit \(0\); appending a final \(1\) to a suitable last-zero prefix reaches every primitive column with last-bit \(1\). The sink is reached by \(11\). Thus every proposed state is reachable.

**Step 4: no two distinct live states merge.** First consider \((v,b)\) and \((w,b)\) for the same last-bit \(b\) and distinct primitive columns. There is a primitive row \(r\in R^{1\times2}\) such that \(rv=1\) but \(rw\ne1\). To prove this, choose \(h\in G\) sending \(v\) to \(e_1=(1,0)^\mathsf T\), and write \(hw=(a,b')^\mathsf T\ne e_1\). If \(a\ne1\), take \(r=(1,0)h\). Otherwise \(b'\ne0\), and take \(r=(1,1)h\). Both rows are primitive. Since \(G\) acts transitively on primitive rows, there exists \(g\in G\) with \(\ell g=r\).

If the last-bit is zero, a suffix from \((0\mid10)^*\) can realize \(g\); if the last-bit is one, a suffix of the form \(0(0\mid10)^*\) can realize \(g\), since left multiplication by elements of \(G\) maps \(T_0\) onto all of \(G\). The chosen suffix is legal from both states and makes the first accepted and the second rejected. Thus every distinct pair with the same last-bit is distinguishable.

Next compare \((v,0)\) and \((w,1)\), whether or not \(v=w\). All suffixes beginning with \(1\) are rejected from the latter state, but from \((v,0)\) a suffix of the form \(10(0\mid10)^*\) realizes any prescribed element of \(G\) and is legal. Transitivity supplies a suffix taking \(v\) to \(e_1\), where \(\ell e_1=1\); thus that suffix is accepted from the first state. Finally, every live state has a legal accepting continuation (using an initial \(0\) when needed), whereas the sink has none. All states are pairwise Myhill–Nerode distinguishable.

**Step 5: count the states.** A residue pair is primitive exactly when it is not simultaneously divisible by any prime \(p\mid m\). Inclusion–exclusion/Chinese remaindering gives \(J_2(m)=m^2\prod_{p\mid m}(1-p^{-2})\) primitive pairs. There are two distinct last-bit copies plus one sink, which proves the displayed formula for every \(m\ge2\). \(\square\)

## Verification
Run `python3 checker.py` using Python 3 and no external dependencies. The script independently builds the complete modular automaton, computes reachable states and Myhill–Nerode refinement for all \(2\le m\le20\), and checks direct distinct-subsequence sets against recognition for every binary word of length at most ten. The checked cases comprise 9,538 transition entries and 38,893 direct word comparisons. These finite checks support the proof, but by themselves certify no infinite-domain statement. The mathematical proof in the preceding section establishes all moduli.

## Relationship to prior work
Żak, *Finding binary words with a given number of subsequences* (first public preprint 2022-10-01, arXiv:2210.00342), develops the integer coprime-pair correspondence for binary distinct-subsequence counts. The modular primitive orbit and the full exact minimal-state count **with an adjacent-one prohibition** are not stated there. Kovačević and Vukobratović, *Asymptotic Behavior and Typicality Properties of Runlength-Limited Sequences* (first public preprint 2021-05-10, arXiv:2105.04617), analyze constrained-run enumeration and information rates, not this modular Myhill–Nerode quotient. A previous exact unrestricted modular distinct-subsequence state count gives \(J_2(m)\) but cannot directly decide which states survive or merge after adjoining the factor-avoidance condition; the essential new step is the legal-block generation and two-copy distinguishability above. General product automata give an upper bound of three times the primitive-state count, not the exact value proved here. Other modular scattered-pattern automata count *embeddings of a fixed pattern*, which is a different statistic from the number of distinct subsequence **words**.

## Limitations
This concerns zero residue of the count **including empty**. Other modular target residues, higher run caps, larger alphabets, nondeterministic state complexity, and minimal transition monoid sizes are not claimed. The group-theoretic proof handles every modulus and does not rely on finite tests. Literature searching and inspection cannot rule out equivalent results outside accessible or indexed sources; independent mathematical and historical-priority review has not been performed.
