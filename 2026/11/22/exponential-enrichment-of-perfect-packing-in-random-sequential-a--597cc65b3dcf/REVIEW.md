# Self-review

## Correctness — PASS
The exact event is characterized by priority inequalities against the fixed alternating target. Conditioning on target priorities gives a nonnegative finite integral; the two-coordinate transfer operator reproduces that integral without independence approximations. The twice-iterated transfer has a bounded strictly positive compact kernel, so Jentzsch spectral theory gives an isolated simple positive eigenvalue and an exponentially small remainder. Product association and disjoint triple selection prove the two rate bounds. The Fibonacci count and two perfect configurations yield the exact uniform probability. Exhaustive small-ladder checks and discretized transfer estimates support the proof but are not substituted for the infinite argument.

## Originality — PASS, qualified
The 2015 exact probability-generating function for precisely this seating model is a strong preceding result; it already fixes every finite event probability, so the displayed finite integral is **not** itself credited as new. The reviewed candidate asserts the additional upper-endpoint spectral asymptotic with strict positive eigenvalue, explicit rigorous rate bounds and an exponential kinetic-to-uniform comparison, which the inspected paper does not state or prove. The 2023 ladder paper compares mean coverages, not the extreme perfect-packing probability. Relevant indexed comparison items concern different adsorption observables, models or blockade ranges. An endpoint asymptotic deducible by further analysis of the earlier full generating function remains an originality and publication-priority risk.

## Mathematical value — PASS
The maximally occupied crystalline configurations are a natural rare-event observable that central and local limit theorems do not quantify. The result compares two physically distinct terminal-state ensembles at the same exact extremal event, proving a growing exponential discrepancy despite both probabilities tending to zero. The sharp exponential rate is a natural invariant of the dynamic jamming process and can be computed by a compact transfer operator. The result does not claim a general large-deviation principle or a stronger result than the prior exact finite probability-generating function.
