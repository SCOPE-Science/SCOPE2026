# Five Weil spectra and four simple Prym types for cyclic-six quartics over \(\mathbb F_{17}\)

## Finding
Let \(k=\mathbb F_{17}\), let \(D=k\setminus\{0,2,-2\}\), and for every \(r\in D\) consider the smooth plane quartic
\[
C_r:\qquad X^3Z+Y^4+rY^2Z^2+Z^4=0.
\]
Write \(L_r(T)\) for the numerator of its Hasse–Weil zeta function, normalized by
\[
Z(C_r,T)=\frac{L_r(T)}{(1-T)(1-17T)}.
\]

**Theorem (complete coefficient-marked arithmetic classification).** All fourteen curves have \(18\) rational points over \(k\). Their degree-six Weil numerators take exactly five values, given by
\[
L_r(T)=(1+17T^2)(1+d_r T^2+289T^4).
\]
The following table is exhaustive; parameters are interpreted modulo \(17\). The last column counts coefficient-marked equations.

| Parameters \(r\) | \(\#C_r(\mathbb F_{289})\) | \(d_r\) | Marked equations |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| \(\pm1\) | 284 | -20 | 2 |
| \(\pm3\) | 380 | 28 | 2 |
| \(\pm4,\pm7\) | 344 | 10 | 4 |
| \(\pm5\) | 392 | 34 | 2 |
| \(\pm6,\pm8\) | 356 | 16 | 4 |

The seven geometric isomorphism classes represented by \(r=1,3,4,5,6,7,8\) thus produce five distinct \(k\)-isogeny classes of Jacobians. In particular, the geometrically nonisomorphic pairs represented by \(r=4,7\) and by \(r=6,8\) have \(k\)-isogenous Jacobians. Every member admits a degree-two elliptic quotient \(E_r\), with \(L_{E_r}(T)=1+17T^2\); the complementary dimension-two Prym \(P_r\) has numerator \(1+d_r T^2+289T^4\). For the twelve members with \(r\ne\pm5\), the Pryms are simple over \(k\), falling into four distinct isogeny classes. For \(r=\pm5\), \(P_r\) is isogenous to the square of a trace-zero supersingular elliptic curve and \(L_r(T)=(1+17T^2)^3\). Precisely these two marked members are maximal over \(\mathbb F_{289}\), having \(392\) points.

For every positive odd integer \(n\) and every \(r\in D\), one furthermore has \(\#C_r(\mathbb F_{17^n})=17^n+1\).

## Assumptions and scope
The ground field is exactly \(k=\mathbb F_{17}\) and the parameter set is exactly \(D\); \(r=0\) defines a smooth curve with a larger geometric automorphism group but is not in this classification, while \(r=\pm2\) give singular plane quartics. The isomorphism-class count refers to isomorphism after extending \(k\) to its algebraic closure; isogeny and Prym simplicity are over \(k\). No assertion is made that the marked equations exhaust all arithmetic twists of the geometric curves. The genus is three for every member.

## Proof
**1. Smoothness, genus and the point at infinity.** On \(Z=1\), the equation is \(x^3=-(y^4+ry^2+1)\). Its quartic branch polynomial is squarefree precisely when \(r^2\ne4\): a common root of \(y^4+ry^2+1\) and \(4y^3+2ry\) would force \(y^2=-r/2\) and \(1-r^2/4=0\). Equivalently, the projective Jacobian criterion shows that \(C_r\) is smooth exactly when \(r\ne\pm2\). A smooth plane quartic has genus three. The line \(Z=0\) meets the curve only at \([1:0:0]\), which is smooth and rational.

**2. Every odd-degree extension has the base count.** When \(n\) is odd, \(17^n\equiv2\pmod 3\). Consequently the cube map is a bijection on \(\mathbb F_{17^n}\). For each affine \(y\), there is exactly one affine \(x\), and there is exactly one point at infinity. Hence \(\#C_r(\mathbb F_{17^n})=17^n+1\), in particular \(\#C_r(k)=18\). The degree-six zeta numerator is therefore even, because all odd Frobenius power sums vanish. By its functional equation it must have the form
\[
L_r(T)=1+c_rT^2+17c_rT^4+17^3T^6.
\]
Newton's identity at degree two gives
\[
c_r=\frac{\#C_r(\mathbb F_{289})-(17^2+1)}2.
\]
Thus a single exact quadratic-field count per parameter completely determines the Weil numerator, including all higher extension-field point counts.

**3. Exhaustive quadratic counts.** Set \(K=\mathbb F_{17}[u]/(u^2+u+1)\), an extension of degree two, and let \(F_r(y)=-(y^4+ry^2+1)\). For \(z\in K\), the number of solutions of \(x^3=z\) is one if \(z=0\), three if \(z\ne0\) and \(z^{96}=1\), and zero otherwise. The exact finite identity
\[
\#C_r(K)=1+\sum_{y\in K}\#\{x\in K:x^3=F_r(y)\}
\]
is evaluated for **every** \(y\in K\) and every one of the fourteen parameters. The output is the second column of the table. The standalone `verify.py` recomputes all counts in two independent quadratic polynomial-basis presentations of \(K\), by two counting rules: direct enumeration of cube fibers and the exponent-\(96\) cube-character test. The two representations are defined by \(u^2+u+1\) and \(v^2+3\), both irreducible over \(k\). Every representation/method pair gives exactly the same full table.

Substituting the counts gives \(c_r=-3,45,27,51,33\) in the displayed row order. The identity
\[
1+c_rT^2+17c_rT^4+17^3T^6
=(1+17T^2)(1+(c_r-17)T^2+289T^4)
\]
then yields the five stated \(d_r\).

**4. Elliptic quotient and Prym.** The involution \((X:Y:Z)\mapsto(X:-Y:Z)\) has quotient function field generated by \(x\) and \(t=y^2\). Completing the square and putting \(V=t+r/2\), \(U=-x\), gives the elliptic curve
\[
E_r:\quad V^2=U^3+(r^2-4)/4.
\]
Its constant is nonzero on \(D\), and it has its rational point at infinity. For every \(V\in k\) there is a unique \(U\in k\), since cubing is bijective, so \(\#E_r(k)=18\) and \(L_{E_r}(T)=1+17T^2\). The degree-two quotient supplies an isogeny \(\operatorname{Jac}(C_r)\sim_k E_r\times P_r\), with \(P_r\) a dimension-two Prym. Its Weil numerator is the complementary quartic in the theorem.

For \(d\in\{-20,28,10,16\}\), \(Q_d(T)=1+dT^2+289T^4\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\): a factorization into conjugate quadratics either requires \(d^2-4\cdot289\) to be a rational square, which is impossible because it is negative, or requires one of \(34-d\) or \(-34-d\) to be a rational square. Their values are respectively \(54,6,24,18\) and four strictly negative integers, none a rational square. A rational linear factor would also produce a quadratic factor in \(T^2\), already ruled out. Therefore each corresponding Prym is simple over \(k\). Distinct characteristic polynomials give distinct \(k\)-isogeny classes by the finite-field isogeny theorem. For \(d=34\), \(Q_d=(1+17T^2)^2\) instead, so the Prym is isogenous to \(E_r^2\).

**5. Pairwise geometric distinction and maximality.** The published geometric classification states that for smooth \(C_r,C_s\), one has \(C_r\cong_{\bar k}C_s\) if and only if \(r^2=s^2\) (Ohashi–Kudo–Harashita, Proposition 2.2.4). In the present seven representative values the squares are pairwise distinct. The equality of Weil factors for \(r=4,7\) and \(r=6,8\) therefore gives two genuine pairs of geometrically nonisomorphic but \(k\)-isogenous Jacobians. The Hasse–Weil upper bound over \(\mathbb F_{289}\) is \(289+1+2(3)17=392\); the table shows equality only at \(r=\pm5\).

**6. A direct Cartier–Manin check at the exceptional parameters.** To verify the stronger geometric superspecial conclusion rather than infer it from the Frobenius polynomial alone, use the canonical adjoint-monomial triples \(v_1=(2,1,1)\), \(v_2=(1,2,1)\), \(v_3=(1,1,2)\). The unrooted Hasse–Witt entry \(H_{ij}\) is the coefficient of \(X^{17(v_i)_1-(v_j)_1}Y^{17(v_i)_2-(v_j)_2}Z^{17(v_i)_3-(v_j)_3}\) in \( (X^3Z+Y^4+rY^2Z^2+Z^4)^{16}\). Exact multinomial coefficient extraction in `verify.py` gives the zero matrix at precisely \(r=\pm5\). At the other twelve parameters the only nonzero entries are \(H_{13}\) and \(H_{31}\), both units in \(\mathbb F_{17}\). Hence the geometric Cartier \(a\)-number equals \(3\) at \(r=\pm5\) and \(1\) otherwise; iterating the semilinear matrix shows geometric \(17\)-rank \(0\) and \(2\), respectively. In particular the two maximizers are superspecial in the Cartier-vanishing sense, and this agrees independently with the point-count spectrum.

## Verification
Run `python3 verify.py` with Python 3 and no third-party packages. The checker verifies irreducibility of the two defining quadratic polynomials by evaluating them at every element of \(\mathbb F_{17}\), enumerates all \(289\) elements in each field, computes all fourteen quadratic point counts with both cube-root counting methods, verifies the \(r\mapsto-r\) pairings, reconstructs all five polynomials, checks four quartic irreducibility criteria, and checks the unique maximal fiber, and reconstructs every entry of the \(3\)-by-\(3\) Hasse–Witt matrix for all fourteen parameters. A successful run emits `CYCLIC6_F17_FIVE_WEIL_SPECTRA_VERIFIED` with the precise census statistics. All operations are exact integers reduced modulo \(17\); no sampled or randomized step is used.

## Relationship to prior work
Lercier–Ritzenthaler–Rovetta–Sijsling (arXiv:1403.0562, 2014) supply the genus-three automorphism stratum and a large finite-field quartic enumeration. Their published experiment reports ground-field rational-point data for \(\mathbb F_{17}\), not the complete quadratic counts or five Prym isogeny polynomials here. Ohashi–Kudo–Harashita (arXiv:2111.09777, first posted November 18, 2021) give the exact \(C_6\)-family, the geometric \(r^2\) isomorphism criterion, Hasse–Witt and \(p\)-rank strata, and a computational affirmative answer for existence of a maximal form for primes up to 300. None of those known results is claimed new here. Liang (arXiv:1902.02914, 2019) computes bitangent and invariant information on the same geometric locus, not the quadratic-field Weil spectrum.

The additional classification is motivated by the fact that all these quartics have identical \(\mathbb F_{17}\)-point counts, while two pairs of distinct geometric classes nevertheless have equal complete Weil polynomials. Similar published isogeny classifications for other curve families do not imply this parameter-specific result.

## Limitations
The result is exact for the specified fourteen coefficient-marked models over \(\mathbb F_{17}\), not a classification of every arithmetic twist or every prime characteristic. The geometric class distinction uses the established isomorphism criterion of Ohashi–Kudo–Harashita. The finite calculation is exhaustive in the field of size \(289\), but makes no general statement for arbitrary prime \(p\). The novelty assessment does not establish exhaustive historical priority; an unpublished or unindexed full-Weil table from earlier census data remains a possible overlap risk.
