# The algebraic distribution of distinct matched-pair erasure outputs of Dyck words

## Finding

For a Dyck word \(w\) of semilength \(n\), let \(D(w)\) be the number of distinct words obtained by erasing the endpoints of one pair matched in the source. Put \(D(\epsilon)=0\). The complete distribution is given by
\[
F(z,u)=\sum_{n\ge0}z^n\sum_{w\in\mathcal D_n} u^{n-D(w)},\qquad
zF^2-\bigl(1+z+z^2-uz(1+z)\bigr)F+(1+z-uz)=0,
\]
with the unique formal solution \(F(0,u)=1\). In particular, for each \(n\ge2\) the maximum of \(D\) is \(n-1\), attained by exactly \(F_{n+1}\) words, where \(F_1=F_2=1\) are Fibonacci numbers. The minimum is \(1\), attained by the fully nested and fully flat words. The contribution is the distribution on Dyck sources and its sharp maximum count, not a separate claim of novelty for the underlying collision classification.

## Assumptions and scope

The two erased positions must be a matching opening and closing parenthesis in the original source. Compare output words literally, not modulo reversal or rotation. In the rooted plane tree of a Dyck word, the extra root is not erasable. Vertex order is the children's given plane order. No claim is made for arbitrary deletions of one opening and one closing parenthesis, or for multiple pair erasures.

## Proof

**The statistic counted by the series.** Let \(U\) be the number of nonroot vertices having just one child, and \(L\) the number of adjacent pairs of leaf children of a common parent, including the root. Then \(n-D=U+L\). Here the equivalence classes are generated by deleting either end of a unary parent-child link or either member of an adjacent leaf-sibling pair. This is also a specialization of the Stirling-permutation erasure theorem [1]: label the matched pairs by opening order and write their labels at both endpoints. This gives a Stirling permutation. Erasing a label and standardizing preserves the opening-order labelling, so equality of the standardized outputs is exactly literal equality in the Dyck encoding. The nested clause is the unary chain, the increasing sibling clause is the adjacent leaf run, and the decreasing clause cannot occur in opening order.

For a direct proof of the statistic, suppose first the erased pairs are disjoint. After cancellation of the common outer context their outputs agree exactly when \(AQ(B)=(A)QB\), where \(A,B\) are balanced Dyck interiors and \(Q\) is the intervening substring. A nonempty \(A\) yields a return before the first return of \((A)\), an impossibility. If \(B\ne\epsilon\), the final \(|B|\) symbols would force \(B\) to equal its tail with a closing parenthesis appended, impossible for a nonempty Dyck word. Thus \(Q()=()Q\), forcing \(Q\) to consist of zero or more copies of \(()\). In the nested case, deleting the outer vertex releases the forest of its children; deleting the inner vertex leaves one irreducible tree. Equality forces the outer vertex to have a unique child. If that child is not the deleted inner vertex, cancellation of the outer parentheses reduces the comparison to the child versus the inner vertex. The converse follows by the same steps. Both kinds of links form paths; a member of a leaf run of length at least two cannot also be an only child. Thus there are \(U+L\) edges on \(n\) vertices in this union of paths, proving the statistic.

**Forest enumeration.** Give each nonroot vertex weight \(z\), each unary nonroot vertex an extra factor \(u\), and each adjacent pair of leaf siblings an extra factor \(u\). Write \(T\) for the tree series (with its first vertex weighted), and \(F\) for the forest series. Write \(F_{\rm L}\), \(F_{\rm N}\) for the series of nonempty forests ending with a leaf or with a nonleaf, respectively. A tree whose root has exactly one child gets the unary factor; appending a leaf to a leaf-ending forest gets the sibling factor. Thus
\[
T=z\bigl(F+(u-1)T\bigr),\qquad
F_{\rm L}=z\bigl(F+(u-1)F_{\rm L}\bigr),\qquad
F_{\rm N}=(T-z)F,\qquad F=1+F_{\rm L}+F_{\rm N}.
\]
The first two equations imply \(T=F_{\rm L}=zF/(1+z-uz)\). Substitution gives the quadratic; its constant coefficient equation fixes \(F(0,u)=1\), and every higher coefficient of \(F\) is determined by the lower ones. At \(u=1\) it is the ordinary Catalan equation.

**Extrema.** In a tree of at least two nonroot vertices, the parent of any deepest leaf either is a nonroot unary vertex or has at least two leaf children. Hence \(U+L\ge1\). The quadratic gives \(F(z,0)=1+z\) and
\[
[u]F(z,u)=\frac{z^2(2+z)}{1-z-z^2}=2z^2+3z^3+5z^4+8z^5+\cdots.
\]
Its coefficient at \(z^n\) is \(F_{n+1}\) for \(n\ge2\), proving the sharp upper tail. If \(D=1\), the collision path spans the tree's nonroot vertices. A unary path of this kind is the fully nested word, and a path of leaf siblings spanning all nonroot vertices is the flat word. Both have one output.

## Verification and limitations

The supplied `verify_matching.py` is a standard-library finite checker for the literal output classes, coefficient distributions and extrema through semilength ten (23,713 nonempty Dyck words). The all-length proof is the collision argument and weighted grammar above. The finite checks do not test originality or channels with unmatched erasures.

## Relationship to earlier results and references

1. *Pair-erasure collision rigidity in Stirling-permutation words*, [record 35167ae70630](https://github.com/Resultary/2026/tree/main/2026/11/22/SCOPE-pair-erasure-collision-rigidity-in-stirling-permutation-words--35167ae70630), theorem in the Finding section. The pair-collision counts in that record range over all Stirling permutations with increasing ancestor labels, not just the canonical opening-order labelings used here. The specialization supplies the pointwise statistic but not the bivariate Dyck distribution. No priority order between the same-day records is asserted.
2. A. Bernini, L. Ferrari, R. Pinzani, J. West, *The Dyck pattern poset*, [arXiv:1303.3785](https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.3785), Proposition 2.1. Its rank-one predecessors allow unmatched erasures. For example, \(()()()\) has two general predecessors but only one matched-pair output. That wider count does not give this distribution.
3. W. Y. C. Chen, N. Y. Li, L. W. Shapiro, *The Butterfly Decomposition of Plane Trees*, [arXiv:math/0511045](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0511045), for plane-tree encodings and related enumeration. The chains discussed there are selections of vertices on a root-to-leaf path, not the deletion-equivalence classes used here.

These comparisons do not establish exhaustive historical priority for the generating function; equivalent formulations in the literature on plane-tree statistics remain possible.
