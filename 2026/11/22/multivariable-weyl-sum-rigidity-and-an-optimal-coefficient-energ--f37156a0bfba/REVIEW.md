# Review of the multivariable affine-Weyl sum theorem

## Correctness — PASS
The claim specifies the Gaussian Hilbert Fock normalization, the complex-linear Hermitian pairing, any finite canonical unitary-affine family and nonzero entire weights. The necessity argument is **direct**: the adjoint on coherent kernels gives a sum of normalized kernels with weighted entire coefficients; on every generic complex ray, the kernel Gram matrix is asymptotically block diagonal according to coincident unitary parts. Blocks of distinct translation centers are uniformly positive definite by a finite Vandermonde independence argument and a common Weyl translation, while distinct unitary parts separate exponentially along the ray. This gives a uniform positive minimum Gram eigenvalue for sufficiently large complex ray parameters; Liouville forces every compensated weight to be constant. Sufficiency is a direct Gaussian change of variables. The exact coherent-kernel cross terms vanish in large-ball averages by either dominated convergence or Riemann–Lebesgue; compact operators vanish uniformly on escaping coherent kernels, yielding the essential-norm bound. Periodic quadratic chirps give equality for every number of summands through exact character cancellation. The packaged deterministic diagnostic confirms 9,448 supplementary finite kernel/phase/Fourier checks; it is not used as an infinite proof.

## Mathematical value — PASS
The result isolates a structurally natural maximal-isometric boundary from the several-variable Fock composition theory and resolves an entire-weight, arbitrary-finite-sum classification there even when unitary parts do not commute or translations are present. It gives a sharp **symbol-independent** quantitative no-cancellation estimate modulo compact operators, a complete coherent-state energy limit and explicit sharpness for every number of summands. The motivated object is the finite Weyl-affine family, and the invariant is its uniform Calkin separation; these are not arbitrary finite numerical slices or known-table recomputations.
