# Optimal powerful alliances of the Heawood graph form a Coxeter double-cover line graph

## Finding
Let \(H\) be the incidence graph of the Fano plane. The minimum cardinality of a *global powerful alliance* of \(H\) is \(8\), and there are exactly \(84\) such alliances. These are naturally indexed by **ordered adjacent vertices** of the Coxeter graph \(C\), defined here on the \(28\) noncollinear Fano triples, adjacent when disjoint. Under one-vertex token jumping between minimum alliances, their reconfiguration graph is canonically
\[
\mathcal R_{\mathrm{TJ}}(H)\cong L(C\times K_2),
\]
where the product is the bipartite (Kronecker) double cover and \(L\) denotes the line graph. It has \(84\) vertices, is connected and \(4\)-regular, and has diameter \(7\), with distance-layer sizes \((1,4,8,16,28,22,4,1)\) from every vertex. Under adjacent-vertex token sliding, all \(84\) optimal alliances are isolated.

Equivalently, the complement of every optimum consists of three selected Fano points and three selected Fano lines: the three lines form a nonconcurrent triple; the three points are the unique point on none of those lines and two of the three points lying on exactly one of those lines. Conversely, every such choice gives an optimum. The \(84\) optima form one orbit under the \(168\) point–line-preserving collineations of the Fano plane.

## Assumptions and scope
The Fano plane has point set \(\{0,\ldots,6\}\) and lines \(\ell_i=\{i,i+1,i+3\}\), with arithmetic modulo \(7\). The fourteen vertices of \(H\) are the seven points and seven lines; incidence is adjacency. A set \(S\subseteq V(H)\) is *global powerful* precisely when each vertex \(v\) satisfies
\[
|N[v]\cap S|\ge |N[v]\setminus S|.
\]
It is global because the inequality forces domination in this cubic graph. For an arbitrary cubic graph the condition is equivalent to every vertex in \(S\) having at least one neighbor in \(S\), and every vertex outside \(S\) having at least two neighbors in \(S\). A token jump replaces one selected vertex by one unselected vertex; a token slide additionally requires adjacency between them. Only cardinality-minimum alliances are vertices of the two reconfiguration graphs.

A *triangle* of the Fano plane means a three-point set not contained in a line. There are \(\binom{7}{3}-7=28\) triangles. Define \(C\) on these triangles, with an edge exactly when the underlying point triples are disjoint. This is the standard Fano-plane model of the Coxeter graph. The bipartite double \(C\times K_2\) has vertex set \(V(C)\times\{0,1\}\) and edges \((P,0)(Q,1)\) for each ordered pair \((P,Q)\) of adjacent vertices of \(C\).

## Proof
**1. Cardinality bound.** Put \(T=V(H)\setminus S\) and let \(e(S,T)\) be the number of crossing edges. The cubic powerful-alliance condition gives \(e(S,T)\le2|S|\) (every selected vertex retains a selected neighbor) and \(e(S,T)\ge2|T|\) (every unselected vertex has at most one unselected neighbor). Hence \(|S|\ge7\). If \(|S|=7\), equality holds in both inequalities, so every vertex of \(S\) has exactly one neighbor in \(S\). This would be a perfect matching on seven vertices, impossible. Therefore \(|S|\ge8\).

**2. Geometry of eight-sets.** Suppose \(|S|=8\), hence \(|T|=6\). Any three selected points of \(T\) cannot lie on a Fano line: if the corresponding line-vertex belongs to \(T\), it has three neighbors in \(T\), and if it belongs to \(S\), it has no neighbor in \(S\). Both contradict the condition. Dually, no three selected line-vertices of \(T\) can be concurrent. Thus the point and line selections of \(T\) are caps in the respective seven-element planes, each of size at most four: five selected points would determine ten distinct secant lines, but the Fano plane has only seven lines. A four-point cap has six distinct secants; those already account for all twelve point–line incidences on the four points, so the remaining seventh line misses them and is their three-point complement. Their sizes must therefore be \((2,4)\), \((3,3)\) or \((4,2)\). If there were four selected points, they would form the complement of a Fano line. Every other Fano line would contain exactly two of those points, so at most one selected line-vertex could have at most one selected point as neighbor; but \(T\) would contain two line-vertices. Hence \((4,2)\) is impossible. By duality \((2,4)\) is impossible. It follows that both shores of \(T\) have exactly three vertices.

The three selected lines of \(T\) are nonconcurrent. They have three distinct pairwise-intersection points (each incident with two selected lines), three private points (each on exactly one selected line), and one point on none of the three. The selected points of \(T\) must avoid all three pairwise intersections because a vertex of \(T\) can have at most one neighbor in \(T\). Among the remaining four points, the three private points are collinear: for instance, after a projective change of coordinates taking the three selected lines to the coordinate axes in \(\mathbb F_2^3\), they are \((0,1,1)\), \((1,0,1)\), \((1,1,0)\). The selected points cannot be these three because of the no-collinear-triple rule. Thus they consist of the unique off-line point plus two private points. Conversely, such a configuration has maximum degree one on \(T\) and leaves no isolated vertex of \(H[S]\), so its complement is a global powerful alliance. There are \(\binom{7}{3}-7=28\) choices of nonconcurrent line triples and three choices of the omitted private point, giving exactly \(28\cdot3=84\) optima and establishing the minimum size.

**3. Coxeter correspondence.** Let \(P\) be the three point-vertices of \(T\), and let \(Q\) be the three pairwise-intersection points of its three line-vertices. By the preceding construction, \(P\) and \(Q\) are disjoint Fano triangles. The three selected lines are exactly the side lines of triangle \(Q\). Conversely, given disjoint Fano triangles \((P,Q)\), the three lines joining pairs of \(Q\) and the three points of \(P\) construct a unique optimum, because among the four points outside \(Q\) the three private points form the sole collinear triple. Each Fano triangle has exactly three disjoint Fano triangles: its four-point complement has four triples, precisely one of which is a line. Hence the \(84\) optima biject with the \(28\cdot3=84\) ordered edges \((P,Q)\) of \(C\).

**4. Reconfiguration graph.** Two different optima differ by one selected vertex exactly when the corresponding ordered pairs have the same \(P\) or the same \(Q\). Indeed, with one coordinate fixed, the other has three admissible values, all pairwise differing in exactly one point or exactly one line-vertex; changing both coordinates changes vertices on both shores. Thus the jump graph is the line graph of the bipartite graph with a point-side copy and a line-side copy of the \(28\) triangles, and edges \(P_0Q_1\) precisely when \(P,Q\) are disjoint. This is \(C\times K_2\). Because all optimal selections contain four vertices on each shore of \(H\), replacing a selected point by a selected line, or vice versa, cannot preserve optimality. Every edge of \(H\) joins the two shores, so no optimal token slide is possible.

The Coxeter graph is connected and nonbipartite (indeed, it has girth seven), so its bipartite double and the associated line graph are connected. Each ordered edge of this cubic graph meets exactly four other edges, giving degree four. The explicit finite distance calculation in the independent verification programs gives the stated diameter and uniform distance profile. Finally, the Fano collineation group acts transitively on a nonconcurrent triple of lines with one distinguished private line/omitted private point: a triple of independent line equations is a basis of the dual three-space, and any permutation extends to an invertible linear map. Hence all optima lie in one orbit.

## Verification
The standalone programs `check_geometry.py` and `check_bruteforce.cpp` use the same explicitly stated Fano incidence convention but implement their tests separately. The first constructs the 28 geometric triangles and all 84 ordered disjoint pairs, checks exact equality with the directly tested 8-sets, and evaluates every graph distance. The second scans all \(2^{14}=16{,}384\) labeled vertex subsets by C++17 bitmasks and separately constructs all optimum-preserving one-token replacements. Both confirm 84 minimum alliances, 168 unordered jump edges, no token-slide edges, connectedness, diameter seven and the same distance profile. To reproduce, run `python3 check_geometry.py` and `g++ -O2 -std=c++17 check_bruteforce.cpp -o check_bruteforce && ./check_bruteforce`. The programs use only Python's standard library and a C++17 compiler; the latter program does not depend on Python.

## Relationship to prior work
Brigham, Dutton, Haynes and Hedetniemi introduced powerful alliances and global powerful alliances; Brigham and Dutton established a half-order lower bound for regular graphs. Those results do not classify the Heawood optimum sets. Fernau and Mann study general token-jumping and token-sliding reconfiguration of alliances, proving complexity and parameterized results; their inspected article does not give this Heawood-to-Coxeter configuration-space identification. The classical Fano-triangle construction of the Coxeter graph and the line-graph/Kronecker-cover operations are used here as known objects, not claimed as new. The new statement is the explicit, exact correspondence between these objects and minimum global powerful alliances of the Heawood graph.

## Limitations
The result concerns the fourteen-vertex Heawood graph and minimum-cardinality alliances. It does not classify larger inclusion-minimal alliances or give complexity bounds for other graph families. The structural correspondence is proved directly; the diameter and distance profile use complete finite verification on the 84 optimum vertices. Publication priority is assessed against inspected sources and indexed mathematical findings, not independently certified.
