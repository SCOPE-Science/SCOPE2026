# Seven-edge exchange bridges between minimum maximal matchings of \(\operatorname{PG}(2,4)\)

## Finding
Let \(G\) be the point–line incidence graph of the Desarguesian projective plane \(\operatorname{PG}(2,4)\), with 21 point-vertices, 21 line-vertices and 105 incidence edges. A matching is *maximal* when no additional incidence edge can be inserted. A minimum maximal matching is a maximal matching of smallest cardinality. Then:

1. The smallest maximal matching has 15 edges. Its six unmatched points constitute a hyperoval \(X\) (six points with no collinear triple), and its six unmatched lines are *exactly* the six lines disjoint from \(X\). Conversely every hyperoval and every perfect matching of its 15-by-15 residual incidence graph determines one such minimum maximal matching. There are 168 hyperovals, each residual graph is the Tutte–Coxeter eight-cage and has 288 perfect matchings; thus there are exactly **48,384** minimum maximal matchings.
2. Let \(\mathcal R_t\) be the graph on these 48,384 optimal matchings, with distinct matchings adjacent if one can replace at most \(t\) selected edges simultaneously, preserving matching size and maximality at the resulting endpoint. For \(t\le3\), all vertices are isolated. For \(4\le t\le6\), there are exactly 168 connected components, each on 288 vertices and consisting of matchings with the same unmatched hyperoval. For \(t=7\), the graph is connected. Therefore the exact connectivity threshold is \(\boxed{t=7}\).
3. For two distinct six-point hyperovals \(X,X'\), there can be a seven-edge exchange between their fibers *if and only if* \(|X\cap X'|=3\). The two corresponding six-line nonincidence sets also intersect in exactly three lines. For every such unordered pair of fibers, precisely **216** unordered seven-edge exchange edges connect them. There are exactly **3,360** such hyperoval pairs, and each of the 48,384 optimal matchings has exactly **30** exchange neighbors in other fibers.
4. The exact seven-exchange graph has **1,669,248** edges, partitioned into **943,488** within-fiber edges and **725,760** between-fiber edges. Its degree sequence has exactly 24,192 vertices of degree 67 and 24,192 vertices of degree 71. The fiber-quotient graph is 40-regular on 168 vertices, connected and of diameter two.

The static edge-domination optimum, the hyperoval count, and the single-fiber Tutte–Coxeter perfect-matching flip graph are separately established in earlier work and are not claimed as the novelty. The new contribution is the complete optimal-matching exchange threshold and the cross-fiber seven-edge bridge and degree census.

## Assumptions and scope
All graphs and configurations are finite and labeled, with no quotient by automorphisms. Construct the field \(\mathbb F_4=\mathbb F_2[a]/(a^2+a+1)\). Points are one-dimensional subspaces of \(\mathbb F_4^3\), represented by their unique normalized nonzero triple; lines are the kernels of normalized nonzero linear forms. Two vertices (a point and a line) are adjacent when the form evaluates to zero at the point. There are 21 vertices of each type and each vertex has degree five.

Write \(\mathcal F_X\) for the set of minimum maximal matchings whose unmatched point set is exactly \(X\). Each \(\mathcal F_X\) contains 288 configurations. A move in \(\mathcal R_t\) replaces \(k\) edges by \(k\) other edges for some \(1\le k\le t\); only the two endpoints need be valid optima, not transient intermediate partial edge sets. The move distance is the number of removed edges, namely \(|M\setminus M'|\). This is a batch-change model, not the standard alternating four-cycle switch chain and not the point-only hyperoval exchange graph.

## Proof
**A. A universal bound on unmatched vertices.** A matching \(M\) of size \(21-k\) leaves \(k\) points \(X\) and \(k\) lines \(Y\) unmatched. It is maximal exactly when no point in \(X\) lies on a line in \(Y\). For each line \(\ell\) let \(d_\ell\) be the number of points of \(X\) on \(\ell\). Since each point lies on five lines and every distinct point pair determines exactly one line, \(\sum_\ell d_\ell=5k\) and \(\sum_\ell d_\ell^2=k(k+4)\). If \(s\) lines meet \(X\), Cauchy–Schwarz gives \(s\ge25k/(k+4)\); the existence of \(Y\) requires \(s\le21-k\). Rearrangement yields \(k^2+8k\le84\), hence \(k\le6\) and every maximal matching has at least 15 edges. Equality forces exactly 15 secant lines, each meeting the six unmatched points twice, and six external lines. Thus \(X\) is a six-point hyperoval and \(Y\) is its full six-line external set.

**B. Attainability and the fibers.** For each six-point hyperoval \(X\), delete its six points and the six external lines. The remaining 15 point-vertices and 15 line-vertices form a 3-regular bipartite graph: every remaining line contains two hyperoval points and three others, while every remaining point meets three of the 15 secants. Hall's theorem guarantees a perfect matching, which becomes a maximal 15-edge matching of \(G\). In fact the 15 secants correspond to duads of six hyperoval points, and the 15 remaining points to partitions into three disjoint duads (synthemes), yielding the classical duad–syntheme incidence graph, the Tutte–Coxeter graph. The exhaustive independent checkers construct all \(\binom{21}{6}=54,264\) candidate sets and find exactly 168 hyperovals; each resulting residual graph has exactly 288 perfect matchings, without assuming the graph-isomorphism recognition for enumeration. Therefore exactly \(168\cdot288=48,384\) optimal matchings exist.

**C. Exchange lower bounds.** Within one fiber all matchings use the same 15 remaining points and 15 remaining lines. A bipartite perfect matching's symmetric difference consists of alternating even cycles, and the residual graph has girth eight, so changing a nontrivial matching inside one fiber requires at least four edges. The 288-state graph under four-edge changes is connected (also established by prior research and independently checked here). If two different fibers have hyperovals \(X,X'\) with \(r=|X\cap X'|\), then the unmatched line sets \(Y,Y'\) have the same intersection cardinality \(r\), as confirmed for every pair by complete incidence enumeration. Exactly \(6-r\) point-vertices and \(6-r\) line-vertices unmatched under the first matching must become matched under the second. No newly inserted edge can match one of these points to one of these lines simultaneously: that would be an incidence between \(X\) and \(Y\), excluded by maximality of the first matching. Thus at least \(2(6-r)\) new edges are required. Distinct hyperovals intersect in at most three points (equivalently, every four-arc completes to only one hyperoval), so no cross-fiber exchange uses fewer than six edges; if \(r\le2\), no cross-fiber exchange uses fewer than eight.

**D. Exhaustive bridge certificate for the remaining borderline case.** The only possible cross-fiber moves of size at most seven join hyperoval pairs with \(r=3\). The independent `check_affine.cpp` and `check_homogeneous.py` enumerate all such pairs (exactly 3,360) and, for each, all \(288^2=82,944\) pairs of perfect matchings in the two 15-by-15 residual graphs. Both independently compute the number of common incidence edges of each pair, and hence the exchange size \(15-|M\cap M'|\). Every such pair requires at least seven edges, and **exactly 216** pairs of matchings require exactly seven. The other hyperoval intersection populations are respectively 840, 6,048 and 3,780 for intersection sizes zero, one and two. These are complete finite loops with explicitly reconstructed incidence matrices and matching generators, not random samples or an assumption that the 3,360 pairs are symmetry-equivalent.

**E. Connectedness and full graph census.** Within each fiber, four-edge exchanges connect its 288 matchings. Between distinct fibers, the seven-exchange graph contains bridges precisely for the 3,360 pairs of hyperovals meeting in three points. The 168-vertex quotient is 40-regular, and exhaustive breadth-first search from every quotient vertex gives diameter two; therefore seven-edge exchanges connect all 48,384 matchings. Six-edge exchanges cannot cross fibers, so seven is sharp. The computed number of bridges is \(3,360\cdot216=725,760\). Exhausting within-fiber matching pairs of exchange size at most seven gives 5,616 internal edges per fiber, or \(168\cdot5,616=943,488\) in total. The graph has consequently 1,669,248 edges. The independent checkers find exactly 30 cross-fiber neighbors for each configuration, and within-fiber degree 37 on half the matchings and 41 on the other half, leading to the stated global degree distribution.

## Verification
The package embeds two full checkers, `check_affine.cpp` (C++17 standard library only) and `check_homogeneous.py` (Python 3 with NumPy). They use different field-coordinate constructions and independently generate the complete set of hyperovals, residual perfect matchings, and all 3,360 relevant cross-fiber adjacency comparisons. To replay:

```bash
g++ -std=c++17 -O3 check_affine.cpp -o check_affine
./check_affine
OPENBLAS_NUM_THREADS=1 python3 check_homogeneous.py
```

Both checkers were executed successfully after the final scientific content was frozen. The source code contains explicit assertions for the 168 hyperovals, all 288 matchings per fiber, the absence of six-edge bridges, the uniform 216 seven-edge bridges, within-fiber connectivity, quotient connectivity, all edge counts and degree classes. All counts refer to the fully enumerated finite graph; neither checker extrapolates to larger projective planes.

## Relationship to prior work
Demange, Ekim and Tanasescu study minimum maximal matchings in regular and bipartite graph classes [1]. Dyer, Jerrum and Müller study perfect-matching switch chains based on different, generally four-cycle, transitions [2]. Spiro, Adriaensen and Mattheus establish the incidence-free method and a sharp projective-plane edge-domination number for powers of four; their Corollary 6.3 already yields the value 15 for this graph and credits maximal arcs [3]. We explicitly **exclude** that scalar optimum from the novelty claim.

A published classification of the six-point hyperoval intersection graph determines 168 vertices, 3,360 pairs meeting in three points, and the point-set three-replacement connectivity threshold [4]. A separate published full enumeration shows that the Tutte–Coxeter graph has 288 perfect matchings, that its four-edge alternating-octagon flip graph is connected, and that this graph has 1,440 flip edges [5]. We also credit the perfect-matching count in a 2025 matching-polynomial table [6]. Even the combination of [3]–[6] does not by itself determine which **entire minimum maximal matchings** can be exchanged across different hyperoval fibers using seven changed incidence edges, nor the constant 216 bridge multiplicity, 30 cross degree, or the global 67/71 degree census. These are the additions verified here.

## Limitations
This is a finite, computer-assisted result only for the Desarguesian plane of order four and the defined batch-reconfiguration model. The all-order inequality is used only as a finite-size bound at order four. Although two independently structured exhaustive checkers agree, independent scholarly audit is not yet performed. Published research indexing is incomplete, so a historical equivalent under different nomenclature remains a priority risk. The 2013 paper [1] was read at abstract/metadata level, not in full; its possible detailed overlap is a residual source-access risk, though its stated focus and later article [3] do not present the full seven-edge bridge census. No result about sampling/mixing or larger planes is asserted.
