# Sharp sixfold fibers of graded singleton types over two-part semigeneric tournaments

## Finding
Let \(\mathcal M\) be the countable homogeneous semigeneric multipartite digraph, and let \(T\) be a finite, pointwise-named induced substructure having exactly two nonempty nonadjacency classes \(A,B\), with \(|A|=a\), \(|B|=b\) and \(n=a+b\). Every pair in different classes supports exactly one oriented edge. Write \(P_T(z)=\sum_{j=0}^n p_j z^j\), where \(p_j\) is the number of complete nonalgebraic singleton types over the named vertices in which the new vertex points toward exactly \(j\) old vertices. Put \(f_m(t)=z^t+z^{m-t}\).

**Exact score polynomial and complete inverse theorem.** For binary vectors \(u\in\{0,1\}^a\), \(v\in\{0,1\}^b\) and \(c\in\{0,1\}\), the cross-orientation matrix is of the form \(M_{ij}=c\oplus u_i\oplus v_j\). If \(r=\sum_i u_i\) and \(s=\sum_j v_j\), then
\[
P_T(z)=(1+z)^n+f_a(r)+f_b(s).
\]
In particular \(n\) is the degree of \(P_T\). The residual polynomial \(Q_T(z)=P_T(z)-(1+z)^n\) has exactly four monomials counted with multiplicity. Write their nonnegative exponents, with repetitions, as \(e_1,e_2,e_3,e_4\). The possible unordered pairs of nonadjacency-class sizes are **exactly**
\[
\left\{\{e_i+e_j,e_k+e_l\}:\{\{i,j\},\{k,l\}\}\text{ is a perfect matching of }\{1,2,3,4\},\ e_i+e_j>0,\ e_k+e_l>0\right\}.
\]
For each matching, its specified pair of complementary exponents supports at most two isomorphism types. Thus every graded singleton-type polynomial has **at most three different class-size profiles and at most six nonisomorphic two-part parameter digraphs**. Both bounds are attained simultaneously, and the smallest order at which they are attained is \(n=6\).

At six points, \(Q(z)=1+z+z^2+z^3\) and
\[
P_T(z)=2+7z+16z^2+21z^3+15z^4+6z^5+z^6.
\]
The three matchings give class sizes \((1,5)\), \((2,4)\) and \((3,3)\); each yields exactly two nonisomorphic digraphs. The first collision of nonisomorphic digraphs occurs at \(n=3\), with \(P_T(z)=3+4z+4z^2+z^3\). The first collision of different class-size profiles occurs at \(n=4\), with \(P_T(z)=2+6z+7z^2+4z^3+z^4\), allowing profiles \((1,3)\) and \((2,2)\).

## Assumptions and scope
The semigeneric condition says every \(2\times2\) rectangle of edges between any two distinct nonadjacency classes has even binary orientation sum. The limit contains all finite such digraphs, and its homogeneity gives a bijection between complete nonalgebraic singleton types over a pointwise-named finite base and admissible one-point extensions over that base. A newly added vertex either belongs to a new class or one of the two old classes. There is no edge within a class; cross-class edges are oriented but never bidirected. The binary convention \(M_{ij}=1\) means \(A_i\to B_j\). The number \(j\) in \(p_j\) counts edges directed **from the new vertex to old vertices**, not the opposite score. All isomorphism classifications below allow exchange of two classes when their sizes agree. No added unary naming of the classes is assumed; only vertices of the base are named for type counting. Distinct finite bases are compared up to ordinary isomorphism.

## Proof
**1. Rank-one representation (known structural fact).** Even parity on every rectangle gives \(M_{ij}=c\oplus u_i\oplus v_j\) for some binary row and column labels and a bit \(c\). Fix a first row and first column; the rectangle identities recover every entry from these. Conversely every such matrix has even rectangle parity. Two simultaneous label complements preserve the same matrix after the appropriate gauge change, and individual row/column label flips can be compensated by toggling \(c\). The existence of this representation is classical for the semigeneric class, not a new assertion here.

**2. One-point type count.** If the new vertex occupies an entirely new class, each of its \(n\) cross-orientations is unrestricted, contributing \((1+z)^n\). If the new vertex joins \(A\), then its orientations toward the \(b\) named points of \(B\) must equal any existing row or its binary complement: indeed, for any two old vertices of \(A\), the rectangle parity fixes all pairwise differences. The corresponding score weights are \(z^s\) and \(z^{b-s}\). If it joins \(B\), the analogous two possibilities contribute \(z^r\) and \(z^{a-r}\). These three kinds of extension are disjoint. All are realized in the homogeneous limit. The formula follows. Since \(a,b\ge1\), the correction has degree at most \(n-1\); the coefficient of \(z^n\) in the first summand is one, so \(\deg P_T=n\).

**3. Inversion by three matchings.** Subtract the now-known \((1+z)^n\). Each of its four residual exponents belongs to exactly one of the two complementary pairs \((r,a-r)\) and \((s,b-s)\). Those pairs form one of the three perfect matchings of four exponent occurrences. Their sums are the class sizes. Conversely, take any such matching with both sums positive, choose binary labels having weights equal to one exponent in each pair, form \(M_{ij}=c\oplus u_i\oplus v_j\), and use step 2. This constructs an admissible base with exactly the proposed polynomial. Repeated exponents are treated as separate occurrences, so no edge cases are lost.

**4. At most two isomorphism types per matching.** Once the two complementary pairs and their sums are fixed, row and column permutations can replace both binary label vectors by canonical \(0/1\) vectors with their specified weights. Complementing a whole label vector merely toggles the single residual bit \(c\) and exchanges the two exponents in that pair. Thus, after the matching is fixed, the remaining ambiguity is at most that single bit, giving no more than two isomorphism types. This holds even when some exponent pairs coincide or the class sizes are equal. Together with three matchings this proves the sixfold upper bound. For an explicit exact test of whether the two bits are isomorphic, normalize the two label weights to \(r\le a/2\) and \(s\le b/2\); sort the class sizes \(a\le b\), and if \(a=b\) sort \(r\le s\). The two bits yield the same isomorphism type if and only if \(a=2r\) or \(b=2s\) or \((a=b\text{ and }r=s)\). Indeed, in the first two cases a balanced label-vector complement toggles \(c\) while preserving its weight; in the last, exchanging equally sized classes toggles \(c\). Otherwise, a part-preserving isomorphism must preserve the two unequal row/column label weight classes and so preserves \(c\), while a side exchange can interchange the weights only for equal sizes with equal normalized weights. This also proves the stated criterion.

**5. Sharpness and first thresholds.** Three distinct class-size profiles require the three matchings to give different sums. If two of the four exponent occurrences coincide, interchanging equal occurrences pairs matchings with the same two sums, so four distinct nonnegative exponents are necessary. Their sum equals \(a+b=n\), and the smallest possible sum of four distinct nonnegative integers is \(0+1+2+3=6\). Choosing precisely those four exponents yields the three profiles in the finding. In each resulting normalized profile, neither side has balanced label weight and, when \(a=b=3\), the two weights are unequal, so step 4 gives two nonisomorphic bases per profile. At orders two and three there is only the profile \((1,1)\) and respectively \((1,2)\); at order two the two edge orientations are exchanged by swapping the classes. At order three the two opposite residual bits give nonisomorphic bases with score polynomial \(3+4z+4z^2+z^3\). At order four, the residual multiset \(\{0,1,1,2\}\) gives matchings with part profiles \((1,3)\) and \((2,2)\); smaller orders cannot accommodate two different positive integer two-part partitions. These prove each first occurrence.

## Verification
The standalone `VERIFY.py` enumerates every semigeneric binary cross-orientation matrix with two nonempty parts, for every total parameter order from two through seven. It directly tests rectangle parity, independently enumerates new-class, old-first-class, and old-second-class one-point extensions by testing rectangle constraints (rather than using the polynomial formula), checks the formula coefficient by coefficient, quotients matrices by all side-preserving vertex permutations and by side swaps at equal part sizes, and compares the resulting profiles with all three exponent matchings. It also checks the first-collision thresholds and the six-element sixfold fiber. The exact-integer run printed `VERIFY_OK matrices_checked= 342 complete_orders=2..7`; the intermediate orbit counts are reproduced by running `python3 VERIFY.py`. These finite checks corroborate the all-order proof but are not its substitute.

## Relationship to prior work
The definition and homogeneous structure come from the classification of homogeneous directed graphs and the semigeneric construction, as discussed by Jasiński–Laflamme–Nguyen Van Thé–Woodrow (2013, Section 10). The binary rank-one representation is explicitly a **known premise**, and an earlier public mathematical result, *Cycle-parity symmetry character for two-part semigeneric digraphs*, determines the permutation fixed-point character and counts unlabeled finite orientations of two nonadjacency classes. The public *Exact Stirling-layer orbit profile of the semigeneric complete multipartite digraph* treats ungraded tuple orbits. Neither statement determines the graded singleton score polynomial's inverse fibers, the matching reconstruction algorithm, or the sharp sixfold first occurrence. Hubička–Jahel–Konečný–Sabok (2025) prove the extension property for partial automorphisms for the semigeneric age, not this score-spectrum inverse theorem. The 2025 work is supporting background, not an archive-dating source.

## Limitations
The classification is limited to base digraphs with exactly **two** nonempty nonadjacency classes and the specified out-neighbor score statistic. It says nothing about bases with three or more parts, scores of longer tuples, or reconstruction from the ungraded total type count. The source comparison checks relevant published structural and adjacent enumerative results but cannot exclude every unindexed historical derivation of the inverse matching observation. The proof is an ordinary mathematical argument; independent external audit has not been performed.
