# Star rigidity and exact geodesic-convexity invariants of finite projective-plane incidence graphs

## Finding
Let \(\Pi\) be any finite projective plane of order \(q\ge2\), and let \(G\) be its bipartite point–line incidence graph. Put \(v=q^2+q+1\). Under ordinary graph geodesic convexity (all shortest paths between any selected pair), the **complete family of proper convex sets** consists of the empty set and all subsets of incidence stars containing their center. In particular, every nonempty proper convex set is either a point-vertex together with an arbitrary selection of incident line-vertices or a line-vertex together with an arbitrary selection of incident point-vertices. The whole graph is also convex. This holds even for non-Desarguesian planes.

The exact bivariate generating polynomial, with \(x\) marking point-vertices and \(y\) line-vertices, is
\[
C_{\Pi}(x,y)=1+x^v y^v+v x(1+y)^{q+1}+v y(1+x)^{q+1}-v(q+1)xy.
\]
Consequently the total number of convex vertex subsets is \(2+2v2^{q+1}-v(q+1)\), and there are exactly \(2v\) largest proper convex sets, all of cardinality \(q+2\). For these convexities, the Carathéodory number is \(3\), the Helly number is \(3\), the Radon number is \(4\), and the VC dimension of the family of convex vertex subsets is exactly \(q+1\). Thus the VC dimension grows with order while all three classical convexity certificate parameters remain constant.

## Assumptions and scope
A finite projective plane of order \(q\) has \(v=q^2+q+1\) points and equally many lines; each point belongs to \(q+1\) lines, each line contains \(q+1\) points, any two points lie on exactly one line, and any two lines meet in exactly one point. Existence of a plane is assumed; no plane is asserted to exist at every integer order. Graph vertices are the points and the lines, with adjacency exactly incidence. For vertices \(a,b\), their geodesic interval \(I(a,b)\) is the union of vertices on **all** shortest \(a\)-to-\(b\) paths. The convex hull \(\operatorname{co}(S)\) is the smallest set closed under all such intervals. This is not the weaker condition of containing *some* selected shortest path, nor the unrelated \(P_3\)-convexity.

The Carathéodory number \(c\) is the minimum integer such that \(z\in\operatorname{co}(S)\) implies \(z\in\operatorname{co}(T)\) for some \(T\subseteq S\) of cardinality at most \(c\). The Helly number \(h\) is the smallest integer such that any finite family of convex sets with all subfamilies of size at most \(h\) intersecting has nonempty total intersection. The Radon number \(r\) is the smallest integer such that any \(r\) distinct vertices can be partitioned into two nonempty classes whose convex hulls intersect. The VC dimension is the maximum cardinality of a vertex subset \(X\) on which intersections with convex subsets realize every subset of \(X\).

## Proof
**Interval forcing.** If a point \(p\) and line \(L\) are not incident, every line through \(p\) and every point of \(L\) belongs to \(I(p,L)\): for each such line \(M\), the path \(p,M,M\cap L,L\) is a length-three geodesic. We claim \(\operatorname{co}\{p,L\}=V(G)\). For each point \(z\notin L\) distinct from \(p\), let \(M=pz\), and choose \(a\in L\setminus M\) (possible since \(q\ge2\)). Then the line-vertex \(M\) and point-vertex \(a\) are already in the interval, and the geodesic \(M,z,za,a\) puts \(z\) into the hull. Every point is therefore in the hull. Every line is now also in the hull since it occurs between two of its distinct point-vertices along a length-two geodesic. This proves the claim.

**All proper sets are stars.** Let \(S\) be a proper convex set. The preceding paragraph excludes every nonincident selected point–line pair. If \(S\) contains two distinct points, their unique joining line \(L\) must be selected (it lies on their length-two geodesic). Any further selected point must lie on \(L\), and the only selected line-vertex is \(L\), because every selected point must be incident with each selected line and two distinct points have only one common line. Thus \(S\) is contained in the star centered at \(L\). Dually, two selected lines force a point-centered star. A set with at most one vertex of each type is empty, a singleton, or an incident flag, and is a star subset. Conversely any vertex together with a subset of its neighbors is convex: two selected leaves have the unique length-two geodesic passing through the center, and a center–leaf pair is adjacent. The whole vertex set is convex. This establishes completeness.

**Enumeration and maximality.** Every nonempty star subset has a specified center point or center line. There are \(v x(1+y)^{q+1}\) point-centered labeled choices and \(v y(1+x)^{q+1}\) line-centered labeled choices. A two-vertex incident flag is counted twice, once for each center, and there are exactly \(v(q+1)\) such flags. There are no other duplicates (for three or more selected vertices, the center is unique). Adding the empty set and the whole graph and subtracting the duplicates gives the polynomial. The only inclusion-maximal proper convex sets are the \(2v\) full stars, all of size \(q+2\).

**Small Carathéodory certificates.** Every non-star subset has a nonincident point–line pair or a triple consisting of three noncollinear point-vertices or three nonconcurrent line-vertices. This follows by first excluding nonincident mixed pairs, then observing that a same-shore set not contained in any line/pencil necessarily has a noncollinear/nonconcurrent triple. Each such triple has full hull: its three pairwise joins (or meets) are already selected line-vertices (or point-vertices), and one of these is nonincident with the opposite vertex of the triple, reducing to interval forcing. So each hull element is generated by at most three original vertices. Three noncollinear point-vertices witness sharpness: no two of them generate an outside point-vertex, while all three generate the whole graph. Hence \(c=3\).

**Helly number.** We use the standard finite-convexity characterization recalled in Changat–Mulder–Sierksma [1]: \(h\le3\) if and only if for every four-element set \(A\), the four hulls \(\operatorname{co}(A\setminus\{a\})\), \(a\in A\), have a common vertex. To check this directly, if \(A\) contains a nonincident point–line pair, two of these four hulls are the whole graph; the other two both contain every vertex of \(A\) outside that pair and hence intersect. Otherwise, when both types occur all four vertices lie in one star and its center belongs to every triple hull. If only points occur, and all four are collinear, their common line belongs to every triple hull. Otherwise at most one of the four triples is collinear, so at most one triple hull is proper; the others are the whole graph. The dual argument treats four line-vertices. Thus \(h\le3\). Sharpness follows from the three convex hulls of the pairs of three noncollinear points: they pairwise intersect in a point but their total intersection is empty. Therefore \(h=3\).

**Radon number.** For any four-element set containing a nonincident point–line pair, put that pair in one class and the other two vertices in the second: the first hull is the whole graph. Otherwise a four-element mixed set lies in one star; partition its two leaves against its center together with the remaining leaf, and the two hulls meet at the center. If all four are points, either a noncollinear triple has whole hull (partition that triple against the fourth), or all four are collinear (partition into two pairs, whose hulls both contain their common line). Dually for line-only sets. Hence \(r\le4\). Three noncollinear point-vertices admit no Radon partition, because the hull of any selected pair is its two points and their joining line and excludes the third point. Thus \(r=4\).

**Shattering.** The \(q+1\) line-vertices through any fixed point \(p\) are shattered: for any chosen trace, take the convex star consisting of \(p\) and exactly the chosen lines. Conversely, let \(X\) be shattered and \(|X|\ge4\). Every three-point subset of \(X\) must have proper hull; otherwise its trace could not be realized while omitting any fourth member. Choose two vertices of the same bipartition class in \(X\). Their unique joining vertex \(c\) of the opposite class belongs to the hull. For a third vertex to form a triple with proper hull, it must lie in the full star centered at \(c\). Therefore \(X\) lies in this star, which has \(q+2\) vertices. If \(|X|=q+2\), it is the whole star, but its leaves without their center cannot be realized as a convex trace: any convex set containing two of the leaves contains the center. Hence \(|X|\le q+1\), and equality is attained as above. Thus \(\operatorname{VCdim}=q+1\).

## Verification
The standalone Python 3 script `verify_geodesic_convexity.py` constructs incidence matrices from homogeneous coordinates over prime fields; computes graph shortest-path intervals by breadth-first search; computes geodesic hulls by iterative interval closure; and builds expected full-star families independently of the hull routine. It verifies the full convex-set classification at order \(2\) by enumerating every one of \(2^{14}=16{,}384\) vertex subsets, including every bivariate coefficient. At order \(3\) it checks all \(14{,}950\) four-vertex subsets against both Radon and Helly conditions, all \(65{,}780\) five-vertex subsets against shattering, and all \(2{,}600\) three-vertex hulls. At order \(5\) it checks all \(37{,}820\) three-vertex hulls and the complete proposed convex family. It prints `VERIFICATION PASS` if every check succeeds. These finite calculations corroborate, but do not replace, the incidence-axiom proof for all existing planes.

## Relationship to prior work
Changat, Mulder and Sierksma [1] study path-based graph convexities and establish general convexity tools, including the finite Helly-hull criterion; their inspected manuscript does not give the all-order projective-plane star classification or this exact bivariate polynomial. Classical geodetic sets [2] concern the union of intervals of pairs from a set, not closure under iterated geodesic intervals. A recent result [3] classifies *geodetic covering bases* in all finite projective planes, with parameter \(2q\), but it does not classify all geodesically convex sets or their Helly, Radon and VC invariants. A separate Heawood-graph result [4] counts \(P_3\)-convex sets, which use closure under every induced length-two path rather than all shortest paths of arbitrary length. For example, its Heawood count is \(366\), whereas the present exact **geodesic** convex count at order \(2\) is \(93\). These are different closure systems. Comparison of actual claims, not only titles, therefore shows no implication that subsumes this theorem. An inaccessible general convexity monograph [5] and any unindexed earlier work remain priority risks.

## Limitations
The theorem requires a full finite projective plane, not merely a regular bipartite girth-six graph or a partial linear space. The plane's existence is assumed. The finite verification covers selected prime-field orders, whereas the proof covers all existing planes, including non-Desarguesian ones. The numerical invariants refer to ordinary all-geodesics convexity; replacing it with \(P_3\)-convexity, monophonic convexity, or one-choice geodesics changes the answer. No assertion of absolute novelty or independent scientific validation is made.
