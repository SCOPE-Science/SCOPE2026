# Scientific review

## Correctness: PASS

The monomial model agrees with the anticanonical divisor \(3H-E\) on the blowup of one torus-fixed plane point. For prime-power \(q\ge5\) the nine evaluation monomials have exponents strictly below \(q-1\), hence dimension nine. The standard-monomial bound reduces all ranks to 511 finite leading-term subsets, with exact affine-in-\(q-1\) footprints. The upper bounds in ranks seven through nine are sharpened by a constructive three-point interpolation argument. Explicit product subspaces attain every claimed maximum, proving the entire infinite-field statement. An executable, standard-library finite checker independently recovers the footprint certificate and exhaustively checks all 65,536 subsets of the 16-point torus over \(\mathbb F_5\). The finite tests corroborate, but do not replace, the general proof.

## Originality: PASS (provisional priority)

The claim concerns the complete hierarchy of a nine-term *dense-torus* anticanonical evaluation code. The 2005 Little–Schenck toric-code paper concerns first minimum distances. Boguslavskii's 1998 del Pezzo hierarchy includes the degree-eight surface but evaluates at its entire projective rational point set and therefore has a different length and generator-column set; its weight table cannot be directly reused as the torus hierarchy. The projective torus second-weight paper, affine Cartesian code hierarchies, and squarefree hypersimplex codes have different polynomial spaces. Searches of published mathematical records and relevant previous claims identified no same-polygon complete hierarchy, but absence in indexed searches does not prove historical priority. The ordinary minimum distance is acknowledged prior work. Residual priority risks include an unindexed specialized code calculation and a general restriction/puncturing theorem that may imply the result.

## Mathematical value: PASS

The anticanonical code of \(\mathbb F_1\) is a canonical nine-dimensional toric evaluation code, and its full hierarchy records the sharp finite-field linear-section geometry for every codimension. In particular, the optimal two- and three-section common-zero counts \(2t+1\) and \(2t\) distinguish adding an isolated point to two ruling fibers, while the higher-codimension transition \(t+2,t+1,t\) reflects residual quadratic interpolation. These are precise, naturally motivated structural data not furnished by the known first minimum distance or by whole-surface projective code weights.
