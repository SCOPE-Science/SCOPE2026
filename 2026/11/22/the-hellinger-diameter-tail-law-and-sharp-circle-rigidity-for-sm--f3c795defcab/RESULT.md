# The Hellinger diameter-tail law and sharp circle rigidity for smooth constant-width ovals

## Finding
Let \(K\subset\mathbb R^2\) be a compact strictly convex body of constant Euclidean width \(w>0\). Assume its support function is \(C^4\) and its curvature radius is positive everywhere. Choose two independent points \(X,Y\) uniformly with respect to boundary arclength and put \(D=\|X-Y\|_2\). Let \(h(\theta)\) denote the support function at outward normal \(n_\theta=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\), and set
\[
 \rho(\theta)=h(\theta)+h''(\theta)>0,\qquad
 f(\theta)=\frac{\rho(\theta)}{\pi w},\qquad
 f_\pi(\theta)=f(\theta+\pi).
\]
Then \(f\) and \(f_\pi\) are probability densities on the normal-angle circle. Define their Hellinger affinity
\[
 \mathcal A(K)=\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{f(\theta)f_\pi(\theta)}\,d\theta
 =\frac1{\pi w}\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{\rho(\theta)\rho(\theta+\pi)}\,d\theta.
\]
**Theorem.** For each fixed such body, as \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\),
\[
 \boxed{\Pr(D>w-\varepsilon)=\frac{2\sqrt2}{\pi}\mathcal A(K)
  \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{w}}+O_K(\varepsilon/w).}
\]
The coefficient satisfies \(0<\mathcal A(K)\le1\), with equality at \(1\) **if and only if \(K\) is a disk**. There is no strictly positive universal lower bound: for fixed \(w\), every value in \((0,1]\) is realized by a smooth, strictly positively curved constant-width body. Thus the full sharp normalized coefficient range is \((0,2\sqrt2/\pi]\).

More precisely, if
\[
 H^2(f,f_\pi):=\frac12\int_0^{2\pi}\bigl(\sqrt{f(\theta)}-\sqrt{f_\pi(\theta)}\bigr)^2\,d\theta,
\]
then the exact coefficient deficit from the equal-width circle is
\[
 \boxed{1-\mathcal A(K)=H^2(f,f_\pi).}
\]
In particular, with \(V=\tfrac12\int_0^{2\pi}|f-f_\pi|\,d\theta\),
\[
 \frac12 V^2\le 1-\mathcal A(K)\le V.
\]
The coefficient therefore detects antipodal curvature imbalance even though width and perimeter are identical for all bodies in the class.

An explicit threefold-symmetric family is given by \(h(\theta)=w/2+a\cos(3\theta)\) with \(|a|<w/16\). Writing \(\lambda=16a/w\), it has
\[
 \mathcal A(K)=\frac2\pi E(\lambda^2),\qquad
 E(m)=\int_0^{\pi/2}\sqrt{1-m\sin^2 t}\,dt.
\]
This is strictly decreasing in \(|a|\) away from the disk, ranging down to the nonattained one-mode limit \(2/\pi\); the full class permits affinities arbitrarily closer to zero.

## Assumptions and scope
The curve is planar, compact, \(C^4\) in normal-angle support coordinates, strictly convex and with a uniform strictly positive lower curvature-radius bound. Both points are independently uniform in Euclidean boundary arclength. The distance is the ambient Euclidean straight-line distance, not an intrinsic boundary distance and not the chord length generated by a Euclidean-invariant random line. Constant width means \(h(\theta)+h(\theta+\pi)=w\) for every angle. Limits fix the body and let \(\varepsilon>0\) tend to zero; the big-O constant is not uniform over bodies approaching vanishing curvature radius. Boundary events of equality have probability zero.

## Proof
**1. Antipodal geometry.** Put \(t_\theta=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\) and let \(x(\theta)=h(\theta)n_\theta+h'(\theta)t_\theta\). Classical support-function differentiation gives \(x'(\theta)=\rho(\theta)t_\theta\). Differentiating the constant-width equation twice shows
\[
\rho(\theta)+\rho(\theta+\pi)=w.
\]
The support-point identity is \(x(\theta)-x(\theta+\pi)=w n_\theta\). Thus every opposite-normal pair is a diameter, and the diameter of \(K\) equals \(w\). Conversely, if two boundary points have Euclidean distance \(w\), they must be supporting contacts for the direction of their chord. Strict convexity and smoothness force the two normals to be antipodal. Hence the complete maximizing locus in the ordered angle torus is precisely \(\theta_2=\theta_1+\pi\pmod{2\pi}\).

**2. Uniform transverse quadratic deficit.** Fix \(\theta\) and write \(\theta_2=\theta+\pi+u\). Differentiating \(x\) twice and expressing vectors in the frame \((n_\theta,t_\theta)\), we obtain, uniformly in \(\theta\),
\[
 x(\theta)-x(\theta+\pi+u)
 =w n_\theta+\rho_\pi t_\theta u
 -\tfrac12(\rho_\pi n_\theta-\rho'_\pi t_\theta)u^2+O_K(u^3),
\]
where \(\rho_\pi=\rho(\theta+\pi)\). Squaring and using \(w-\rho_\pi=\rho(\theta)\) yields
\[
 \|x(\theta)-x(\theta+\pi+u)\|_2^2
 =w^2-\rho(\theta)\rho_\pi u^2+O_K(|u|^3),
\]
so
\[
 w-D(\theta,u)=\frac{\rho(\theta)\rho_\pi}{2w}u^2+O_K(|u|^3).
\]
The positive product has a positive minimum. Compactness and Step 1 give a positive distance gap away from any fixed tubular neighborhood of the maximizing locus. In a uniform sufficiently small neighborhood, the negative second derivative of distance in \(u\) is bounded away from zero; the allowed section \(D>w-\varepsilon\) is consequently one interval with endpoints
\[
 u_\pm(\theta,\varepsilon)
 =\pm\sqrt{\frac{2w}{\rho(\theta)\rho_\pi}}\sqrt\varepsilon+O_K(\varepsilon),
\]
uniformly in \(\theta\).

**3. Arclength probability, including the Jacobian.** The independent normal angles have joint density \(\rho(\theta_1)\rho(\theta_2)/(\pi w)^2\), because \(ds=\rho\,d\theta\) and Barbier's perimeter identity follows here directly by integrating \(\rho+\rho_\pi=w\): \(L=\pi w\). Integrating over the tubular region of Step 2 gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \Pr(D>w-\varepsilon)
 &=\frac1{(\pi w)^2}\int_0^{2\pi}\rho(\theta)
 \int_{u_-}^{u_+}\rho(\theta+\pi+u)\,du\,d\theta\\
 &=\frac{2\sqrt{2w\varepsilon}}{(\pi w)^2}
  \int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{\rho(\theta)\rho(\theta+\pi)}\,d\theta+O_K(\varepsilon/w).
\end{aligned}
\]
After substituting the definition of \(\mathcal A(K)\), this is the claimed coefficient. This uniform local expansion is a proof for all bodies satisfying the assumptions, not an inference from numerical sampling.

**4. Hellinger rigidity and quantitative stability.** Both densities integrate to one. Expansion of their squared-root difference gives \(H^2(f,f_\pi)=1-\int\sqrt{ff_\pi}=1-\mathcal A\). Cauchy–Schwarz shows \(\mathcal A\le1\), with equality precisely when \(f=f_\pi\) almost everywhere. By continuity and \(\rho+\rho_\pi=w\), this is equivalent to \(\rho\equiv w/2\). Solving \(h+h''=w/2\) yields \(h=w/2+A\cos\theta+B\sin\theta\), the support function of a translated disk. Positivity is immediate from \(\rho>0\). For total variation, the pointwise inequality \((\sqrt f-\sqrt{f_\pi})^2\le|f-f_\pi|\) gives \(H^2\le V\). The Cauchy–Schwarz estimate \(V\le\sqrt{(1-\mathcal A)(1+\mathcal A)}\) yields \(H^2=1-\mathcal A\ge V^2/2\).

**5. Sharp infimum and complete coefficient range.** Fix \(w>0\), and for \(0\le\lambda<1\) and \(M>0\) prescribe
\[
 \rho_{M,\lambda}(\theta)=\frac w2\{1+\lambda\tanh(M\cos3\theta)\}.
\]
It is smooth and positive, satisfies \(\rho_{M,\lambda}(\theta)+\rho_{M,\lambda}(\theta+\pi)=w\), and has period \(2\pi/3\). In particular its first Fourier harmonics vanish, so the periodic support equation \(h+h''=\rho_{M,\lambda}\) has a smooth solution, unique modulo translations. Its odd-harmonic part is antiperiodic, whence \(h(\theta)+h(\theta+\pi)=w\), and positive radius constructs a strictly convex constant-width oval. The corresponding affinity is
\[
 \mathcal A(M,\lambda)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}
 \sqrt{1-\lambda^2\tanh^2(M\cos3\theta)}\,d\theta.
\]
It is continuous and decreases strictly from \(1\) as \(\lambda\) increases from zero. Choosing \(\lambda\uparrow1\) followed by \(M\to\infty\), dominated convergence gives \(\mathcal A\to0\), because \(\cos3\theta\ne0\) almost everywhere. For each prescribed \(c\in(0,1)\), first choose \(M\) so that the \(\lambda\uparrow1\) limit is below \(c\), then apply the intermediate value theorem for \(0<\lambda<1\). Thus the exact range is \((0,1]\), with its lower endpoint not attained in the positive-curvature class.

**6. Explicit harmonic specialization.** For \(h=w/2+a\cos3\theta\), differentiation gives \(\rho=w/2-8a\cos3\theta\); writing \(\lambda=16a/w\), its geometric mean with the antipodal radius is \((w/2)\sqrt{1-\lambda^2\cos^2 3\theta}\). The sixfold repetition of the elementary quarter-period integral yields \(\mathcal A=2E(\lambda^2)/\pi\). For \(0<|\lambda|<1\), differentiation under the integral shows \(E(\lambda^2)\) strictly decreases with \(|\lambda|\); the limit at \(|\lambda|=1\) is \(E(1)=1\).

## Verification
The standalone `verify_constant_width_tail.py` derives the support points directly from the threefold support family, checks the transverse distance Taylor coefficient at 68 normal-angle configurations, and computes the *exact finite-threshold probability* by solving the two angular boundary equations independently, integrating the second endpoint's curvature density analytically, and then integrating over the first angle. The resulting probabilities agree with the theoretical leading coefficient for four amplitudes and two small thresholds; the program also checks the circle normalization and coefficient ordering. Run `python verify_constant_width_tail.py` with Python 3, NumPy and SciPy. A successful run prints `CONSTANT_WIDTH_HELLINGER_TAIL_CHECKS_OK 79`. These finite checks are corroboration only; the proof above supplies the uniform expansion, rigidity and full-range construction.

## Relationship to prior work
Mayer and Molchanov (2006) analyze maximal interpoint distances for samples in a unit ball and compute a boundary-circle formula even for nonuniform angular densities (their Section 5.4). A circle with altered endpoint density is not an arclength-uniform **noncircular** constant-width oval: its local distance deficit is independent of position, whereas the present deficit contains the nonconstant product of antipodal curvature radii. Their general probabilistic mechanism assumes a two-point tail and does not supply the present coefficient or its sharp rigidity/range. Bárány and Schneider (2014) analyze curvature radii and typical bodies of constant width, not this boundary-point distance statistic. Gečiauskas's oval/ovaloid paper (scanned issue marked 1968) analyzes random-ray chord models and interior-point distances, not two independently arclength-uniform endpoints. The source's online date metadata conflicts with its printed received date, so no exact earliest publication day is inferred from it.

Two closely related publicly indexed mathematical research findings were compared at their complete theorem and proof statements: *A logarithmic near-circle crossover for nearly diametral chords of ellipse boundaries* analyzes nonconstant-width ellipses and the changing dimension of their diameter-maximizing locus; *Curvature–width Pareto law for bounded random tangent polygons* analyzes large areas of random circumscribing tangent polygons and includes a harmonic constant-width illustration. Neither gives an all-oval near-diameter boundary-endpoint law, the Hellinger identity, or the sharp full range. Their different sampling events and formulas prevent implication by direct substitution. These are statement-level distinctions, not a proof that no historical equivalent exists.

## Limitations
The theorem makes no assertion about nonsmooth constant-width bodies, arcs with vanishing curvature radius, random-line chords, or area-uniform interior points. The \(O_K(\varepsilon/w)\) remainder is only for each fixed curve; the coefficient's infimum is approached by a nonuniform family and does not imply a simultaneous two-parameter tail limit. A single coefficient detects circularity but does not reconstruct a generic noncircular oval. Historical priority beyond the inspected mathematical literature and research records remains unverified, and no independent mathematical audit has been performed.
