# Review of the precision-allocation theorem

## Correctness
PASS. The observation covariance is diagonal due to stationarity and evenness of the Matérn kernel; exact Gaussian conditioning yields the stated reduction. A derivative sign identity proves the noiseless domination needed for a global large-budget result. Uniform convergence on the compact two-dimensional design rectangle plus strict one-sided derivatives proves the low-budget *exact* endpoint class. For the mixed-only example, exact rational inequalities dominate every pure-channel design, because each pure-channel maximum is established by monotonicity. The standalone verifier reproduces the rational margins and supplementary integrations. No finite grid is used to justify global optimality.

## Originality
PASS under available primary-source and database comparisons, with historical-priority qualification. Wu–Aoi–Pillow (2017), original full text sections 3 and 5.2, develops gradient-enhanced Bayesian quadrature and numerical experiments, not a fixed absolute-precision single-site co-located Matérn theorem. Karvonen–Kanagawa–Särkkä (2018), full text sections 2–4, studies positive and bounded quadrature weights, not modality-allocation phases. The closest earlier two-node Matérn result concerns two noisy function values, not one site with derivative measurement. No equivalent or stronger covering theorem was found by the recorded queries; absence from those searches is not proof of uniqueness.

## Value
PASS. Co-located gradient measurements are commonly proposed as a route to more informative numerical integration. The model asks a concrete resource-allocation question: when do gradients warrant their precision cost? It has an unexpected mixed-only phase sandwiched between two pure-channel regimes, certified without relying on a numerical optimizer. The result is qualitative and exact but limited to the stated covariance and noise normalization.

## Limits and residual risk
The positions/allocations of mixed global optima and sharp switching budgets are open within this result. Alternative measurement units, derivative costs and covariance models can change the phases. Further historical literature and independent mathematical audit may reveal an earlier equivalent or an overlooked error.
