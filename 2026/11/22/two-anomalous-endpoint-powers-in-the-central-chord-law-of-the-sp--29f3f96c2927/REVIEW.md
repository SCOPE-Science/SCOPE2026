# Scientific review of the sphericon central-chord endpoint laws

## Correctness

**PASS.** The standard construction yields two half-bicone radial exit times; their sum is exactly \(f(|u_1|)+f(|u_2|)\). The bounds \(1/\sqrt2\le f\le1\) classify all extrema. Four lower minima have a positive quadratic angular term and positive quartic vertical term, giving a \(3/4\) exponent and an explicit beta integral. The six upper maxima have two diamond tangent domains of area \(2\) and four nonpolygonal domains of area \(4/3\), giving \(7/(3\pi)\). The formulas include normalized Haar measure and use area rescaling rather than finite enumeration as proof. A standard-library checker corroborates critical expansions and constants.

## Originality

**PASS, with historical-priority risk.** Stewart (1999) introduces the sphericon, Phillips (1999) treats its surface curvature and geodesic structure, Weisstein records volume/area/inertia, and Kaufman (2007) explains the assembly. These accessible accounts do not state the central-chord distribution or the asymmetric endpoint exponents. Publicly described extreme-chord results for centrally symmetric polytopes or affine-line chords of ellipsoids do not imply this specific quartic lower endpoint. The 1999 magazine article was not inspected in full; its publisher record and construction are verified, but the unread body remains a residual risk. Searches for sphericon chord distributions and equivalent bicone terminology found no decisive coverage, which alone is not proof of novelty.

## Mathematical value

**PASS.** The minimum central chord is a natural inradius statistic of the developable roller, and the maximum is its diameter. The unexpected \(3/4\) exponent records a genuine quartic degeneracy caused by joining the differently oriented half-bicones, while the maximum has a sharp mixture of two distinct tangent-cone geometries. This supplies the complete support, an exact CDF integral, both leading constants, and conditional extreme-direction laws useful for directional stereology of a nonsmooth classical solid.

## Limits and closest literature

The claim is for Haar-distributed directions conditioned to pass through the center; it is not the Euclidean-invariant random translated-line model. Only one fixed standard sphericon geometry is covered. Ian Stewart, *Scientific American* 281 (1999), and Tony Phillips, AMS (1999), are the primary historical comparisons; full magazine text could not be retrieved.
