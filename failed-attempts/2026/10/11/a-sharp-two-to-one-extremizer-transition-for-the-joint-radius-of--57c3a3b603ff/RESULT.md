# A sharp two-to-one extremizer transition for the joint radius of rank-one projections

## Finding
Let \(H\) be a complex Hilbert space of dimension at least two, let \(u,v\in H\) be unit vectors, and let \(P_u x=\langle x,u\rangle u\), \(P_vx=\langle x,v\rangle v\). Set \(c=|\langle u,v\rangle|\in[0,1]\) and define the balanced Euclidean joint numerical radius
\[
R(P_u,P_v)=\sup_{\|x\|=1}\left(\frac{|\langle P_ux,x\rangle|^2+|\langle P_vx,x\rangle|^2}{2}\right)^{1/2}.
\]
Then
\[
R(P_u,P_v)=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1-c^2}{\sqrt{2(1-2c^2)}}&0\le c<\tfrac12,\\[5pt]
\dfrac{1+c}{2}&\tfrac12\le c\le1.
\end{cases}
\]
The number of distinct maximizing complex rays is exactly two for \(0\le c<1/2\), and exactly one for \(1/2\le c\le1\). For \(0<c<1/2\), after choosing phases so \(\langle u,v\rangle=c\), put \(s=\sqrt{1-c^2}\), \(w=(v-cu)/s\), and \(\phi=\arccos c\). The two maximizing rays are spanned by
\[
x_{\pm}=\cos(\theta_{\pm})u+\sin(\theta_{\pm})w,\qquad
\theta_{\pm}=\frac\phi2\pm\frac12\arccos\!\left(\frac{c}{1-2c^2}\right).
\]
At \(c=0\), the only maximizing rays are those generated by \(u\) and \(v\). For \(1/2\le c<1\), the unique ray is the phase-aligned angular bisector spanned by \(u+v\); at \(c=1\), it is the common range of the two projections.

There is a sharp square-root symmetry-breaking transition at \(c=1/2\). As \(\delta=1/2-c\downarrow0\), the angular separation of the two maximizing rays and their excess radius over the bisector value obey
\[
|\theta_+-\theta_-|=2\sqrt{3\delta}+O(\delta^{3/2}),\qquad
R(P_u,P_v)-\frac{1+c}{2}=3\delta^2+O(\delta^3).
\]

## Assumptions and scope
The two operators are rank-one **orthogonal** projections (not arbitrary rank-one operators); the radius uses exactly equal weights \(1/2,1/2\) and the original Hilbert inner product. The result holds for every complex Hilbert dimension at least two, finite or infinite. Its claims about maximizers refer to complex rays, so multiplication by a unimodular scalar does not produce a new maximizer.

## Proof
Since \(\langle P_ux,x\rangle=|\langle x,u\rangle|^2\) and similarly for \(v\), the squared objective is \((|\langle x,u\rangle|^4+|\langle x,v\rangle|^4)/2\). For \(c<1\), let \(L=\operatorname{span}\{u,v\}\). If \(x=x_L+x_\perp\), putting \(r=\|x_L\|\le1\), both inner products are multiplied by \(r\). The squared objective scales by \(r^4\), hence every maximizing unit vector is in \(L\). Fix phases so \(v=cu+sw\) with \(s=\sqrt{1-c^2}>0\) and \(u,w\) orthonormal. A unit \(x\in L\) can, after a global phase, be written \(x=\cos\theta\,u+e^{i\gamma}\sin\theta\,w\), with \(0\le\theta\le\pi/2\). For \(0<c<1\), at an interior maximizer the second overlap is maximized precisely at \(e^{i\gamma}=1\), so all maximizing rays are represented by the real phase-aligned vectors \(x_\theta\). The same representation suffices at \(c=0\), when the maximizing parameters are endpoints. On \(\phi\le\theta\le\pi/2\), both \(\cos^4\theta\) and \(\cos^4(\theta-\phi)\) are nonincreasing, so it suffices to consider \(0\le\theta\le\phi=\arccos c\).

Set \(t=\theta-\phi/2\), \(y=\cos(2t)\). As \(\theta\) traverses \([0,\phi]\), \(y\) traverses \([c,1]\), attaining each interior value twice. Expanding the fourth powers yields the exact polynomial identity
\[
F_c(y):=\frac{\cos^4\theta+\cos^4(\theta-\phi)}2
=\frac{2-c^2}4+\frac c2y+\frac{2c^2-1}4y^2.
\]
For \(0<c<1/2\), this polynomial is strictly concave and its vertex \(y_*=c/(1-2c^2)\) satisfies \(c<y_*<1\). This gives exactly two maximizing parameters and
\[
F_c(y_*)=\frac{(1-c^2)^2}{2(1-2c^2)}.
\]
For \(1/2\le c<1/\sqrt2\), \(F_c\) is concave but its derivative at \(y=1\) is \((c+2c^2-1)/2\ge0\), so the unique maximum is at \(y=1\). For \(1/\sqrt2\le c<1\), \(F_c\) is nondecreasing on \([c,1]\) since its derivative is \((c+(2c^2-1)y)/2>0\); again the maximum is uniquely at \(y=1\). At \(c=0\), \(F_0(y)=1/2-y^2/4\), with maxima only at \(y=0\) corresponding to \(x=u,v\). At \(c=1\), the projections coincide, and the unique maximizing ray is their range. Since \(F_c(1)=(1+c)^2/4\), the claimed radius and all equality cases follow.

Finally, for \(0<c<1/2\), direct subtraction gives the exact identity
\[
F_c(y_*)-F_c(1)=\frac{(1-c-2c^2)^2}{4(1-2c^2)}.
\]
For \(c=1/2-\delta\), \(1-y_*=6\delta+O(\delta^2)\). Since \(\arccos(1-h)=\sqrt{2h}+O(h^{3/2})\), the separation is \(2\sqrt{3\delta}+O(\delta^{3/2})\). The displayed squared-radius excess is \((9/2)\delta^2+O(\delta^3)\); divide by \(R(P_u,P_v)+(1+c)/2=3/2+O(\delta)\) to obtain the radius excess \(3\delta^2+O(\delta^3)\).

## Relationship to prior work
Dragomir (2006) defines the ordinary Euclidean radius and proves general operator inequalities. Feki (2024) and Altwaijry, Dragomir, Feki and Furuichi (2024/2025) develop bounds for joint radii. Aljawi, Alotaibi, Dragomir and Feki (July 2026) introduce a more general weighted \(A\)-joint radius; the present balanced Hilbert case is a specialization of that already established definition. The distinct claim here is **the exact value together with the complete maximizing-ray bifurcation and its sharp singular scaling**, not the definition of the joint radius or a universal inequality. The full 2026 manuscript was not available for a conclusive whole-document comparison; its accessible abstract discusses general bounds and applications, and possible unstated overlapping examples remain a bibliographic risk.

## Limitations
The theorem does not classify arbitrary rank-one operators, arbitrary projection ranks, unequal weights, or general semi-Hilbertian metrics. No assertion is made about a new general definition or a universal bound. The analytic formulas are proved, but originality relative to inaccessible full texts has not been independently audited.
