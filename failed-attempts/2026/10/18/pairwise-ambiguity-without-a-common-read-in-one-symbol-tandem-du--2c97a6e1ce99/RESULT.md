# Pairwise ambiguity without a common read in one-symbol tandem duplication

## Finding
Fix integers \(q\ge2\) and \(n\ge1\). On the \(q\)-letter words of length \(n\), join two different words whenever their sets of length-\(n+1\) descendants under **exactly one single-symbol tandem duplication** intersect. For each \(s\ge3\), every \(s\)-vertex clique belongs to exactly one of two explicitly defined classes: an **upper** clique with no common descendant, or a **lower** clique with precisely one common descendant. The numbers of the two classes are
\[
K_s^{\mathrm{upper}}(q,n)=q\sum_{r=s}^n(q-1)^{r-1}\binom rs\binom{n-2}{r-1},\qquad
K_s^{\mathrm{lower}}(q,n)=q\sum_{r=s}^n(q-1)^{r-1}\binom rs\binom{n-s}{r-1}.
\]
Here \(\binom ab=0\) unless \(a\ge b\ge0\). In particular, for \(n\ge4\), the count of pairwise-confusable triples **without** a common read is
\[
K_3^{\mathrm{upper}}(q,n)=\frac{q^{n-4}(q-1)^2(n-2)(n-3)}6\left((n-4)(q-1)+3q\right),
\]
whereas \(K_3^{\mathrm{lower}}(q,n)=K_3^{\mathrm{upper}}(q,n-1)\) for \(n\ge5\). The upper count is zero for \(n\le3\); the lower count is zero for \(n\le4\). The confusability graph has clique number \(n-1\) for \(n\ge3\), although no single output has more than \(\lfloor(n+1)/2\rfloor\) distinct parents; this latter bound is attained. For fixed \(q\), the proportion of confusability triangles without a common read converges to \(q/(q+1)\) as \(n\to\infty\).

## Assumptions and scope
A single-symbol tandem duplication inserts a second copy of one symbol immediately beside its original, with no substitution. For a word \(x=x_1\cdots x_n\), define
\[
D(x)=\{x_1\cdots x_i x_i x_{i+1}\cdots x_n:1\le i\le n\}.
\]
Duplicate results are identified because \(D(x)\) is a set. A clique is a *set of distinct words* for which every pair has intersecting descendant sets. A *common read* of a clique belongs to the intersection of descendant sets of **all** its vertices. These are exact-one-error balls, not cones generated by arbitrary numbers of duplications.

For any nonempty word, its run root is the ordered list \(\rho=(a_1,\ldots,a_r)\) of its maximal constant-run symbols, where \(a_i\ne a_{i+1}\). If its run lengths are \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r\), its run excess vector is \(v=(\lambda_1-1,\ldots,\lambda_r-1)\); it has nonnegative integer coordinates and sum \(n-r\). For a fixed root, single duplication increments precisely one coordinate of \(v\).

The two clique classes, defined for any size \(s\ge3\), are exactly the following, where \(I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,r\}\) has \(|I|=s\), and \(e_i\) is a standard basis vector:

* **Upper:** \(\{b+e_i:i\in I\}\) with \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^r\) and \(\sum_i b_i=n-r-1\).
* **Lower:** \(\{c-e_i:i\in I\}\) with \(c\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^r\), \(\sum_i c_i=n-r+1\), and \(c_i\ge1\) for every \(i\in I\).

The coordinate descriptions apply within one run root; they are not claims that words from different roots can conflict.

## Proof
**1. Confusability is a unit transfer.** A duplication preserves the run root. Thus any two words with one common descendant have the same root \(\rho\). Under the run-excess map, the output vectors of a source vector \(v\) are exactly \(\{v+e_i:1\le i\le r\}\). If distinct sources \(v,w\) share an output, \(v+e_i=w+e_j\) for two distinct indices \(i,j\). Consequently \(w-v=e_i-e_j\). Conversely each legal unit transfer gives a unique shared output, because its two nonzero difference coordinates determine the indices uniquely.

**2. Every clique of at least three vertices has one orientation.** Take distinct adjacent \(v=b+e_i\) and \(w=b+e_j\), with \(i\ne j\) and \(b\ge0\). Solving the two unit-transfer adjacency conditions shows that any third vector adjacent to both is either \(b+e_k\) for \(k\notin\{i,j\}\), or \(b+e_i+e_j-e_k\) for such a \(k\) with \(b_k\ge1\). Indeed, both possibilities are obtained by moving one unit from a coordinate of \(v\) to a different coordinate and demanding the same property relative to \(w\); all other moves leave at least four total units of coordinate difference from one of them. Any vector of the first kind differs from any vector of the second kind by \(\ell_1\)-distance at least \(4\), so they cannot be adjacent. Hence a clique with at least three vertices consists either entirely of the first kind (together with \(v,w\)), or entirely of the second kind (together with \(v,w\)). The resulting vector families are exactly the stated upper and lower classes.

For a clique of size at least three, the coordinatewise minimum is precisely \(b\) in the upper case, and the coordinatewise maximum is precisely \(c\) in the lower case. Thus these parameterizations are unique. An upper clique of size at least three cannot have a common read: any common output would have to exceed the base \(b\) in at least three distinct coordinates, although a single output has total excess \(\sum b_i+2\). Every lower clique shares the read with vector \(c\), and the uniqueness of the common read of any pair forces this to be its sole common read.

**3. Count the two classes.** There are \(q(q-1)^{r-1}\) possible run roots of length \(r\). Choose the \(s\) positions in \(I\) in \(\binom rs\) ways. For an upper clique, \(b\) is a weak \(r\)-composition of \(n-r-1\), hence there are \(\binom{n-2}{r-1}\) choices. For a lower clique, subtract one from each of the \(s\) selected coordinates of \(c\); the number of resulting weak compositions is \(\binom{n-s}{r-1}\). Summing over \(r\) proves both formulas without overcounting by the uniqueness in Step 2.

For triples set \(N=n-2\) and \(z=q-1\), and use the identity
\[
\sum_{t=0}^{N}\binom Nt(t+1)t(t-1)z^t
=N(N-1)(N-2)z^3(1+z)^{N-3}+3N(N-1)z^2(1+z)^{N-2},
\]
obtained by differentiating \((1+z)^N\). Substitution gives the stated closed upper formula. In the lower triple sum, \(n\) is replaced by \(n-1\), proving its shift identity. The zero-boundary cases follow directly from the impossible composition requirements.

**4. Two different notions of largest ambiguity.** An upper clique uses \(s\le r\le n-1\), and a lower clique has \(s\le r\) with \(n-r+1\ge s\), so \(s\le(n+1)/2\). In particular \(s\le n-1\) whenever \(n\ge3\). Equality \(s=n-1\) is attained by the upper vectors \(e_1,\ldots,e_{n-1}\) on any run root of length \(n-1\). For \(n=1,2\), there are no graph edges and the clique number is one.

A received word of length \(n+1\) has precisely one distinct parent for every constant run of length at least two, obtained by shortening that run once. Since each such run uses at least two symbols, their number is at most \(\lfloor(n+1)/2\rfloor\); alternating runs of length two, with a possible final singleton, attain it. Finally, the two triple formulas give \(K_3^{\mathrm{lower}}(q,n)/K_3^{\mathrm{upper}}(q,n)\to1/q\) at fixed \(q\), proving the stated limiting proportion.

## Verification
The standalone `verify.py` enumerates source words and their duplicated outputs directly, not by generating the predicted formulas. It enumerates confusable pairs and triples, tests whether every member of a triple admits one shared read, classifies by independently extracted run lengths, and checks the two exact counts. It also checks every higher clique size in smaller independent finite instances. All tests passed: binary lengths \(1\) through \(10\), ternary lengths \(1\) through \(7\), and four-letter lengths \(1\) through \(6\), a combined \(28{,}232\) confusability triangles. The nontrivial upper/lower uniqueness and general counting identities are proved above; finite checks are corroboration, not the argument for all lengths.

## Relationship to prior work
Lenz, Wachter-Zeh, and Yaakobi (2017) introduce tandem-duplication codes and sphere bounds; Kovačević and Tan (2018) give an asymptotically optimal single-duplication coding framework using run-derived coordinates. Yehezkeally and Schwartz (2018) analyze descendant cones and reconstruction codes. Chee, Chrisnata, Kiah, and Nguyen (2019) study confusability of **unbounded descendant cones** and compute optimal codes using the opposite graph convention (edges joining nonconfusable words). Those methods concern another channel and do not classify exact-one-error common reads. These works motivate the exact-error descendant sets but do not state this complete hierarchy of cliques or its distinction between pairwise and simultaneous reads.

A more recent published exact-one tandem-duplication **edge-count** result identifies the unit-transfer adjacency and proves that any confusable pair has one common descendant. That fact is consistent with Step 1; it does **not** classify triples, general cliques, or whether the distinct shared read for each pair is the same read, and does not imply the two counts without the three-way classification. The contribution here is the channel-specific *higher-order* ambiguity structure, including exact counts for every clique size and the maximum pairwise-versus-joint discrepancy.

## Limitations
Only one duplicated symbol and exactly one such duplication are allowed. The theorem is not a result for substitutions, deletion, noisy duplications, fixed duplications of length more than one, or arbitrary numbers of duplications. It does not calculate the maximum code size (independence number) or classify all maximum correcting codes. The counting formulas do not imply the absence of an alternative formulation in unindexed literature; independent expert examination remains outstanding.
