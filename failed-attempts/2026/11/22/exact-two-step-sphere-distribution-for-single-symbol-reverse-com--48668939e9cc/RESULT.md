# Exact two-step sphere distribution for single-symbol reverse-complement duplication

## Finding

Let \(\Sigma\) be a labeled alphabet of even size \(q\ge 2\), equipped with a fixed-point-free involution \(a\mapsto\bar a\). For \(x=x_1\cdots x_n\in\Sigma^n\), \(n\ge1\), one length-one reverse-complement duplication replaces an occurrence \(a\) by \(a\bar a\), immediately following that occurrence. Let \(D_2(x)\) be the **set of distinct words** obtained after exactly two successive such operations, where the second may act on a newly inserted letter.

Write \(x\) as maximal constant-letter runs \(a_1^{r_1}\cdots a_s^{r_s}\), where \(r_i\ge1\) and \(a_i\ne a_{i+1}\). Define

\[
\lambda(x)=\sum_{i=2}^{s}r_i\,\mathbf 1\{a_i=\bar a_{i-1}\}.
\]

Then the exact two-step descendant sphere size is

\[
\boxed{|D_2(x)|=\frac{n(n+3)}2-\lambda(x).}
\]

More strongly, the **complete distribution at every length** is encoded by the rational bivariate generating function

\[
\boxed{\sum_{n\ge1}z^n\sum_{x\in\Sigma^n}t^{\lambda(x)}=\frac{qz(1-zt)}{1-(q-1)z-2zt+qz^2t}.}
\]

Thus the coefficient of \(z^nt^j\) counts source words whose exact-two sphere size is \(n(n+3)/2-j\). The sphere sizes attained at length \(n\ge2\) are precisely all integers from \(n(n+1)/2+1\) through \(n(n+3)/2\). The maximum is attained by \(q(q-1)^{n-1}\) words; the minimum by \(q2^{n-2}\) words. In particular, for DNA (\(q=4\)) and \(n=6\), the counts of sphere sizes \(27,26,25,24,23,22\) are respectively \(972,1188,1008,608,256,64\).

## Assumptions and scope

Words and their positions are indexed, not identified under rotation, reversal, or complement. Complementation has no fixed points; no stochastic weighting is imposed. Exactly two insertions occur (not zero, one, or at most two). The parameter \(\lambda(x)\) counts the length of every constant run whose letter complements the **preceding** run, not merely the number of complementary transitions. The empty word is excluded. No claim about optimal code cardinality or general duplication lengths is made.

## Proof

**Canonical two-marker representation.** Index the original positions by \(1,\ldots,n\). Every two-step history produces one of the following \(\binom n2+2n=n(n+3)/2\) canonical expansion words:

* \(P_{ij}\), \(1\le i<j\le n\): replace both \(x_i\) and \(x_j\) by their two-letter expansions \(a\bar a\), leaving other letters alone.
* \(A_i\): replace \(x_i=a\) by \(a\bar a a\), leaving other letters alone.
* \(B_i\): replace \(x_i=a\) by \(a\bar a\bar a\), leaving other letters alone.

To check completeness, if the second duplication acts at an original site other than the first one, the two one-letter expansions commute and give a \(P\)-word. If it acts at the inserted copy it gives \(A_i\); if it acts again at the original occurrence it gives \(B_i\). Every listed word is thus attainable.

**Two-marker alignment lemma.** Partition the source into maximal consecutive blocks belonging to one fixed complement pair. Since a copied symbol stays in the same complement pair, the sequence of pair labels of the output displays every block boundary and the number of insertions assigned to each block. Two histories with different allocations to these blocks therefore cannot coincide. It suffices to analyze a block over one complementary pair, where every adjacent run change is a complement change. All equalities between canonical words are generated by the following two local moves (transitive closure is understood):

\[
A_i=A_{i+1}\quad\text{if }x_{i+1}=\bar x_i,
\tag{1}
\]

and

\[
P_{ij}=A_{j+1}\quad\text{if }x_i=a,\quad x_{i+1}=x_{i+2}=\cdots=x_{j+1}=\bar a,\qquad i<j<n.
\tag{2}
\]

These identities are verified by expanding the displayed source factors, including their unchanged prefixes and suffixes. For the converse, align two canonical outputs from left to right, retaining the common original occurrences as anchors. At their first differing expansion, one side emits the extra \(\bar a\) after an original \(a\), while the other side emits the next unexpanded original letter. Equality forces that letter to be \(\bar a\). The extra symbol can thereafter be shifted past a consecutive run of \(\bar a\)'s. With at most two extras in either expansion, its only resolutions are (i) two three-letter \(a\bar a a\) expansions on neighboring complementary original letters, giving (1), or (ii) the insertion at the left \(a\) and one further expanded position inside the following constant \(\bar a\)-run, giving (2). In a second-marker alignment, an extra \(\bar a\) cannot pass an original \(a\), or an unrelated letter, without a mismatch; any alternative would require a third inserted symbol. In particular two distinct \(P\)-words never coincide, \(B_i\) coincides with no other canonical word, and every coincidence involving a \(P\)-word is mediated by (2). This exhausts the possibilities. The accompanying checker independently tests both directions of the lemma on all source words in several alphabets and lengths; this finite test supplements rather than replaces the alignment argument.

**Counting the equivalence classes.** Identity (1) contributes one edge for every neighboring complementary pair across distinct constant runs. For a complementary transition from a preceding run \(a^u\) to the next run \(\bar a^v\), identity (2) contributes exactly \(v-1\) distinct edges: the left index is the final \(a\), and the right marked site may be any but the last position of the \(\bar a\)-run. For a fixed left index these \(P_{ij}\) labels are different and each attaches to a single \(A\) label. The graph on \(A\)-labels formed by (1) is a subgraph of the index path, so is acyclic; adding the \(P\)-leaves from (2) remains acyclic. Hence each complementary run transition creates exactly \(1+(v-1)=v\) independent identifications, and no other transition creates any. Subtracting \(\lambda(x)\) from the canonical count proves the sphere formula.

**Generating function.** Every word is uniquely a sequence of maximal constant runs. The first run has \(q\) choices of letter and length generating series \(R(z)=z/(1-z)\). At every subsequent run, there is exactly one complementary letter, contributing \(R(zt)=zt/(1-zt)\), or one of the \(q-2\) other different letters, contributing \((q-2)R(z)\). Therefore

\[
\sum_{n\ge1}z^n\sum_{x\in\Sigma^n}t^{\lambda(x)}
=\frac{qR(z)}{1-R(zt)-(q-2)R(z)}
=\frac{qz(1-zt)}{1-(q-1)z-2zt+qz^2t}.
\]

For extremizers, \(\lambda=0\) exactly when no adjacent pair is complementary, giving \(q(q-1)^{n-1}\) words. Its maximum \(n-1\) occurs precisely when the first run has length one and all subsequent runs stay within the same complement pair; fix \(x_1\) (\(q\) choices), force \(x_2=\bar x_1\), then choose each remaining letter from the same complementary pair (\(2^{n-2}\) choices). All intermediate deficits are possible, for example from a source whose first run has length \(n-j\) and whose remaining \(j\) letters all equal its complement. This finishes the proof.

## Verification

Run `python3 verify.py` using Python 3 and no external dependencies. It directly generates two sequential duplications, compares their distinct outputs with the closed formula, checks that the canonical expansions produce precisely those outputs, independently checks the complete rewrite-equivalence classes, and compares exact distribution coefficients with the rational-function recurrence. The included executable covers all \(q\)-ary sources through \(n=11\) for \(q=2\), \(n=7\) for \(q=4\), and \(n=5\) for \(q=6\), totaling 35,268 complete source cases. These finite calculations are consistency tests; the preceding proof establishes arbitrary length and every even alphabet size.

## Relationship to prior work

Ben-Tolila and Schwartz, *On the Reverse-Complement String-Duplication System* (arXiv:2112.11811, first made public 2021-12-22), establish the duplication model, expressiveness, binary length-two capacity/entropy, and single-error code constructions, rather than the exact-two source-dependent sphere distribution. Yohananov and Schwartz (2025) determine capacities for correcting unlimited reverse-complement or palindromic duplications, not the fixed-source two-error spectrum. Zabokritskiy (2026) studies two-error spheres primarily for **even** duplication lengths and proves an extremal sphere formula; it does not furnish this complete length-one per-source distribution. A separately indexed published-finding corpus finding on all **odd** duplication lengths gives the maximum exact-two sphere; its length-one specialization is a corollary of the present formula, but it does not imply the nonmaximal sphere sizes, their exact word counts or the rational generating function. Results for length-two palindromic or reverse-complement duplications have a different local operation.

## Limitations

The claim covers exact-two length-one duplications only. The alphabet must have a fixed-point-free complement involution, and the proof does not determine confusability intersections between *different* source words or optimal two-error-correcting codes. The result has not undergone an independent mathematical audit. The literature comparison is substantial but does not exclude every obscure equivalent formulation.
