# Independent mathematical audit

## correctness

PASS

A face with at least three vertices can neither contain the center nor repeat an arm; a center-free partial transversal is always a face. This gives the r-fold join of the L depth labels and precisely the stated extra one-cells. The join orbit space is the sorted-weight chamber: sorting is continuous and the compact quotient maps bijectively into a Hausdorff polyhedron. Adding the other weights of a depth to its largest while scaling those others down preserves the simplex sum and never increases the total number of occupied slots. It fixes the section with one slot per depth and retracts onto the simplex skeleton. The one-pivot Morse matching on the skeleton is acyclic because all possible downward covers deleting the pivot with nonempty lower face are reversed; it has one critical vertex and exactly binom(L−1,r) critical top faces. This count is zero for L≤r, first becomes one at L=r+1, and is separated from degree one by r≥3. Edge stabilizers cannot exchange distinct depths or exchange the center with an arm vertex. Consequently the extra edges are intervals attached at their stated vertices, with one central edge a contractible attachment and the others contributing the asserted loops. For L=1 this adds no loop. The barycentric flag computations recorded at four primes are corroborative and were not replayed; the deformation, matching, and attachments prove the integral statement.

## originality

FAIL

The same quotient ranks are explicitly indexed, but that alone does not settle the homotopy question. More decisively, the published symmetric-join proof of Kallel–Karoui makes the core homotopy and existence of the strong deformation retraction a routine finite-polyhedral specialization of its labelled-weight merge. Appending the extra orbit edges to that core uses the already available exact face decomposition and elementary graph attachments. The paper is scoped to connected X, so this is an implication of its proof, not a claim that its connected-space theorem literally states the discrete case.
