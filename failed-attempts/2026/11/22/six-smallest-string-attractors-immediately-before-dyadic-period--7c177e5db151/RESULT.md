# Six smallest string attractors immediately before dyadic period-doubling lengths

## Finding
Let \(\varphi(0)=01\), \(\varphi(1)=00\), and let \(D_\infty=\lim_{k\to\infty}\varphi^k(0)\), with its letters indexed from one. Put \(T_k=D_\infty[1..2^k-1]\). For **every integer \(k\ge4\)**, the set \(\mathcal A(T_k)\) of all minimum-cardinality string attractors is
\[
\mathcal A(T_k)=2^{k-4}\cdot\big\{\{4,8\},\{4,10\},\{5,10\},\{6,11\},\{6,12\},\{8,12\}\big\}.
\]
Here scalar multiplication acts separately on the two one-based positions in each pair. Consequently, every \(T_k\) has exactly six minimum two-position attractors. By the previously established theorem of Banbara et al., the complete length-\(2^k\) period-doubling word has exactly two minimum attractors, so appending the last letter at these boundaries decreases multiplicity from six to two.

## Assumptions and scope
For a finite word \(W\), a **string attractor** is a set \(\Gamma\subseteq\{1,\ldots,|W|\}\) such that each nonempty contiguous factor of \(W\) has some occurrence containing a position of \(\Gamma\). Distinct attractors are distinct sets of one-based positions. Write \(\mathcal A(W)\) for those of minimum size. No periodicity assumptions beyond the specified binary morphism are used. The theorem concerns the just-before-dyadic lengths, not arbitrary prefix lengths. It is not an assertion about shortest *unique substrings* or scattered subsequences.

## Proof
**1. A morphic attractor-lifting lemma.** For every finite binary word \(W\) containing both letters and each attractor \(\Gamma\) of \(W\), the doubled set \(2\Gamma=\{2j:j\in\Gamma\}\) is an attractor of \(\varphi(W)0\). Indeed, take any nonempty factor \(X\) of \(\varphi(W)0\). If an occurrence of \(X\) contains a second position of a substitution block, the indices of all such positions form one nonempty interval \([a,b]\). The factor \(X\) is completely determined by the letters \(W[a..b]\), together with the presence or absence of a leading zero (a first block letter) and a trailing zero (the next first block letter, or the appended zero). Thus every other occurrence of \(W[a..b]\) gives an occurrence of \(X\) with the same relative position of the second block letters. Some such occurrence crosses \(\Gamma\), and the corresponding occurrence of \(X\) crosses \(2\Gamma\). If \(X\) contains no second block position it must be the singleton \(0\). Since \(\Gamma\) must attract the singleton factor \(1\), it contains a position of \(W\) holding \(1\), whose image \(00\) has a zero at its second position. The lemma follows.

The last letter of each \(\varphi^j(0)\) is discarded in \(T_j\), and \(\varphi\) maps either letter to a two-letter block starting in zero. Consequently, for \(j\ge2\),
\[
T_{j+1}=\varphi(T_j)0.
\]
The lemma therefore propagates each candidate attractor of \(T_4\) by doubling.

**2. A finite, globally exhaustive parity certificate.** We prove that for every \(k\ge5\), the two positions of every minimum two-position attractor of \(T_k\) are both **even in one-based indexing**. The symbol \(1\) appears only at even one-based positions, so a two-position attractor must have one selected \(1\) and one selected \(0\). It suffices to rule out a selected \(0\) at an odd one-based position. Form the collection \(U\) of all distinct nonempty factors of \(T_5\) of length at most five: \(|U|=24\). Each attractor of \(T_k\), \(k\ge5\), must cover all these factors, since \(T_5\) is a prefix of \(T_k\). At a position, the subset of \(U\) whose occurrences can cross that position is completely determined by its length-nine centered window, truncated at the beginning as necessary. Every complete length-nine window of \(D_\infty\) is contained in \(\varphi^4(xy)\) for an adjacent symbol pair \(xy\) in \(D_\infty\), because a level-four block has length sixteen. The only adjacent pairs are \(00,01,10\): \(11\) is absent since the second letter of a substituted block is \(1\) only when the source letter is \(0\), and every first letter is \(0\). Therefore checking the length-nine windows of these three explicitly defined thirty-two-letter words, plus the four initial truncated windows, exhausts **all** possible coverage masks. A short standalone program `verification/local_parity_certificate.py` implements precisely this finite enumeration, finding eight possible masks for a \(0\) at odd one-based positions and five masks for a \(1\) at even one-based positions. Not one of the forty mask pairs covers all twenty-four factors of \(U\). A position near the right boundary has no *more* crossing factor occurrences than the corresponding infinite window, so remains ruled out. Hence both selected positions are even. This is a finite exhaustive proof of a local condition at arbitrary positions, not extrapolation from bounded-length words.

**3. Reverse projection.** Suppose \(\Gamma\) is a two-position attractor of \(T_{k+1}\), with \(k\ge4\). By step 2 it consists of even positions, hence \(\Gamma/2\) is a two-element set of positions in \(T_k\). Because \(\Gamma\) covers the singleton symbols \(0\) and \(1\), its inverse positions in \(T_k\) contain both source letters (the second letters of images \(01\) and \(00\) are \(1\) and \(0\), respectively), so both singleton factors of \(T_k\) are attracted. Let \(Y\) be any factor of \(T_k\) with \(|Y|\ge2\). The word \(\varphi(Y)\) is a factor of \(T_{k+1}=\varphi(T_k)0\) of even length at least four. Every occurrence of any length-at-least-three factor of the period-doubling fixed point begins with the same parity. This is the well-known parity-rigidity property (Banbara et al., Corollary 32), also following from the fact that the distinct three-letter factors are \(000,001,010,100,101\), where all occurrences of each share an offset parity. In particular, every occurrence of \(\varphi(Y)\) begins at the first position of a substitution block. Moreover, because it has even length, such an occurrence cannot use the appended last zero. It therefore comes from an occurrence of \(Y\) in \(T_k\). The attractor \(\Gamma\) covers some occurrence of \(\varphi(Y)\); its even marked position projects to a marked source position \(\Gamma/2\) inside a corresponding occurrence of \(Y\). Thus \(\Gamma/2\) attracts every factor of \(T_k\).

**4. Base case and induction.** `verification/attractor_direct_check.py` independently enumerates every factor and the full set of valid two-position attractors of \(T_4\), obtaining exactly
\[
\mathcal A(T_4)=\big\{\{4,8\},\{4,10\},\{5,10\},\{6,11\},\{6,12\},\{8,12\}\big\}.
\]
The lifting lemma yields at least these six doubled pairs at each level. Conversely, the parity certificate and reverse projection force each pair at level \(k+1\) to halve to a pair at level \(k\); induction gives at most those six. Finally both letters occur in every \(T_k\) for \(k\ge4\), so an attractor needs at least two positions to meet the two singleton factors. All six are therefore minimum and the classification is complete.

## Verification
The local certificate exhausts factor sets and window masks, not just a finite prefix bound: it reports 24 factors, 8 odd-position zero masks, 5 even-position one masks, and zero covering pairs. The independent direct implementation checks every two-position pair against every distinct factor for \(k=4,5,6,7,8\) (lengths 15, 31, 63, 127, 255), finding precisely the stated six pairs each time. Reproduce with `python3 verification/local_parity_certificate.py` and `python3 verification/attractor_direct_check.py`. These finite checks support the proof; the arbitrary-order assertion follows from the independently stated lifting and projection arguments.

## Relationship to prior work
Schaeffer and Shallit (2020) establish that **every** period-doubling prefix of length at least two has minimum string-attractor size two. Banbara et al. (first posted 18 February 2026; latest inspected version October 2026) establish exactly **two** minimum attractors for the *complete* dyadic words of length \(2^k\). Their appendix also gives a finite Walnut automaton recognizing all minimum attractors of arbitrary prefixes and explicitly notes that its interpretation is not straightforward. Thus the present just-before-dyadic six-pair formula and the special size-preserving lifting/projection mechanism give a direct structural interpretation for a natural family of arbitrary prefixes; they do **not** claim that the general Walnut relation fails to encode these six cases. The existing result on greedy period-doubling attractors concerns a different optimization rule and does not give this multiplicity classification.

## Limitations
The classification is restricted to \(k\ge4\) and lengths exactly one less than a power of two. It does not simplify the full Walnut automaton or determine the spectrum at all arbitrary lengths. The finite certificate depends on the exhibited window classification and the property that no \(11\) occurs; arbitrary morphisms need not obey the same projection. Earlier equivalent formulas obtained by processing the published arbitrary-prefix automata cannot be excluded without further independent historical verification. Neither source priority nor independent validation is claimed.
